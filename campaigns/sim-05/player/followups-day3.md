# Follow-ups for Day 3

For the DM. Few, and each from something about the god that bears on its people. Weave them in where the talk allows; the (oog) form is there if it does not. Write the player's answer under each before the close.

## 1. Who keeps the line

The card says a claimed line is kept when the god's people walk it and stand its marks back up at least once in each year.

In the fiction: "A line is walked once and then it is kept. You will not be the one who walks it every year. Who is?"

(oog): Kept ground needs someone among your people whose yearly work it is to walk each line and stand the marks back up. Is that an office, a duty every household shares, something the captains do, or something else? And who does it in a season when you are away?

### The player's answer, creation Day 3, 2026-11-23

Asked in the fiction. The office is the Warden of Marks, the god's own, made in play: "The Warden sends out a walking party each year to stand the marks back up and logs each one in the roll." Who walks:

> A duty the households along the line share. Each household stands up the mark nearest its land once a year, as part of its dues. The Warden's small paid party leads the walk, checks every mark against the roll and logs it. It's a yearly outing as much as a duty, with a fire at the end and bread.

In a season when the god is away, the same: the Warden's party and the households. The Warden's office does not exist at landing and is designed, not yet serving; that the households take up the walk is heard, not promised.

## 2. What your people measure by

The god cannot state or put its name to a false measure, and wants to be the unit.

In the fiction: "They had a foot and a mile and a fair weight before you came. What becomes of those?"

(oog): Do your people already have their own measures when you meet them, and do you mean them to take yours, keep theirs, or hold both? This is about the people and their custom; it changes nothing on your card.

### The player's answer, creation Day 3, 2026-11-23

Asked in the fiction.

> They had a "ford-foot", a pace and a mile of their own, and they're proud of them. They stay. I don't tear them down. I set my foot and my chain in iron on the market wall beside theirs, with the ratio cut between them, and I tell them plainly how the two compare. For a year both are lawful in trade. After that the market weighs and measures by the iron, but the old names stay in speech. I'd rather they say "ford-foot" and mean mine than lose the name.

Both are held for a year, then the god's in trade with the old names kept. What the market does after the year is the Carrow's, heard and not promised.
