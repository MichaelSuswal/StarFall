## Player:

**The sky Limit.** Yes. Put it on as a dated entry: "It reads the ground and what stands on it. It gives no distance, height or bearing for anything in the sky." I said the sky was outside my sense, and I would rather have it written on the card than argued in a scene. I have one question, not an objection. A tower or a wall stands on the ground, so I take it that I read its shape up to its top, as built. The Limit covers only what is in the air: birds, smoke, weather and anything flying. If that's wrong, say so.

**Helpers.** Understood. They're free as ordinary custom and skill, and you'll price anything that gives my people a real edge over other peoples. I'm not asking for one.

**Who they are.** About five thousand, a river and plains people who have lived on this ford a long time. Most are farmers on the wet ground below the rise, with barley, beans and flax. A good share are drovers, keeping cattle and horses on the plain. The rest are the ferrymen, smiths, carters and traders who live off the road and the ford. They aren't great warriors and they're not meek either. They're a stubborn working people with a long habit of arguing over fences. That habit is the reason I fell to them, and it's why a god who sets the lines is welcome there.

**How they live.** Their town is on the rise above the ford, wooden and stone, with a market, a granary and a wall of earth and timber that hasn't been tested lately. They keep early hours, because the melt and the floods punish late ones. They take pride in fair weights and honest measures, so a short bushel is the worst thing one neighbor can do to another. That's the thing I mean to build on. They believe the old paved road was laid by people before them. That's their belief, and I don't claim it as fact.

**My seat.** A plain hall on the highest ground of the rise, with a long table, a map wall and a yard where the chain can be laid out straight. I don't need a throne. I need a room where a line can be drawn on the floor.

**Who runs things when I'm out walking.** Five offices, each with a person, each answering to the one named after it:
1. **The Reeve** runs the town day to day: grain, labor, disputes, the market. The Reeve speaks for me when I'm away and carries out what my decisions and standing conduct allow, and nothing more.
2. **The Marshal** commands the muster and the captains. The levy is drawn from the drovers and farmers, with a core of drilled soldiers who are paid. I'll set the army's shape with the Marshal later. The Marshal answers to the Reeve in peace and to me in war.
3. **The Warden of Marks** keeps the stones and stakes of my lines. This is the most important office I have, because a line is only claimed if it's kept. The Warden sends out a walking party each year to stand the marks back up and logs each one in the roll. The roll is written, in duplicate, and one copy is kept away from the hall. I will not lose a roll again.
4. **The Clerk of the Tally** keeps the counts: people, grain, weights, tribute and tax. The Clerk can't lie in the books any more than I can, and I want everyone to know it.
5. **The Judge of the Ford** hears disputes over boundaries, weights and debts. I want a judge who can't be bought and a people who know it.

**How an order travels.** I speak it to the Reeve, or write it in a short plain hand. The Clerk copies it and seals it. Runners carry it to the headman of each district, and he reads it aloud at the ford market on market day, where everyone can hear it and check it. The headmen send back a tally of the households that heard it. An order that comes back without a tally is treated as not delivered. That is slower than a shout, but it means the order is the same at the last household as it was in my mouth.

**What I want from them.** Compliance with a standard, as I said, and not worship. I'd like them to say "that's the Measure's mile" the way they say "that's a fair weight." I'll set the foot and the chain first, in iron, on the market wall.

**What I'd like to know when you can tell me.** Whether the people above the ford can see the gap in the hills from their walls. I'd like to start the first line from there.
