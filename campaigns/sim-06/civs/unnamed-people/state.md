# The unnamed people: state at the close of Year 3

Campaign: sim-06. Written by the civ agent at the end of Year 3 (early autumn of Year 4 opens next). Each line below says why it moved from the close of Year 2. The close of Year 2 follows, as the stretch was told again.

population: about 5,140, in the unnamed market town and its raised banks, channels, flats and clay farms
  Why: about 190 born, about 145 dead, about 5 hill folk moved into the town to stay. Two wet winters with their hard spells; the fever took the old and the very young in the back houses as it does, fewer than the year before, because the Stallreeve kept four stalls and four braziers under the hall's roof from the first frost and the old came there to sit. No famine, no flood loss, no raid.

stores: grain and dried fish through the coming winter; the grain came in as in most years and the autumn catch was ordinary; salt in plenty; reed and clay without stint; charcoal and ore thin again, as every late summer, until the hill carriers come down
  Why: ordinary year, a warm summer, an ordinary flood, a wet winter. The deep-winter bins were lower than the Stallreeve liked, not low. A few forges went cold for some days in the sixth month, as before. The flats' salt came to the landing cleaner after the sixth month of Year 2 and the loss of a sixth on one boat did not recur.

treasure: about as it was. The hall's fired weights (the ten), the old six in the chest and the seventh seat unwiped, the potters' first nest in the hall, the second at the potters' hall, the third new nest at the potters' hall, the chest of bronze and scrap silver, and salt kept as a store of worth. More in private hands than anyone admits
  Why: the hall spent a little on the drag in Year 2 and on the new nest in Year 3, and took in a little more from the pinch at the bench, which is the hall's and not the stranger's. The stranger takes no metal, silver or fee for the seat. The Tally-keeper's table of the twentieth and her boat sticks cost the hall a lamp and an apprentice's keep, paid from the ship owner's monthly gift cut on her stick. The seventh weight is not in the hall's store.

trade: salt, pots, reed goods and dried fish out; ore, charcoal, timber and bronze in; a market every few days, louder than two years ago and no worse; dawn readings at four landings on the days with no market; boats unshown sell about a tenth low; the hill carriers came down in the middle of each autumn and the old weigher with them in Year 3; upriver boats came down in each summer; a southern coaster came in the first month of summer in Year 2 and none came in Year 3
  Why: the true scale at the gate and the landing made the town's measure better known up the river, and the boat readings made loads easier to buy. The hall's pieces were still heavy by a twentieth and the town knows the whole of it. The clay trade is poorer for the cracking, which two pits days and two years of test tiles did not explain.

strength: a watch of sixty-four with spears; up to three hundred more at the call, few trained; no wall
  Why: no change. The Stallreeve put eight of the watch at the post on the morning after the sixth month of Year 2 and sent six home by midday.

mood: practical, suspicious, short-tempered at market, easier than two years ago about the gate and the hall, since the weight is set on paper. Everyone knows the one at the gate says it is a god and keeps the standards for a term; most are used to it; the back houses and the flats' old are not. The ring is split on it, six and four, and counts the room. The hall's weight changes at the first market of the new autumn if the new nest holds
  Why: the stranger said what it is before the ring and the yard and took the seat before the yard; it read a ship owner's own boat against him and kept the gate; it has asked nothing and taken only a pinch. The yard has more to say of that than it can settle. The old of the flats and the hill kin each have a saying about it that is wrong in a way that matters. The weight, which was the town's shame up the river and down the coast, has at last a decision, and the town is a little ashamed of how long it took.

## State at the close of Year 2, as the stretch was told again

population: about 5,090. stores: grain and dried fish through the winter; salt in plenty; reed and clay without stint; charcoal and ore thin until the hill carriers come down. treasure: about as it was; the seventh weight is not found; the drag of the lowest water went down in the last ten days of the eleventh month and what it brought up is the Keeper's. trade: salt, pots, reed goods and dried fish out; ore, charcoal, timber and bronze in; dawn readings at the ship owners' landings since the fourth month; unshown loads sell about a tenth low; a southern coaster in the first month of summer; upriver boats in summer. strength: sixty-four with spears and three hundred at the call.
  Numbers unchanged from the Year 2 file. Why they stand: nothing in the stretch told again moves a count. The salt on the flats cost one household a sixth in clean salt and one ship owner some standing, not a number.
  mood, as the stretch was told again: the town knows what the stranger is, since the sixth month, and that it has taken the seat, since the ninth. Easier about the gate, worse about the hall's weight, which the southern captain asked about and nobody could answer. The ring is six and four on the seat.

## What became of the four held lines in the Keeper's Year 2 section

- The Assay-master's question on the evening of the weighing was answered. He asked it and it told him it is a god. He would not hold it alone. The Stallreeve was told that night. The ring and the yard were told in the sixth month, with the door open.
- The channel ship owner's bargain at the landing was struck, on the stranger's terms, with a pinch for the fee. The Stallreeve had it that evening. It is the dawn readings.
- The eleven's offer of the seat was taken at the gatepost in the ninth month, on terms, for three years and a day. The term runs to the day after the third anniversary of the gatepost, in the ninth month of Year 5.
- The southern coaster's master at the head of the line came, asked three things, answered three, called it a landlord, and went down the channel after three days. The town has no answer to the last question she left, except what the ring decided in Year 3.

## Keeper, Year 1, pass two (kept as written by the Keeper)
- Month 6: the bonded man did not get the cup. He was taken at the landing and held by the watch. The Stallreeve asked the stranger at the gatepost whether it asked a price of him and got no answer. The council set a price in measure for a theft tried on a guest of the yard; the Tally-keeper holds the stick and the price lies unclaimed. The numbers above stand.
- The potters' sorting of clay by weight: by summer the kilns that sorted had cracked as many loads as before. Some potters now say the gate's scale is no use for clay; the digger with the heavy loads still sells low.
- The walker to the hill kin arrived and came back. Any witness comes down with the carriers next autumn.
- Month 11: the council's invitation was spoken at the gatepost and the half-stick laid at its foot. The stranger has not answered. That matter is open at the year's turn, and the day the council set is the first market of the new autumn.

## Keeper, Year 2, pass two
- The kiln-masters' test tiles did not tell which loads would crack. The quarrel of diggers and kilns keeps, and the tiles are called Orrin's folly or Orrin's patience by who one asks.
- The potters' second nest came out agreeing with itself to a hair. It is kept at the potters' hall.
- Held and open at the year's turn, in order: the Assay-master's question on the evening of the weighing; the channel ship owner's bargain at the landing; the eleven's offer of the Weightward's seat; the southern coaster's master at the head of the line. The numbers above stand.

## Keeper, Year 3, pass two
- The drag at the lowest water in the eleventh month of Year 2 brought up nothing that was a weight: silt, old pots and a sunk boat. The hall's rooms, the old keeper's house and the boatmen gave nothing more. The seventh is not found.
- The potters' third nest, fired to the old six, agrees with itself and with them to a hair. The date the ring set for the town's weight holds.
- The three questions put to the Weightward about what it can do (whether it can be killed, whether it can cure a fever, whether it can tell the height of the water) each met no answer. Your numbers take the branch with no answer.
- Held at the year's turn: the ring's yes to a keeper's word for the coast, and its asking for the words, at the gatepost in the first month of Year 3. The half-stick lies at the foot of the post. The numbers above stand.

## Keeper, Year 4 night, pass two (Year 3 again, as far as it is told)
- The numbers above were written for a Year 3 told to its end with the eleven's asking unanswered. From the fifth day of the first month that year is told again in `years/004.md`, as far as the fourteenth day of the seventh month. No number here is moved until the year is told to its end.
- Among about thirty stones brought to the landings' beams, one read near the Weightward's figure: a door-stone, 3,011. The seventh is not found.
- Held: the Weightward at the door of the potters' hall, the fourteenth day of the seventh month.
