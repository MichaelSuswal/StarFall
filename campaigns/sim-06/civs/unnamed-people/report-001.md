# Report to the Keeper: Year 1, civ unnamed-people, pass one

Sources: the record, state, knowledge file and people files; decisions-001; the conduct profile; and the pass-one release.

## How the god was met

**Autumn.**
- The Assay-master stood through the whole first-day reading. He ruled the salt short and said aloud the bench had been wrong in its ten. He took the readings as true. He took its claim of how it knew as something to test, not believe. He took the offer and made it his own (see the meeting below).
- The Stallreeve watched until noon on the third day. He put two of the watch at the gatepost "to keep the line, not to guard you" and asked nothing of it. In month 2 he brought a charcoal buyer and a hill carrier to the gate with their two scales, and ruled at the stall on the reading. From then he took every dispute of weight to the gate, and he took its readings as true.
- The Tally-keeper said nothing on the first day. That night she began a stick of the gate's readings, unasked.
- The eleven heard the offer on the ninth day from the Assay-master as his own. The clay head and the potters' master wanted it at once. The smiths' master said "A stranger's brass is a stranger's brass." The head of the hill goods said nothing. The council decided only to see how the stranger kept.
- Bren and the channel folk took its word whole. The salt-rakers took it quietly. The hill carriers took it after their scale was read true in month 2. The potters used the readings for their own purposes from month 3.

**Winter.**
- The Assay-master tested it with fourteen scales, a few with small faults he had set himself. He took the readings as true and told only the Stallreeve.
- The Stallreeve ordered the "read at the gate" tokens down in month 4.
- The Tally-keeper asked it to name a mark and got silence. She kept her split stick anyway.

**Spring.** The council voted seven to three (the Assay-master, the clay head and the reed and fish head, the potters', boatwrights' and weavers' masters, and the channel ship-owner for; the hill-goods head, the smiths' master and the salt head against; the open-water ship-owner absent) to ask it to weigh the old weights before the eleven. It is to happen after the harvest, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. The hill-goods head sent a walker to Ruddfold.

**Summer.** In month 11 the Stallreeve carried the invitation to the gatepost himself. The stranger gave no answer, and he reported it delivered.

## The meeting at dusk

The Assay-master took the stranger up to the hall at dusk and shut the door.

1. "You weighed my ten in front of the yard and made a fool of my bench... Tell me first how you knew." The god's words answer it: "My scales tell me what is in the pans, and what's weighed beside them while I'm near. That's the whole of it, and I'll show it again if asked." He takes it as a claim that can be tried.
2. "Then show me again." The god's words answer it. He set out a small fired weight he had marked himself twenty years ago, and two other things the stranger had not seen. The readings matched what he knew. This is where my year turns on the power.
3. "What do you want here?" The god's words answer it: "I want to see the Weightward's old weights set right. I'll weigh them in front of the council, not in a back room. I'd like that to be the offer you take to the eleven, and I'd like it to be yours, not mine." He thinks it odd that the stranger wants no thanks it can't disown.
4. "Why should the eleven take a stranger's scale for the town's standards?" Not answered by the god's words. On silence, he says "A man who is selling has an answer to that" and counts the silence in its favor.
5. "Are you something more than a man with good brass?" Not answered by the god's words. On silence, he notes that it did not say no.
6. "Who sent you? Does a king of the river towns want his share of our market again?" Not answered beyond the first-day line. On silence, he takes it as neither yes nor no.
7. "Have you a name yet to put in the other pan?" The god's words answer it: none but the Weigher. He says "Then that is what I'll call you, for now."
8. "Why did you ask how the old Weightward died?" Not answered by the god's words. On silence, he says only "He was my friend."
9. "What does the Weigher want of the hall after the weighing?" Not answered by the god's words. On silence, he takes it as undecided and thinks that honest.

Unasked, he said he would stand at the bench with his hand on the beam. I wrote no reply for the god.

**What he decides.** He carries the offer as his own. He tells the Stallreeve first, then waits nine days so it isn't said he acted in the heat of a bad day. He puts it to the eleven on the ninth day with his hand on the beam as a clause.

## What was done in the god's name

Nothing by any helper, since none exists. No seal, licence, promise or ruling was given in its name.

Mortals only: in month 3 some stallholders hung "read at the gate" tokens on their scales. The Stallreeve ordered them down in month 4. Two kept them and were fined a small fish each.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

**A. Theft attempt on the nest, month 6, last week.**
- Who tries: a debt-bonded man of a stallholder whose scale was read out by about a tenth in the stallholder's favor.
- What he tries: to take one cup from the nest at the landing in the dark, where the stranger sits late with a lamp.
- Circumstances from my record: the town's code, that even the worst thieves will not touch a house with the door open and a lamp lit, marks of someone sheltering. A lit landing lamp is one of those marks. He is a man of that code and hesitates for it. He is bonded and afraid of his master, which cuts the other way. The watch of sixty is not at the landing at night. Whether the stranger is awake, watching or armed is not mine to say.
- If it succeeds: a cup is gone and the man runs. The town learns in the morning.
- If it fails: the man is at the gatepost at first light, held by the watch, with his master's name to give. That moment reaches the god, and I stop the thread there.
- The rest of my year does not depend on it.

**B. The potters' sorting of clay by weight, from month 3.**
- Who tries: the east kilns first, then five of the seven kiln-masters. They weigh a fixed basket of each digger's clay and refuse heavy loads as gritty.
- Factors from my record: practiced potters (+10), true scale and tools in store (+5), within the people's craft.
- If it succeeds: cracked loads fall, the east kilns do well, and the heavy digger is wronged by a rumor.
- If it fails: heavy loads were good clay, some good loads were set aside, and the potters blame the scale or the stranger.
- I wrote no kiln results either way.

**C. The walker to Ruddfold, month 8.** He went by the Old Rudd Way on foot, alone, from the hill-goods head. The record gives 7 days on foot; the Keeper prices the trip. He carried a split stick saying the hall will weigh its old weights and the hill folk should send a witness with their own weights. Success: a witness comes down with the carriers in mid-autumn. Failure: he is turned back or gets no reply.

## What turns on a power

1. **The readings.** I assumed every reading in its cups is true. If any came out otherwise, these change: the Stallreeve's custom, the carriers' trust, the Assay-master's winter test and the potters' sorting.
2. **The fees, and whether it sleeps.** Not seen. Bren's household says its lamp burns all hours and it is never seen to sleep. I wrote that as their saying only. The Keeper may confirm or strike it.
3. **The tide-reckoner.** She called the midwinter water four days ahead, right in height and half a day late in hour. I decided that inside her priced knack. Her hands were numb through winter. In spring she declined to tell an ordinary flood.
4. **The star.** The town saw it dim early in the year and in the dead of winter. I gave no count or cause. Nothing in my year turns on it.

## What touches anyone else

- The hill carriers came down as every year. One asked the stranger whether it came from Hask and got no answer. They carried the gate's story back up.
- The walker to Ruddfold (see C).
- Aldermark boats came down in summer, as most summers. Two traders had scales read on a dare and took the story upriver. Nothing came from the king.
- Southern ships lay in the Gulf. The town's boats carried their goods up, a trip I am not pricing. A coaster's mate asked in month 11 for its captain's scale to be read, and it was.
- The water folk left the channels at the lowest tides in months 2 and 8. The town did not follow.
- Nothing was sent to Hask, the Sutherwold or the fen people.

## Asked of the god

"Answered in front of them" means by the decided words only.

1. Month 1, a ship-owner's runner with scrap silver, for a place at the head. Answered by decision 3. He stood all day and was read last.
2. Month 1, stallholders and a few of the watch offering more than the fee to go ahead. Answered by decision 3.
3. Month 1, a stallholder: "How long will you stay?" Not answered. Silence.
4. Month 1, an old salt-raker told the stories of Hale the Raker and the larger god drowned at the Maun Stair. No question was asked. It read the next scale, as the profile line on the lost house gives.
5. Month 1, a child: "Are you the drowned one's child?" Not answered. Silence.
6. Month 2, a hill carrier, in jest: "Do you come from Hask?" Not answered. Silence.
7. Month 2, a salt-raker offering salt as the fee. Not answered; decision 3 names grain, fish and a fired weight. He paid in fish next day.
8. Month 2, the carriers' and buyer's scales, brought by the Stallreeve. Answered by decision 3.
9. Month 3, the kiln-master Orrin: "Bring your brass to the diggers' pits and say which clay is good." Not answered. Silence. He had his baskets read instead.
10. Month 3, a potter with a ball of clay: "Is this clay clean?" Not a question of weight. Not answered.
11. Month 3, a stallholder: "May I say my scale was read at your gate?" Not answered. He hung a token anyway.
12. Month 4, the Tally-keeper: "Name a mark, and I will cut it." Not answered. She kept her own stick.
13. Month 4, a debt-bonded net-mender: reading of her owner's grain-scale, answered by decision 3 (out by about a twentieth in the owner's favor). Her plea "Reckon my debt for me, and strike it" was not answered. The council later reduced the debt by a twentieth.
14. Month 5, two households quarreling over the reed island that split: "Who owns it? You say what is true." Not a question of weight, and the council did not ask. Not answered; they took it to the council.
15. Month 6, a stallholder asking it to stand witness to a bargain. Not answered; no standing order covers it. Two of the watch witnessed.
16. Month 7, the yard: "Will you be our Weightward?" Not answered.
17. Month 9, the master of the potters: "Will you weigh our new nest of eleven against the old and set the hall's standards by them?" Not answered. The profile marks the checking of standards for the player's confirmation.
18. Month 10, the Aldermark traders' scales. Answered by decision 3.
19. Month 11, the coaster mate's captain's scale. Answered by decision 3.
20. Month 11, the Stallreeve at the gatepost, before the yard: the council's invitation to weigh the old weights before the eleven, after the harvest, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. I am unsure whether decision 2 answers it in full, because the day, the Assay-master's hand and the setting are the council's terms and not the player's. The moment: the Stallreeve has laid the cut stick at the foot of the gatepost and has spoken the words. The yard is silent. The stranger holds the next scale. **I stop the thread there.**

## Names said aloud

Names said in the god's hearing:
- **Bren**, said by herself on the first day.
- **The Weigher**, said by the yard on the first day and by the Assay-master in the meeting.
- **Hale the Raker** and **the Maun Stair**, said by an old salt-raker in the line in month 1.
- **The list-singers**, said by an old woman in the line in month 1.
- **Ruddfold** and **Hask**, said by hill carriers in month 2.
- **Orrin**, a kiln-master, who said it himself in month 3 with his fee.
- **The Aldermark**, said by a trader in month 10.

No name for the town or the people was said in its hearing. No office holder's own name was said. The town's nicknames for the stranger were not said to it: "Bren's weigher" (channel folk), "the Cupman" (salt-rakers), "the coat" (hill folk).
