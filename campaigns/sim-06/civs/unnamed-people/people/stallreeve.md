# The Stallreeve

Unnamed: his name has not been said aloud in the stranger's hearing. The town's own office, the one the player calls the Reeve of the Gate (see reeve-of-the-gate.md). Fixed by the civ agent at the close of Year 1.

A man of 44, of the channel people, wiry and dark from the sun, a former boatman with a boatman's habit of reading weather in faces. Practical and well liked. He runs the market's stalls, fees, the watch and the runners, and has done so for nine years. He settles most quarrels at the stall with a sentence. He is quick to see who is lying and slow to say so. He keeps his own counsel on whether the stranger worries him.

History: he took the office after the last Reeve died of a fall from a barge. He took nothing from the yard at the gate and asked nothing of it, which he thinks is the only safe way to deal with a stranger. He knows the market is older than any lord and that the old people say a reeve of the Aldermark's king once sat on the bank and took a share. He does not want it said that the town handed its measure to a stranger, and he does not want a king's reeve to come asking why the town needed one.

Temper: careful, humorous, a little vain of his good name. Would rather a thing be done in the open and a little late than quickly in a back room. Likes the stranger's way of reading in the order of the line and says so when no one is listening.

This year: put two of the watch at the gatepost, brought every dispute of weight to the gate, moved four stalls under the hall's roof in the worst of winter, took on four more men, carried the council's invitation to the gatepost himself in the eleventh month, and had the Tally-keeper cut it on a stick first.

Year 2: carried the yes and the stranger's terms up to the hall, and was told his rule on the sticks was a good one. Gave leave and later answered for the watch and the Tally-keeper for the stranger's day at the pits. Held the yard at the hall door on the day of the weighing and told it in the open, and a little late, what the ring had done and what the four pieces had shown. Ruled the market stand on the hall's present pieces until the eleven said otherwise. Went down alone to the landing after the carriers' market in the second month and asked the boatmen about the old Weightward's last weeks (the Keeper's). Went up the loft with the Assay-master and the clay head. Had the two pans scraped from the stallholders' beams. Carried the eleven's offer of the seat to the gatepost in the ninth month (held, see report).

Year 2 told again and Year 3: stood over the Assay-master while he locked the chest the evening of the weighing. Was told by the stranger itself at the hall door that it is a god, said "A year at my gatepost for a pinch of barley. Well.", kept the gate as it was, and said nothing in the yard. Carried the Assay-master's asking round that night and named no matter. Went down alone to the boatmen in the second month and told the Assay-master and the clay head what he was told within the week. Added the gate-first rule and the landing's-own-line rule to the ship owner's bargain, and sent the boat sticks to the Tally-keeper. Ruled the salt short measure and not theft. Put the stranger's asking to the eleven and held the door at the sitting. Carried the eleven's offer and, at the ninth month, said at the gatepost that the key is taken in the ring, before the ring, and not before. Answered the stranger's question about a keeper's word for the coast: "Not this summer. Ask us after the grain sitting." Led the drag at the lowest water. Put four braziers under the hall's roof the second winter. Carried the ring's yes about the coast's word to the gatepost (held).

Year 3 again, from the fifth day of the first month, to the fourteenth day of the seventh month: took the word at the post on the fifth for the eleven and carried the second stick up. Kept the second stick for the ring and said so. Forbade a wager at the beam and the buying of a stone at a landing for its weight. Gave the day at the pans on the fourteenth of the second month on his own terms, that the household be told and not found, and told the yard himself. Carried the door-stone figure to the eleven at dusk on the eighteenth of the fourth month, second, as the Weightward said. On the seventeenth of the sixth month brought the ring's answer on the second stick to the post, with counts and one reason, and asked the Weightward as himself what it would have cut for the carrier now. It gave him no answer. He said there was time.

Year 3, played live (Year 5 play day): on the seventeenth of the seventh month told the Weightward no watch was needed at the gate on the twenty-fifth or the first, neither being a market day. On the second of the eighth month, at the gate, was given the Weightward's answer to his question of the sixth month on what is to be cut for the carrier: nothing more of the Weightward's. "Nothing more of yours, then. That is an answer, and I had waited for one."

Year 3, from the fourteenth day of the seventh month, to Year 4, the fourteenth day of the tenth month: on the seventeenth of the ninth month of Year 3 brought the ring's counts to the post, thing by thing: the hall's paying for the spares seven for, three against, one not voting; and, of the Weightward's answer of the second of the eighth month, a motion to cut the second stick for the carrier, five for, five against, one not voting, with one reason, that a stick cut on a tied vote is a word the ring is not agreed on. Told the carrier at the post on the twenty-ninth, in the Weightward's hearing, what the Weightward would have him told. On the second of the first month of Year 4 held the yard back at the hall door for the ring's day and told the yard the order of it from the step. Ruled the two bargains made before the day by the ten, as made. Put five braziers under the hall's roof in the fifth month. At the post on the seventeenth of the ninth month said the term is three years and a day and that nobody had said otherwise. On the thirteenth of the tenth month, at the fish landing, kept two of the watch on the gate as always when the southern coaster came in.
