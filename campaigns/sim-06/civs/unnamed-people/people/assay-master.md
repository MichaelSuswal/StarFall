# The Assay-master

Unnamed: his name has not been said aloud in the stranger's hearing, and the stranger was told he would have one when it had a name of its own to put in the other pan. This is the town's own chief assayer, the holder of the office the player calls the Warden of the Hall (see warden-of-the-hall.md). Fixed by the civ agent at the close of Year 1.

A man of 58, of the clay-bank people, broad and slow, a little stooped from forty years over a beam. Patient, honest, humorless in the way of a man who has been right about small things for a long time and been thanked for it rarely. He has tested weights for the town since his thirties, sits among the eleven, and has never been accused of favor. He is not loved and not disliked. He does not sulk when he is wrong in public, and he said so aloud in front of the yard on the first day.

History: he was the old Weightward's friend of thirty years and the one who held the second key to the chest of old weights. The Weightward died last spring within a fortnight of a chill. The Assay-master has opened the chest twice since and says he does not know the true state of what is in it. He has told nobody what he thinks of the Weightward's death. He suspects the chill was not the whole of it and cannot say why.

Temper: tests everything slowly and trusts the test. Argues from weight, not from feeling. Does not like surprises and handles them by making a plan. Does not like to owe. Will argue with the stranger and has said he will.

This year: heard the stranger at dusk, took the offer to the eleven as his own on the ninth day of the year, tested the stranger quietly through the winter with scales carrying faults he had set himself, carried the council's vote in the ninth month, and agreed with the Stallreeve to set the day after the grain is in. He means to stand at the bench with his hand on the beam.

Year 2: opened the chest with the second key before the ring on the day of the weighing and said plainly that the rack held six of seven and the seat was empty, that he had found it so after the laying-out and twice since, alone each time, and had told only the Stallreeve, the evening before, that the rack was not full. Misread the fifth stone piece as level at the first trial, read it light on the second, said so aloud, and had both readings cut. Said that he had tested the town's scales for thirty years against pieces that ran a twentieth heavy and had not known it. Did not vote on the motion to make the old six the town's weight; voted against the head of salt's; voted for the clay head's, to wait. Went up the old keeper's loft with the Stallreeve and the clay head on the third day after. Held back after the eleven rose and asked the stranger, at the shut door, the question it had promised to answer after (held, see report). In the ninth month spoke for the seat as an office with a term and the eleven's hand on it and his own beside the stranger's on the beam. Waits.

Year 2 told again and Year 3: asked the stranger on the evening of the weighing what it is and was told. Would not hold it alone and sent for the Stallreeve, who stood over him while he locked the rack in the chest. Asked for the sitting. Said before the ring that it was said to him in the hall on the evening of the weighing and that he asked, that he would not say he believed it and would not say he did not, and that he would not be alone with a second. Came down to the gate on market mornings and watched its hands, its face, and what it did with a reading that went against someone who could hurt it. Told the ring in the shut hall what the three saw in the loft. Spoke for the seat in the ninth month and said what he had not said before, that it had said it was a god before it was asked to take the seat. Told the stranger what he saw in the loft, once, quietly, in the summer. Opened the chest with his own key before a pair of the eleven at the first sitting after the grain. Brought his own beam to the gate for the hill weigher's three large stones. Asked it to check six scales he had passed, and mended the four himself. Did not like the hill saying of the seventh and would not stand at the old man's second laying-out. Waits.

Year 3 again, from the fifth day of the first month, to the fourteenth day of the seventh month: spoke up on the fifth, on the rounded calls of the fired pieces, and the word was mended before the split. Brought his own beam for the old weigher's three large stones on the eighth of the second month. Went to look at the old net-maker's door-stone the day after the eighteenth of the fourth month and said it was never cut and was a door-stone. Brought six scales to be checked in the fourth month. At the post on the seventeenth of the sixth month said the fifth was his own reading and he would read it light on a third trial. Walked with the Weightward to the potters' hall on the fourteenth of the seventh month. His name has still not been said.
