## Year 2 again

### The autumn, with the word held by the pair of them

- The evening of the weighing. The door shut on the Assay-master and the stranger; when it opened he sent his apprentice for the Stallreeve. In the hall the Assay-master locked the rack in the chest with his own key, with the Stallreeve standing over him. The key on the peg was not touched. The seat was left unwiped. The stranger touched nothing in the chest. At the hall door it told the Stallreeve (decisions item 2), the Assay-master beside it. The Stallreeve gave the lines the release gives: the gate kept as it was, the watch where it stood, nothing in the yard, the asking carried round that night. He asked it nothing about what else there is to it. It also gave him the word for the old weigher at Ruddfold (item 8); the Tally-keeper cut it on a stick before the carriers came.
- The asking. The Stallreeve went to each of the eleven that night and named no matter. The ring would not call a sitting that week, since it had just sat a whole day and had the loft to look in. It went to the winter sitting, middle of month 6. The head of salt pressed (a matter no one will name should be named for him; then the stranger is put above the ring). The Assay-master answered that it had asked to stand at the foot of the ring. The smiths master and the hill goods head, the clay head, the head of reed and fish each reacted as their files have them; none of them called a sitting.
- Months 1 to 3, how each carried it. The Assay-master began coming to the gate on market mornings, arms folded, looking first at its hands, then at its face, then at what it did with a reading that went against someone who could hurt it. He never said the word to anyone but the Stallreeve, and not to the Stallreeve in the first weeks. The Stallreeve looked at its face as a boatman reads weather and found it the same each dawn. The two sat together once in each ten days in the hall with the door open. Neither was alone with it. The clay head, who had been the loft's third, was told nothing of the other matter until month 6; he asked the Assay-master why he had been the one asked and was told "Because you will say what you saw." The head of salt asked the Stallreeve in month 2 whether the matter was a threat to the market and was told it was no threat to the market's measure.
- The loft, third day after: three went up (Assay-master, Stallreeve, clay head). What they saw is the sealed note's. The town saw them come down quiet. The yard settled on two stories: that the stranger would say yes or no to the seat, and that the Assay-master had found the seventh.
- Month 2: the carriers; the word to Ruddfold went by stick. The Stallreeve went to the boatmen below the hall; the stranger saw him from the lamp and heard nothing. Through the autumn he, the Assay-master and the clay head kept their own counsel.
- The pits day (last week of month 2), the first stale stick, the tiles, the scraped marks: as the Year 2 file has them, carried over.

### The landing and the sticks for boats (month 4)

- The ship owner of the channels made his offer to Bren and two boatmen at dusk by the lamp. It refused "my landing and no other", gave its terms (item 3), he took them: "I came down here to buy a year of you, and you have sold me a morning and kept the rest." And on the pinch: "A pinch. I offered you a measure a boat and you beat me down to a pinch. Every man on this water will know by morning what I asked and what I got, so I had better be seen to like it." They shook hands. The Stallreeve that evening: the gate first; no reading on a market morning anywhere but the gatepost; a landing is its owner's ground and its line is the owner's to keep, not the watch's. Of boat sticks: "That's her knife and her mornings. Ask her at the gate, the pair of you, and ask her what it does to her season's count."
- The Tally-keeper's answer at the gate: yes, on her own additions: cut at the landing's table at the end of the dawn and not at the dawn; a captain who wants none gets none and is still called; the landing's mark on the back of every boat stick, none hung; one half to the captain and one to the buyer if he wants one, holding for that day only, the landing's man holds neither; the pinch for the stick is the captain's and the buyer's, none the stranger's; the ship owner's offered gift (a measure of salt a month) she took as the hall's and cut on her own stick each month where anyone can read it. Her season's count held. Item 9's terms for the sticks stand as the stranger gave them; she added only what is written here.
- The other owners. The ship owner of the open water asked, through his landing man, in month 5, for a dawn when his coasters were in, and had it by item 3 on the same terms. Two smaller owners of the channels asked in month 6 for a dawn a month. Bren's household asked at the fish landing in month 6 and found it cost them more in standing than in fish. No other owner refused. Some captains would not show. It said it was not shown and went on; it chased no one. A load unshown sold about a tenth lower at the first stall within the month.
- What the water says. That it reads a hold like a net, counting what is in the mesh; "I wasn't shown" as a thing to say of any hidden matter; that a captain who turns out a basket has nothing to turn out against. His rivals say it is his man; he says it is nobody's and that is why he sits it first.

### The salt (early month 6)

- Called as the release gives: basket after basket about five parts salt and one part sand and grit under a clean top, called before the captain, the ship owner's landing man and whoever stood on the landing, nothing said of who or how. The ship owner came down before the baskets were done and said "Read the rest." It did.
- What the town found: one raking household on the flats, a thin season, last pan raked and the baskets topped clean; the captain bought on their word and did not turn one out; the ship owner did not know and said so before the water.
- What was done. The Stallreeve ruled it short measure and not theft (a quarrel between buyer and seller is the gate's). The household made good the sixth in clean salt in two instalments, cut by the Tally-keeper. The captain was docked half of the sixth from his share; the ship owner bore the rest and said so in the open. From then the captains turned out every fifth basket at the landing unasked. The head of salt took the household's part, quoting its own first-morning line that a short measure and a cheat are different things. The household stayed from the gate a month and then came with a sieve and had their baskets turned out first; the flats' salt came cleaner that winter. The ship owner's own words a week after, before any of the ring who cared to hear: "It read my own boat against me in front of the water, and I would sooner it read mine first than the buyer's. I told it so." The water said he had paid it to read his own boat first for a show. They are wrong about that.
- The Assay-master and the Stallreeve both took it as what it had told the Assay-master to watch for.

### The winter sitting (middle of month 6)

- How the eleven argued the yard in. Nine for, two against (head of salt, master of the smiths). The head of salt: a thing said before the yard cannot be unsaid. The master of the smiths: a stranger who asks to speak before the yard means to speak to the yard. The head of the hill goods was for, because the hill kin dislike things set in back rooms. The Assay-master: if the ring hears it shut, the yard hears it from a boy by dusk. The clay head and the master of the potters for. The head of reed and fish said she had never seen him ask the yard in and wanted to see what he wanted it to hear. The ship owner of the channels was for anything said in the open on that water.
- What was said before the yard: the stranger said it first, items 1 and 6, the whole of what the release gives it: it is a god; others came down when it did and it has felt none; it does not know where it came from or whose child it is; its scales; it cannot give a false measure and can keep silent; there is more and it answers yes or no on a particular power; "I weigh, and they decide"; "I do not ask to be Weightward and I do not refuse it yet"; it will answer for the seat when the loft has been looked in and the seventh looked for. The Assay-master asked it to say what it said of the seventh; it said before the ring that it did not take it, never touched the chest, and did not know the rack was cut for seven until he lifted it out. The Assay-master added only that it was said to him in the hall on the evening of the weighing and that he asked. He said he would not say he believed it and would not say he did not, had six calls his beam could not fault and a seventh denied before anyone asked, had held one thing for a year and would not be alone with a second.
- What each of the eleven said and did, in the ring after the stranger had spoken:
  - The Assay-master: as above; then stood the sitting out. In the shut hall after, with the clay head and the Stallreeve, told the ring what the three saw (sealed).
  - The head of reed and fish: asked "What else are you?" (answered by item 1: there is more, yes or no on a particular power); said that was an answer a good broker gives. In the shut hall she spoke after the three.
  - The head of salt: asked whether it can tell when a man is lying (item 7, whole); said "Then no bargain in this town is safe from you"; said he would not ask and his people would not. Moved in the shut hall that it be asked to stand down from the gate until the ring had sat on what it was. Seconded by the smiths master. Nine against. The motion did not reach the stranger. He did not vote on the looking.
  - The head of the hill goods: was for the yard let in; said the hill kin had been measured by their betters at last; sent word up the old road by the first walker after the thaw. I have the stick cut by the Tally-keeper and nothing of what Ruddfold answered until Year 3, month 2.
  - The clay head: said it had told him what it could not do, which he had not met in a god before; moved that the seventh be looked for in every way open to the town. It carried ten to none, the head of salt not voting.
  - The master of the potters: said that if it was a god it was a gentle one; said she would bring the second nest to the gate like anyone.
  - The master of the boatwrights: asked whether the landing readings stood. Told yes by item 3.
  - The master of the weavers: said he weighs wool and had not weighed this one. Said no more.
  - The master of the smiths: asked it across the ring whether it could be killed and what it would take to break it (see Asked of the god, for the Keeper). Seconded the head of salt's motion. Afterward said the ring's right to put it down was an edge he would hold to.
  - The ship owner of the channels: said he had bargained with a god over a pinch of salt and did not like the bargain the less.
  - The ship owner of the open water: asked for his dawn; had it.
- What the ring decided in the shut hall: the stand-down motion failed, nine to two. The looking was set (see Uncertain outcomes, A). The ring set its sitting on the seat for after the sowing. Nothing in the hall to be filed, mended, replaced or carried out stands as before.
- What the yard did: silence for the length of a long breath, then a great many breaths. An old salt-raker woman said "The Maun Stair." A child asked if it was the one that drowned; the stranger said again that it did not know where it came from or whose child it was (item 1, a decided answer). The next market the line was twice as long and nobody bought fish until noon, then everybody did. The Stallreeve put eight of the watch at the post and sent six home by midday. Bren said loud that she had fed it first and would feed it again, and fed it at her table. The back houses said a god of scales is a god of scales. The old of the flats took bread to the Maun Stair at the next low tide. The salt-rakers say it knew about the seventh because it asked after the key; they say it at the pans.
- A woman of the back houses whose child was in the fever asked whether it could cure a fever; her child died in the last week of the month with the cold. She said nothing at the gate. The back houses did not stop reading their scales. The Stallreeve put two more braziers under the hall's roof for the next winter.
- Last week of month 6: two boatwright households asked it to stand witness to a boat sale and said, before the line, that they asked nothing of the other thing. It stood, as the Year 2 file has it.

### The gatepost (month 9)

- Seat: six for (Assay-master, clay head, master of the potters, head of reed and fish, master of the boatwrights, ship owner of the channels), four against (master of the weavers, head of salt, master of the smiths, head of the hill goods), the ship owner of the open water away on the Gulf. Why six still wanted it with a term, the Assay-master's hand and the ring's right to put it down: the Assay-master, that it had said it was a god before it was asked to take the seat, said it before the ring and before the yard, had read the ship owner's own boat against him when the reading cost it a standing place, and had said the ring could put it down (a keeper the ring cannot put down is a lord, said first by it); the clay head, its bread refused, its "I cannot say which clay"; the potters' master, that it weighed her nest and she was not the worse; the head of reed and fish, that her people said for years the bench ran heavy and she did not mind who said it so long as someone counted; the boatwrights' master, shorter quarrels at the landing; the channels' ship owner, a stake, a grudge and a bargain, the grudge against himself. Why four did not: the weavers' master, a seat for a thing that does not sleep is a throne and the ring's right is a paper against it; the head of salt, a thing that can read a bargain should not keep the standards of what it can read; the smiths master, he would not seat what he could not break; the hill goods head, the hall waited forty years for a weight and got a keeper, he would have had the weight first, and a term ends hard on a thing the ring cannot move.
- At the gatepost: the Stallreeve said the eleven's words as the Year 2 file has them and laid the other half of the stick. It read a stallholder's scale first, heavy on the buying side by about one part in thirty, and said so. It said it is a god, said yes, and gave its terms (item 4 whole), the last to the yard: "I am the Weightward, for a term. I am not their god, and nothing here is mine." The Tally-keeper fitted her half and said the terms back. The Stallreeve and the Assay-master spoke as the release gives.
- The yard: a boy cried "Weightward" first and the whole line took it up, channel folk and potters loudest, until the Stallreeve put up his hand. The salt-rakers stood silent. The old of the flats said that a god has never stayed to count what it was owed and here was one that had, and said nothing in the town was its own, and that it had come to count and what it counted would be paid. Bren's household would feed it at their table when it was hungry, not as a small god. The line was the longest of the year.
- How the eleven took the terms it added: well. The unwiped seat, the not waiting on the seventh, no fee, the chest never opened by it alone. Nobody called a sitting to put it down.
- When the ring next sat and the key came off the peg: a no-market day in the first days of month 10, before the coaster. It took the key from the peg in front of the ring; the Tally-keeper cut it. The ring settled that any pair of the eleven who come may stand for the chest, one of the pair to be of the four who voted against. Nothing was opened that day.

### The summer

- The coaster's master, month 10: as the release gives, all of it, in what the line saw and heard. The town made of it: "a god ashore is a landlord" went into the yard as a saying and a joke; the line had not known the south keeps no common weight; the hill goods head called it a fair among thieves; the town could not answer her last question.
- Asked once of the Stallreeve in front of the Assay-master, at the gatepost at dusk, with the coaster three days from sailing: whether the town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast, in the plain terms it gave the eleven. The Stallreeve: "Not this summer. Ask us after the grain sitting." It was not his to say and the ring had not said which was the town's weight. The Assay-master added that its terms are the ring's own and the answer to a southern captain who asks which is the town's weight is whatever the ring decides, and not before. No word went down with the coaster. (Item 12, first ask, answered no for this summer.)
- Asked once of the Assay-master, quietly, at the hall's doorway at dusk after the coaster went: what the three saw in the loft. He told it. His words, exactly:
  > "You asked what we saw in the old keeper's loft. I will tell you what I saw and no more than I saw. The old man's coat and boots were hanging on a peg, stiff with dried river silt to above the knee. Nobody had moved them. Nothing else in the room was out of its place. We touched nothing and came down. The ring has had this since the winter. I do not know what it means."
  He did not say more of it and did not say what the Stallreeve was told by the boatmen. It is the first time the loft was said in the stranger's hearing.
- The pits again, month 11: the diggers wanted it. Orrin brought lumps of both kinds and a tile fired from each. The pan said as before. The quarrel kept.
- The drag: see A4.
- The upriver boats and the grain: as the Year 2 file has them, with the added line that the traders went away with the story that the hall had been found heavy and had said so.
- The ring did not sit in month 12 on the weight; its sitting on it is the first market of the new autumn.

## Held

1. The ring's yes to a keeper's word for the coast. Year 3, month 1, the first market after the ring's first sitting after the grain. What has just happened: the six were weighed again before the ring; the ring voted the old six the town's weight from the first market of the autumn that comes round next year; the ring then said yes to what the Weightward had asked of the Stallreeve at the gatepost at dusk a year before. The moment: the yard in its line, the Weightward holding a scale, the Tally-keeper beside the Stallreeve with the cut stick, the Assay-master a little apart with his arms folded. The Stallreeve says, in his own words: "The eleven say yes to what you asked of me at the gatepost, Weightward. The town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast, in the plain terms you gave the eleven: what each of the hall's pieces is now, in your ones, and that the eleven have said which is the town's weight. The ship owner of the open water will carry it when his coasters go out past the Horn. The eleven ask you for the words." He lays the other half of the stick at the foot of the gatepost. Stop there. For the rest of the year the Stallreeve has said nothing more at the gate on it, the ship owner's coasters wait on it, and the town waits. No word is mine to write for the stranger. The coasters went out in month 10 either way; what they carried is the Keeper's.
   - The rest of my year both ways. If the Weightward gives words in time for the coasters: they go with the open-water owner's boats in month 10 and what comes of them is the Keeper's; the Aldermark traders in month 11 carry back the same. If it gives none, or says it will give none: the ring waits, the Stallreeve says the south is waiting on the weight, and the ring asks again at the first sitting of Year 4.
2. No bargain was put to it to be read. I have nobody ask for a reading of a bargain this year; a stallholder who wished to be read to be called honest was told by the Stallreeve that the gate is for scales and the hall for the rest. Nothing is held on the reading of bargains.
3. No person of note from outside the town sought it out. Traders of the Aldermark, the old weigher of Ruddfold and the carriers had their scales read or their stones weighed, and the master of the coaster was settled in Year 2 above.

## How the god was met

**Autumn.**
- Month 1. The ring took its offer to weigh the six again, seven to two, two not voting. The head of the hill goods wanted it for the hill kin's sake; the head of salt said a stick cut once cannot be recut; the Assay-master said a weight weighed once is a weighing and weighed twice is a keeping. The Assay-master opened the chest before the head of the hill goods and the master of the potters. Its key was in the room, in its keeping, and was not turned. The six read as before. He read the fifth light by a hair on the first trial and said he had learned that one.
- The ring voted the old six the town's weight, six for, two against (salt, smiths), three not voting (boatwrights, weavers, open-water owner), from the first market of the autumn that comes round next year, with the head of salt's clause that bargains made before be reckoned by the present pieces, and the master of the potters to fire a nest to the old six in spring. The Stallreeve ruled that any bargain after the change by the old pieces should say so and have it cut. The Tally-keeper cut a table of the twentieth. The seventh seat stays as it is.
- Three who voted against the seat counted the room and found no cause to call it down.
- Month 2. The old weigher came down with the carriers, a year older, in a cart. On the third market morning at the gate he stood with the head of the hill goods, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve. The three larger stones wanted a beam; the Assay-master sent for his own, saying the hall's was the hall's. The master of the smiths looked at it and said it was a good beam. It called 48, 96, 193, 384, 768, 1,536. The old weigher said the hill kin's stones and the hall's agreed as far as anyone knew and he had come to see it done. Asked what his people say the seventh was, he said it was a story: that in Ruddfold they tell boys the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, and that the hall dared not weigh with it. He said it was his grandfather's saying and he would not stand behind it. He asked nothing of it. The Assay-master did not like the saying and said so to the Stallreeve. Nobody moved to look anywhere it pointed.
- Month 3. The dawns ran on the terms given. Nothing else.
**Winter.**
- Month 4. The Assay-master asked it, as the terms allow, to check six scales he had passed. Two read level and four a hair out, as scales do. He mended the four himself. The boatwright households asked for a witness again and said again that they asked nothing of the other thing.
- Months 5 and 6. The tide-reckoner's word was told at the gatepost and it heard it. The back houses brought no sick to the gate.
**Spring.**
- Month 7. The master of the potters brought pieces to the line like anyone. The tide-reckoner told the Stallreeve it would be an ordinary flood. A netmaker at the gate asked it whether it could tell the height of the water as the tide-reckoner does (Asked of the god, for the Keeper).
- Month 8. The flood, the sowing, the low tides. Nothing else.
- Month 9. The ring sat on the third nest and not on the seat. Nobody called a sitting to put it down.
**Summer.**
- Month 10. The open-water owner's coasters went out. The south sent none.
- Month 11. Traders of the Aldermark asked at the landing and in the yard what the keeper of the town's weights was. The town told them what the town says. No one asked it directly.
- Month 12. The ring did not sit.

## What was done in the god's name

Nothing by a helper, since none serves. No seal, licence, promise or ruling was given in its name.

Mortals only. The Tally-keeper cut boat sticks, a table of the twentieth, the dates of the key and of the weighings. The Stallreeve ruled that bargains after the change should say if they are by the old pieces. The ring's pair rule (one of every two at the chest is of the four who voted against) is the ring's. A few stallholders moved their bargaining nearer or further from the gatepost; nobody said it was the Weightward's rule.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

**A. The looking for the seventh, month 6 of Year 2 onward.** The ring set it; the stranger said it would answer for the seat when the seventh had been looked for. I send the ring only where the town would go.
- A1. The rooms of the hall, three days, in the last week of month 6 after the sitting: the Assay-master, the Stallreeve, the clay head and the Tally-keeper. Success: something turns up in the hall that the ring takes for the seventh, or a mark of where it went. Failure: nothing does. Circumstances: four searchers who know the rooms (+5), several days (+10), the seat clean where the others are dusty (a thing taken out of its seat and not lost behind it; no factor), the loft already looked in by three (no factor).
- A2. The old man's house and whatever kin he had, month 7: the Stallreeve and the Assay-master, with the head of reed and fish to speak to whoever they found. The Assay-master knew him thirty years. I name no kin. Success: the house or a kinsman yields the weight or a word of it. Failure: nothing. Circumstances: two men who knew him (+5), a house untouched since the laying-out (no factor, not in my record), the Assay-master's trust (no factor).
- A3. The boatmen of every landing, months 7 and 8: the Stallreeve, who is a former boatman, asked at every landing. Success: an account that bears on where something went. Failure: none. Circumstances: a boatman's trust in his old fellow (+10), a night more than a year gone (-5), the boat people not asked (they are not the town's).
- A4. The drag, the last ten days of month 11, at the lowest water: the Stallreeve with twelve of the watch and the boatmen of two households, with drag hooks, weighted nets and long poles, in the reach below the hall's landing and the reaches either side of it. Anything heavy and of stone to be carried to the hall's beam and put before two of the eleven and the Weightward. Success: a stone is brought up that the ring takes for the seventh. Failure: nothing but silt, a sunk boat or two, old pots. Circumstances: a dozen hands over ten days (+10), the channels run low and the channels move (a bed that has changed since the old man's last spring, -10), a place chosen from what the ring knew and not from a find (no factor), a weight of the size the Assay-master reckons, "twice the sixth, by the run of them", which is his inference and not a fact.
- The rest of my year both ways. If any of the four finds it: it is brought before the ring at once; the Weightward weighs it before two of the eleven; the Assay-master's reckoning is tried against it. The ring's sitting on the weight in Year 3, month 1 would then have seven weights in the room, and I would write it again after your pass two. If none finds it: as written, the six are the town's weight, the seat stays unwiped, and the ring did not ask for more looking by the end of Year 3, since the hill kin's saying pointed nowhere the Assay-master would go.

**B. The potters' third nest, month 7 of Year 3.** Who tries: the master of the potters, fired to the old six as the ring asked, twenty-two pieces in one kiln, eleven chosen by the gate's scale to agree with each other and with the old first. Success: a nest of eleven that agrees with itself and the old six to a hair, so that the old six can be the town's weight from the first market of Year 4 as the ring set it. Failure: still uneven by a thirtieth; she fires again next spring and the ring puts the date back a year, and the head of salt says he told them so.
- Circumstances: a practiced master with the true scale at the gate (+10 and +5); a second nest that came out agreeing with itself to a hair, learned from (+5); twenty-two fired to choose eleven (+10); a standard to fire to that is the old six and not the hall's heavy pieces she matched last time (no factor, a change of aim); spring firing, not winter frost (no factor).
- The rest of my year both ways. If it succeeds: the nest is read at the ring after the sowing, month 9, and the date holds. If it fails: she says nothing, fires again, and the ring's date is the Keeper's to move, as I move it in Year 4.

I have no other contested outcome. The votes, the salt, the reactions, the ring's weight and the old weigher's visit are mine and I settled them.

The old weigher arrived in Year 3 month 2 with his stones and went home with the figures on his stick and the ring's decision of the old six. The hill kin have the saying about the seventh; they say the stranger is the coat that weighed their stones the same as the town's.
- Of the Aldermark: traders arrived in summer of each year, had a pair of scales read, and went home with the story that the hall's pieces were found heavy and the keeper said so. In Year 3 they asked at the landing what the keeper of the town's weights was. No one came from the king. What they carried back is the Keeper's.
- The southern coast. The coaster's master heard the stranger say it is a god, that it asks what the southern harbors read their scales against, that it does not leave the gate, and that the town has no answer to which is its weight. She sailed after three days. She took no word from the town. No coaster came in Year 3. The ring's yes to a keeper's word is held.
- The open-water ship owner's two coasters go out past the Horn each summer; in Year 3 they wait on the held word.
- The boat people were away at the lowest tides of autumn and spring in each year and came back. Nobody followed.
- The old man's kin and the landing boatmen are touched by the looking (A2, A3), at the Keeper's pleasure.
- Nothing was sent to Hask, across the great forest or to the fen people.

## Asked of the god

"Answered in full" means by the decided words only. "For the Keeper: yes, no, or no answer" marks a question about a particular power.

11. **Month 6, the master of the smiths, across the ring: "Can you be killed? What would it take to break you?" For the Keeper: yes, no, or no answer.** Written on both ways, briefly. ## Names said aloud

- **Maun Stair**, said by an old salt-raker woman in the hall at the winter sitting, Year 2 month 6, to the hall and the stranger.
- **Corrith**, said first by the stranger in its own question to the coaster's master, Year 2 month 10, and then by her in her answer. A boatman had said it in ordinary talk earlier.
- **Ruddfold**, said by the old weigher in Year 3, month 2, at the gate, to the Weightward and the ring members present.
- **Alder**, said by the Stallreeve at the gatepost, month 5 of each year, giving the tide-reckoner's word.
- **Orrin**, said by himself at the pits in Year 2 month 2 and again in month 11, to the stranger, giving his fee.
- **Aldermark**, said by a trader at the landing in Year 3 month 11, to the boatmen in the stranger's hearing, in ordinary talk.
- **The Horn**, said by the Stallreeve at the gatepost in the held moment, Year 3 month 1, naming where the open-water owner's coasters go.
- No name for the town or the people was said in its hearing. No office holder's own name was said, nor the potters' master's, the head of the hill goods', the old weigher's, the coaster master's or any of the eleven's. The town's names for the stranger were not said to it: "Bren's weigher" (channel folk), "the Cupman" (salt-rakers), "the coat" (hill folk), and the new hill saying that it is the seventh that walked, said only among themselves.
