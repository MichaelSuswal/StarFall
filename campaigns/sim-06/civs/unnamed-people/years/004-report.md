# Report to the Keeper: Year 3 told again from the fifth day of the first month, civ unnamed-people, pass one

Sources: `CLAUDE.md`, the civ role file, the pace rules and the encounter rules (sections 5 and 6), my whole folder (record, state, knowledge, lore, people files, year files and reports), decisions-004, the conduct profile, `messages/m-0005.md` and the release for this turn. The stretch runs from the fifth day of the first month to the fourteenth day of the seventh month, at the door of the potters' hall. Nothing is written past that door in any public file. `years/003.md` and `years/003-report.md` carry the one set-aside line at their head and are otherwise unedited.

Three reckonings of mine, so the Keeper can overrule them. First, the calendar: I took the months as thirty days each, because only then do the release's two market days (the fourteenth of the second month and the fourteenth of the seventh) fall on the market count started on the fifth of the first month. The market then falls on every day of a month that leaves two when divided by three: the second, fifth, eighth and so on. Second, the stone readings in the table below are mine, chosen inside the release's range (about 800 to about 6,000, most between 1,500 and 4,000, none within a twentieth of 3,072 but the door-stone). Third, the release names "the boat sale it stood witness to". The draft's Year 3 has the boatwrights' households ask in the fourth month for the lease of a boatyard, and the boat sale itself was in the sixth month of Year 2, before this stretch. I carried the lease as the draft has it.

## The stretch, in order

**Month 1.**
- Day 5, the gatepost, as played: the fish seller's balance read out, buying pan heavy by about one part in sixty, taken away to mend; the word given and mended twice; the first stick (the town's word) to the Stallreeve for the eleven, the carrier's half to the ship owner of the open water; the second stick up to the eleven, neither yes nor no; the carrier took three of its instructions and not a pair; its standing on the master of the smiths' ask and the woman of the back houses' ask told to the Stallreeve, who would not carry it. I wrote only what the yard saw and heard.
- That night and each night after: the landing, the lamp, the cups run smallest first, then the count. The boatmen's three reasons and name for it ("running the cups") are in the year file and lore.
- Day 6 onward: the asking for stones at the landing dawns in the words of item 8. Seven stones in the month. The Tally-keeper cut each on a stick of her own kind at the landing table. The Stallreeve forbade wagers at the beam.
- Day 8: the fish seller back with a mended cord, read level on the second trial.
- Day 22 (night): the star dimmed. Day 23: a boy asked whether the star is its own; silence.

**Month 2.**
- Five stones. The carriers and the old weigher came down in the first days. Day 8: his six stones weighed at the gate as 48, 96, 193, 384, 768 and 1,536 before the head of the hill goods, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve, the Assay-master's own beam brought for the three large stones; his saying of the seventh carried over. The carriers' scale read out a few parts in a hundred in their own favor and mended.
- Day 11: a young carrier asked whether it can be killed (answered) and what would break it (not answered). The Weightward asked the Stallreeve for the fourteenth in the words of item 9. He gave it, on his own terms: the household told the night before by the tide-reckoner's child, not found.
- Day 14: the walk to the pans. The post stood empty, two of the watch at it, no notch cut. The household turned out every basket unasked. It weighed a basket it chose itself and called it clean salt. The head of the household put a pinch in its pan and it took it. It was back before dusk. Day 17: the weavers' master said at the post that it weighed a basket.
- The boat people were gone from the channels from the twelfth and back on the sixteenth. Nobody followed.

**Month 3.** Rain from the first days. Four stones, among them the boatwrights' yard's door-stone and the smiths' master's hearth slab (5,960, the heaviest). Day 14: the potters' hall's own scale read level at the gate. Day 20: a young stallholder asked whether it can tell who is lying and the follow-up; answered by the decided words.

**Month 4.** Wet. Four stones. Day 18: the net-maker's door-stone, 3,011. Day 20: the Assay-master's six scales, two level and four a hair out. Day 24: the boatwrights' lease, stood witness.

**Month 5.** Hard spell. Three stones. Fever in the back houses, fewer than the year before, four stalls and four braziers under the hall's roof from the first frost, a fifth brazier after the old woman's question. Day 11: the tide-reckoner's word told at the post; the old woman asked whether it can cure a fever. Two nights of the star dimming.

**Month 6.** The leanest of the stores. Three stones. Day 15: the ring's winter sitting, door shut, the Weightward not sent for. Day 17: the Stallreeve's word at the post, counts and one reason. His asking of it what is now to be cut for the carrier; silence. The open-water owner asked in the open and said it changes nothing he carries.

**Month 7 to the fourteenth.** The thaw, the Alder rising, an ordinary flood foretold. Four stones. Day 2: a netmaker's question about the height of the water; silence. Day 5: the master of the potters said at the post that the third nest is shaped and drying on its boards. Day 7 (night): the star dimmed. Day 14: the gate done, the Weightward, the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper walked to the potters' hall and came to its door.

## How the god was met

- **The yard.** After the fifth the line ran as before. The yard took the word as a thing the Weightward had said before them, with its own cuts and its own flaws, and was proud and ashamed of it in the same breath. They learned the figure 3,072 within three days. "Running the cups" became what the landing says of anyone who counts before he speaks.
- **The Stallreeve.** Kept the second stick for the ring and said so. Forbade wagers at the beam. Gave the day at the pans on his terms (told, not found). Carried the figure of the door-stone to the eleven at dusk, second, as it said. Forbade buying a stone at a landing for its weight. Brought the ring's answer to the post on the first market after the sitting, with counts and one reason, and asked it then what it would have cut for the carrier.
- **The Assay-master.** Spoke on the fifth and was heard. Stood with the old weigher's stones with his own beam. Checked six scales with it in the fourth month. Went to look at the door-stone and said what he saw. At the post on the seventeenth of the sixth month said the fifth was his own reading. Walked with it on the fourteenth of the seventh.
- **The Tally-keeper.** Cut the word on a pair of sticks and split both. Cut a stick for every stone at the landing's table. Cut no notch on the empty day. Cut the Stallreeve's report at the post, thing by thing, and read it back.
- **The ring.** Heard the second stick on the fifteenth of the sixth month and took six things and refused two. It did not send for the Weightward. It gave the Stallreeve leave for one reason.
- **The ship owners.** The channel owner brought the first stone and brought another. The open-water owner brought both coasters' anchors and kept the carrier's half. Neither asked it for anything the decided words did not answer.
- **Others.** The master of the smiths brought a forge slab and asked it nothing. The master of the weavers watched the day it walked to the pans and said it weighed a basket. The head of salt kept his word that his people would not ask. The old net-maker kept her step. The raking household turned out every basket unasked.

## What was done in the god's name

By a helper: nothing; none serves.

By mortals, where it said so or plainly took it for granted:
- The Stallreeve and the Tally-keeper kept its asking for the fourteenth of the second month: the watch kept the line, nobody was read, no notch was cut. The Stallreeve told the yard so himself.
- The Stallreeve carried the door-stone's figure to the eleven at dusk of the eighteenth of the fourth month, since it had said the ring hears it second.
- The Stallreeve said at the post what the ring said, as he had told it he would.

By mortals, not in its name: the Tally-keeper's stone sticks, the Stallreeve's ruling against wagers and against buying stones for their weight, the Assay-master's visit to the net-maker's step, the boatmen's name for the cups. No seal, licence, promise or ruling was given. It read no bargain and no person.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

None. Each thing that could have gone two ways was a mortal's own will and mine to settle: the Stallreeve's leave and his terms; the household's welcome; the ring's counts and reasons; the Assay-master's sorting at the door-stone; the yard's talk. The settled readings were taken as given: the basket at the pans clean salt; no stone within a twentieth but the door-stone; the door-stone at 3,011; the second stick taken and refused as the release says.

One thing for the Keeper's eye that is not an outcome. The stones at the landings are the Weightward's own readings and I wrote them as it would have called them. If a stone should read otherwise, the table above is the only place they are written together.

## What touches anyone else

- **The south coast.** The first stick waits in the carrier's keeping. The second stick's contents were said before the yard and are known to anyone who was at the post, and the carriers took them up the old road. Nothing was sent. The ring's answer on the second stick is that six things are the town's word and two are not; no second stick has been cut for the carrier.
- **Ruddfold.** The old weigher came down with the carriers, had his six stones weighed, said the saying of the seventh, and went home with the figures on his stick. He had already heard from the carriers that the hall said before the yard what it did not know. The carriers say Ruddfold will make something of sixty-one.
- **The Aldermark.** No one came in the stretch, and none are asked for. What traders hear in the summer is past the held moment and not written.
- **The boat people.** Gone from the channels at the lowest tide of autumn, back on the sixteenth. The Weightward watched old women of the flats go along the shore toward the Maun Stair and did not follow them.
- **The tide-reckoner.** Called the winter water four days ahead in the fifth month, right in height and a few hours off the hour, and lost the feeling in her hands for the season. In the seventh month she said she did not need her feet for an ordinary flood.
- **The raking household.** Told the day before, turned out every basket unasked, was weighed and called clean. The town's word is that it was looked at and came out clean.
- **The old net-maker's household.** Has a door-stone four people tried to buy. A boy chipped it with a nail.
- **The eleven's counts on the second stick.** Said at the post and cut by the Tally-keeper. They are on a stick in the town and the town knows them.

## Asked of the god

"Answered" means by its decided words or its conduct profile. "Silence" means no decided words answer it and it met silence, pending. "Held" means held. I held nothing in this stretch beyond the moment at the potters' hall door, which is the Keeper's board to write.

1. Month 1 from the 6th, the owners and captains at the landings, on hearing the asking: whether it is the seventh they are to bring or any stone. Answered by item 8: it asks for large stones, weighs each, says only what it is.
2. Month 1, day 23, a boy at the gate: "Is the star yours?" Silence. Pending. The line told him to hush.
3. Month 1 onward, the yard: where it sleeps, how long it will stay, whose child it is. Answered by its standing answers.
4. Month 2, day 11, a young carrier from Ruddfold: "Can you be killed?" Answered by item 10: "No. I can be hurt, held or scattered, and I come together again." The same man: what would break it. Answered by item 10: it does not answer, unless the ring asks in session.
5. Month 2, day 14, a boy at the empty post: "Is the star yours?" Silence. Pending. The watch had no answer to give for it.
6. Month 3, day 20, a young stallholder: "Can you tell who is lying?" Answered by the decided words (what it said before the ring). Then: whether the man it read knows it has read him. Answered by item 11: both pans show, and anyone it reads knows whether it means its own terms.
7. Month 4, day 18, the old net-maker: whether the stone is the seventh. Answered by item 8: it says only what it is. And whether it wants her step for the hall. Answered by item 8: it is not asking anyone to give anything up.
8. Month 4, day 20, the Assay-master: "Check these six scales." Answered by conduct: it checks what he has passed when he asks.
9. Month 4, day 24, two households of boatwrights: stand witness to a lease and say what you saw and heard, "and we ask nothing of the other thing." Answered by conduct: it stood and said what it saw and heard, and no more.
10. Month 5, day 11, an old woman of the back houses: "Can you cure a fever? It is my daughter's boy." Answered by item 10: "No. I have no power for it." The woman who asked it a year before did not come to the gate.
11. Month 6, day 17, the Stallreeve, as himself: what would it have cut for the carrier now, since the things the ring took are not on the stick he holds. Silence. Pending. Nothing more is cut in this stretch.
12. Month 7, day 2, a netmaker: "Can you tell the height of the water as the tide-reckoner does?" No decided words answer it. Silence. Pending. He said it was good at that too.
13. Throughout, at the landings: captains and owners asking to be read on their dawn. Answered by the standing terms of the dawn readings.

Not asked of it in the stretch: the master of the smiths asked nothing, at the gate or in session. The woman of the back houses did not come. The ring did not ask it anything in session; it was not sent for. No bargain was offered to be read. No seal or licence was asked. No one from outside the town sought it out.

The town's names for it were not said to it: "Bren's weigher", "the Cupman", "the coat", and "running the cups" for the habit.

## What the people tell their god

- **The Stallreeve** would tell it that the ring took six things on its second stick and refused two, and that it was the ring's to refuse; that he gave it one reason and no more; that there is time before the coasters go; that the pans day cost the market no notch and the household came out clean; that the stone at the net-maker's step was a door-stone and the Assay-master said so; that the yard says sixty-one short.
- **The Assay-master** would tell it that the fifth is light by a hair on his own beam and he would read it so again; that the ring refused to carry it south and could not refuse to know it; that the net-maker's stone was never cut.
- **The Tally-keeper** would tell it that she has cut every stone and every day and her count holds, and that the ring's counts are on her stick for anyone who can read a notch.
- **The open-water owner** would tell it that he carries what is on his stick as cut, and the pair he would not promise stands as he said it.
- **The old net-maker** would tell it that the stone is her step and she will have it back on her step by dark.
- **The head of the raking household** would tell it that her household was looked at and came out clean, and that the flats' salt is clean now because it was made to look.
- **The old weigher of the hill kin** would tell it that the hill stones and the hall's agree and that agreeing was what he came to see.
- **The town** would tell it that it was proud and ashamed of the word it gave, that the yard has the figure 3,072 and "sixty-one short" by heart, that the boatmen run their cups after it, that the master of the smiths said nothing and the master of the weavers watched, that the ring said no to two things and the town does not know why, and that the star dimmed on the night of the twenty-second of the first month, on two nights of the fifth, and on the seventh night of the seventh.
