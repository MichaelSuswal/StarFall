# The unnamed people: Year 2

Campaign sim-06. Written by the civ agent. First the tail of Year 1, from the market day in the eleventh month when the Stallreeve laid the half stick at the gatepost, then Year 2 from early autumn round to early autumn. Months are counted from the opening of each year. Names are used only where a mortal said one aloud in the stranger's hearing (see 002-report.md). Everything else is by office or plain description. Where a thread is held for the Keeper it stops at its moment and is written in the report.

## The end of Year 1, after the gatepost

**The market day, month 11.** The stranger took up the half stick in front of the yard and said yes. It said it would weigh the hall's old weights before the eleven, at the first market after the grain was in, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. It spoke its terms aloud to the yard so that nobody could say they were set in private: that it weighs each piece in its pans and calls it in its cups, out loud, one at a time; that the Tally-keeper cuts what it calls and the Assay-master checks each reading on his own beam beside its own; that it does not set the town's standard but says what each piece is now, and the eleven decide which is the town's weight; and that if the eleven want the potters' new nest weighed the same day, the potters' master may bring it. The Stallreeve carried the terms up to the hall that evening, with two more it asked him to put to the eleven as the hall's own and not as its habits: that the weighing is done in the hall and nothing leaves the hall, and that every figure it calls is cut, the Assay-master's reading cut beside it, and where the two differ both cut.

It asked the Stallreeve where the old weights are kept and who holds the key. He said that was the eleven's to tell and he would put it to them. It said it would wait.

It named a mark to the Tally-keeper, two pans, level, nothing else. She cut it at the head of her stick of the gate's readings, in front of the line. It said to the yard that it had not said yes to being Weightward and would say after the weighing, not before, and the yard did not like that and laughed anyway.

Of the bonded man taken at the landing in the winter, it said it would not claim a price from a bonded man, and asked the Tally-keeper to cut the price as remitted if his master's scale read level at the gate within the month. If the scale was not brought, or read out, the price would lie unclaimed as before. It said it was not asking for it either way. The Tally-keeper said she would cut it as said and that the council would hear of it.

The same day it read a stallholder's scale out, the selling pan heavy by about one part in forty in her own favor, and said so aloud: that it favored her, and nobody in the line was going to thank her for it. She took up her cord in the yard, with the line watching, and the scale read level on the second trial. She was not pitied and not laughed at. The line gave her a place to stand.

It gave its standing answers where the line could hear: how long it stays (till it has seen the town's weights set right, and past that it does not know); whose child it is (it could not say, it has a name it answers to and one it lost); what the fee is (a pinch of salt, fish, grain or a fired weight, weighed in its pans with the payer watching); and that a stallholder may say a scale was read on a day and what the reading was, which pan and how far, and may not say it is true. The salt-rakers took the fee answer as an opening and a good many pinches of salt were weighed that week.

**The next market, and the sticks.** It asked the Stallreeve's leave, with the Tally-keeper beside him, for split reading sticks: one stick for a scale, the day and the reading cut on it, one half the owner's and one half hers, holding for that day and no longer, lapsing if the scale is not read again within a season, her fee a pinch from the owner and nothing to the stranger. The Stallreeve gave leave on his own rule: no stick is hung on a stall or a scale, and if one hangs they all come down. The Tally-keeper took it on with her own addition: the stick ends at a season, and a notch is cut for each market a scale is not brought. Five sticks were cut that day. No mark of the stranger's is on them. It thanked her and asked her to change nothing.

At dusk of that market day, when the bench closed, it went up to the hall door with its brass, and the Assay-master met it there. What was said is in 002-report.md. The Assay-master told the Stallreeve afterward, in the hall with the door open, one thing of it: that the stranger said no king sent it and nobody was behind it that it knew of. He told him nothing else. The Stallreeve asked if the Assay-master believed it. The Assay-master said he had noted the clause "that I know of." The Stallreeve said that was a careful way to say no, and the clause was what he would have used himself.

**The weeks after.** The Stallreeve, next at the gate, was told by the stranger that his rule on the sticks was a good one. He said nothing for a moment. He said it had been the Tally-keeper's notch that made it a rule, and then he went back up to the stalls, pleased in the way of a man who will tell nobody.

The master of the potters was asked by the stranger to bring the new nest to the hall on the day and not to the gate, so the eleven would see it from the first. She said she would carry it up herself, in a reed basket on her back, and not in a cart through the yard.

Bren's people were asked, quietly and once, by the stranger at the landing, what they would be willing to say about the low-tide trips if someone asked. It did not ask to come and it did not follow. Bren answered for the household with the others standing by. She said they would say what the town says: that the water folk go out at the lowest tides and come back, that the salt-rakers say it is to the Maun Stair and the channel folk say it is to meet kin, and that none of them has ever followed or knows. She said they would say it again to anyone who asked as quietly as the stranger had, and that they would tell the Stallreeve first if the question came from anyone else. The stranger heard it and said nothing more. The household talked of it that night and decided the stranger was not asking because it meant to go.

The bonded man's master, the stallholder of the yard's corner whose scale had been read out by about a tenth, brought his scale to the gate in the third week of the month, in the open, in the yard's full line, on a market day. He had been seen in the weeks before mending it himself in the back of his stall. He stood at the head of the line with his arms folded, and he said nothing, which in a stallholder is a speech. The stranger read it and said it read level, two pans, level. The Tally-keeper cut the price as remitted. The stranger asked nothing of the master or of anyone. The yard did not know whether to call it generosity or a bargain and settled on neither. The bonded man went back to his master's corner with no price on his stick and no change to his bond. The master said at his stall, to anyone, that he had never been a cheat and that it was a cheap scale in the first place. The town said that was what a cheat would say too.

**The eleven, before the harvest.** The council sat in the first half of the twelfth month, before the reaping. The Stallreeve carried the yes and the terms. The eleven took them whole, as the hall's own rules for the day, eight to three, the open-water ship owner in the room this time and voting with the eight. The three against were the same three as before: the head of the hill goods, the master of the smiths, the head of salt.

The head of salt spoke longest, and said again what he had said in the spring: that a store of worth is made by agreeing what the weight is, and that a stick cut once cannot be recut, so that every figure cut on the day is a figure the hall will be held to by anyone who can read a notch. The master of the smiths said again that a stranger's brass is a stranger's brass, and asked that a smith's hand sit on the beam beside the Assay-master's. He was refused: the terms said the Assay-master's, and the eleven had the Assay-master's hand because he held the office. He did not come to the next sitting. The head of the hill goods did not speak against the terms. He asked that his witness from Ruddfold, who would come down with his own stone weights, be given a place at the beam on the day. The Assay-master said the hall's beam is the hall's and the Weigher's calls are the Weigher's, and the witness might set his stones against the old ones and have his readings cut, and nothing called in ones; the head of the hill goods said that would do. He paid for that by becoming the witness's surety, so that anything the old man said in the ring would be the head's to answer for.

The cost to the head of salt fell in his own trade. The salt-rakers' own scales had been going to the gate for a year. A woman of the pans stood up at the salt-rakers' sitting that week and said the Weigher had read her scale without taking a grain more than the pinch, and that she did not see a store of worth cracked. He was greeted at the pans for a month with "read at the gate," said with a straight face. The eleven did not alter his place.

The eleven answered the stranger's question about the old weights. The Stallreeve spoke it at the gatepost with two of the watch standing near: that the old weights are kept in the chest in the hall, in the room under the old keeper's loft; that the chest has two keys; that the second is the Assay-master's; and that the first has hung in the ring since the laying-out, by the custom that a dead keeper's key hangs until a new keeper takes it, and is not turned. No one had been told by the eleven what the chest held beyond that. The stranger said nothing. The Assay-master was in the room when the eleven agreed the answer, and said what the custom was. He did not say what he knew of the rack.

**The year's end.** The grain came in, well enough. The salt pans ran. The reading sticks were in use among the stalls by the score, and the Tally-keeper cut them at the end of each market in a corner of the hall with a lamp, and the Stallreeve's rule held: no stick hung anywhere. The yard at the gate was long on market days and short on the others. The town said it was a thing to be argued over when the day came, and went on working. The potters' master had the nest wrapped in reed and sat with it in the potters' hall.

## Autumn, months 1 to 3

**Month 1.** The old weigher of the hill kin came down the Old Rudd Way alone, a few days before the market, at the head of the hill goods' asking, with a leather bag of his own stone weights on his back. A small bent man, grey, hill-tall once and stooped now, he lodged in the head's house and said nothing in the yard. He watched the gate for two mornings and spoke to nobody there.

The first market of the autumn was the day. The Stallreeve held the yard back from the hall's door with the watch, and the door stood open so the yard could hear. The Tally-keeper sat at the end of the bench with a long fresh stick, her apprentice at the door to call each figure to the yard once she had cut it, as the runners call a ruling. The eleven sat in their ring. The stranger stood where it had been told to stand, at the end of the bench with its small brass pair on a board and the nest of cups at its hand, and said nothing until it was told to begin.

The Assay-master read the terms aloud as the hall's rules for the day. He opened the chest with the second key, in front of the ring, and lifted out the rack and set it on a cloth. The rack is cut for seven weights. It held six. The seat for the largest was empty, and clean where the other seats are dusty. He said it plainly: he had found it so when he opened the chest after the laying-out, and twice since, and it was so each time; he had told no one but the Stallreeve, the evening before, and then only that the rack was not full. The head of reed and fish asked whether he had opened it alone. He said alone, both times. The ring said nothing for some time.

The three smallest pieces are old bronze and fit the stranger's pans. It called them aloud in its own ones: forty-eight, ninety-six, a hundred and ninety-two. The three larger are cut stone and too heavy for its pans, and they went on the hall's own beam with the Assay-master's hand on it and the stranger standing over it with its small brass in its hand. It called three hundred and eighty-four, seven hundred and sixty-six, fifteen hundred and thirty-six. The Assay-master checked each on his beam beside its calls, making the second of each pair with the sand bag the old keeper had taught him to balance against the lower piece. Each piece balanced two of the one below it. The fifth, the stone with the old chip at its foot, he read at first as level and then, on the second trial, as light by a hair, and said aloud that he had read it wrong the first time; the Tally-keeper cut both readings. Nowhere else did his beam and the stranger's calls differ.

The Assay-master then did what the old keeper's practice was: he set four of the hall's small fired pieces against the first bronze. The hall's side went down. He said so. He asked the Tally-keeper what the stranger had called his eight good pieces a year ago at the door, and she read it from her stick: about twelve and a half, each. The head of the hill goods did the sum aloud. Four of the hall's make fifty, and the first old weight is forty-eight. The hall's good pieces run heavy against the old standard by about a twentieth. The Assay-master said that he had tested every scale in the town for thirty years against these pieces and that if they were heavy then every scale he passed was passed heavy. He said he had not known it. He did not sulk.

The master of the smiths then spoke to the stranger across the ring. He asked where the seventh was, what it weighed, and whether it could find it. The stranger gave no answer. The master of the smiths said it was good at that, and the Assay-master said the stranger weighs what is put in front of it and there was nothing there to weigh.

The clay head put out bread and a jar of water for the stranger at midday, as the hall's custom is for a guest who works through a meal. It said it took no fee and no gift for the day, from the hall or from any of the eleven. The clay head put the bread away.

The master of the potters then brought out the nest of eleven new clay standards, and the ring agreed to have it weighed since it was there. The stranger called each in its pans, smallest first: thirteen, twenty-five, thirty-seven, fifty-two, seventy-five, eighty-nine, ninety-eight, a hundred and thirty, a hundred and fifty, a hundred and seventy-nine, a hundred and eighty-one. The Assay-master set them against the hall's fired pieces and against each other. They did not agree with themselves. Two of the second did not make the fourth, which went down by two. The master of the potters said they had been fired in one kiln so they would shrink alike. The ring looked at the nest. She asked that it be left in the hall under the Assay-master's keeping and not carried back through the yard, and the Assay-master agreed. All the nest sat about a twentieth heavy against the old weights, as the hall's pieces do.

The old weigher of the hill kin then set his stones on the hall's beam beside the old six. The Assay-master let him on the terms the eleven had agreed: his readings cut, nothing called in ones. His stones agreed with the old bronze to the beam's tremble at the first and second, ran a hair heavy at the third, agreed at the fourth and sixth, and at the fifth went down a hair against the old fifth. He said in the slow voice of the hill kin that Ruddfold's stones had been the hill kin's weight for longer than anyone alive could say, that the hill kin had sold ore and charcoal by the hall's pieces for as long as the town had had a hall, and had supposed the hall's pieces heavy for as long, and had said nothing because a market is a market. He asked the stranger to weigh his stones in the ring and got no answer. He took the figures down on a stick of his own, and no one stopped him, since the figures were called aloud in front of the ring and a stick is a stick.

The ring then argued the day's work. The head of the hill goods moved that the old six be the town's weight and the hall's pieces be made again to them. The head of reed and fish and he voted for it, five against, four not voting, and it failed. The head of salt moved that the hall's pieces stand as the town's weight and the old six be kept as a thing of the hall's past. He and the master of the smiths voted for it, six against, three not voting, and it failed. The clay head moved that no weight be named the town's until the seventh was found or the ring had given it up and the nest had been fired again; that the market stand on the hall's present pieces meanwhile; that every debt and bargain made before the day be reckoned by them as made; that nothing in the hall be filed, mended, replaced or carried out; that the chest be opened only before two of the eleven; and that the matter come back to the first sitting after the next grain was in. It carried, nine for, one against (the head of the hill goods) and one not voting (the master of the smiths). The head of salt voted for it for the clause about debts. The ring also agreed, without a count, that the old keeper's loft, which custom keeps shut until a new keeper takes the seat, be looked in by three. The stranger was not asked what it thought and did not say.

The Stallreeve told the yard from the step, in the open and a little late, what the ring had done and what the hall's four pieces had shown. He said he would sooner they heard it from him than from a boy at the stalls. The yard took it badly for an hour and well for a day. The hall's pieces were heavy, a twentieth, and always had been. Sellers said buyers had had a twentieth of them for forty years. Buyers said the hall's pieces were the town's weight and always had been, whatever a stone said. The Stallreeve ruled at the stalls the next market that the hall's present pieces stood as they had until the eleven said otherwise, and that anyone who asked for the old reckoning in a bargain made after the day should say so in the bargain and have the Tally-keeper cut it.

At dusk the eleven rose, the yard went to its supper, and the Assay-master stayed in the hall with the stranger when the door was shut. What passed there is in the report as a held matter and it is not written here.

On the third day after, three went up the old keeper's loft stair: the Assay-master, the Stallreeve, and the clay head as witness, by the ring's agreement, which waived the custom that the loft stand shut until a new keeper took the seat. They came down within the hour. What they said afterward is in the Keeper's hands. The town noted that they came down quiet.

The old weigher of the hill kin went back up the Old Rudd Way at first light the next morning, ahead of the carriers, with the figures on his stick. He did not wait for the carriers. He said the road did not get shorter.

**Month 2.** The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides and came back. Nobody followed. More people looked this time, because the town had been told by Bren's household that the stranger had asked about it, and the story went round that the stranger meant to follow. It did not.

The blue star deepened on some nights early in the month and stood dim in the middle of the night for about an hour, and then it was as before. The old said it was the drowned god turning in its sleep. The young said it had come with the stranger. The line said nothing of it, because by then the stranger had said it watched the light and meant to learn its habits, and the line was a little afraid of a thing that watched.

The hill carriers came down the old road in the middle of the month with ore and charcoal, as every year. They had the old weigher's stick of figures from his own mouth, and they asked to have the carriers' scale read at the gate, and it was read, and it was a little out, in the carriers' own favor by a few parts in a hundred. They took it without a word and mended it at the stalls. The head of the hill goods said it was a thing his kin would sooner have heard from the gate than from a buyer.

The Stallreeve went down to the landing alone one evening a few days after the carriers' market. He is a former boatman, and the boatmen at the landing below the hall are his old company. He asked them about the old Weightward's last weeks: who came to see him, who he sent word to, whether any boat took anything for him. What the boatmen answered is the Keeper's.

The stranger asked the Stallreeve whether the watch would keep the line and the Tally-keeper keep her sticks, for a day at the diggers' pits. He said the watch would keep the line and nobody would be read; that the Tally-keeper would cut no notch against any scale for a day the gate was shut by the stranger's own word; and that the day should be a day without a market, so no scale lost a notch. The Tally-keeper said the same. The day was set with Orrin, the east kiln master, for the last week of the second month, a day with no market and no rain.

On the day the stranger went out at first light with its small brass and came back before dusk, and the gate stood empty with a watchman at the post. At the pits on the clay banks the diggers, the kiln masters and the potters had all come, and the heavy digger from the lower pit stood a little apart. Orrin brought three lumps from loads that had fired well, and a kiln master of the west kilns brought three from loads that had cracked; one lump in each set came from the lower pit. The stranger weighed each in front of them and called what the pan said: the weight, the clay, and the part that was not clay, sand and shell. It weighed ninety-six, ninety-one clay, five not clay; a hundred and four, ninety-nine clay, five not; eighty-eight, eighty-four clay, four not, from the well fired. From the cracked it weighed a hundred, ninety-six clay, four not; ninety-two, eighty-eight clay, four not; a hundred and eight, a hundred and two clay, six not. Each was about a twentieth part sand and shell, a hair more or less, and the cracked lumps carried no more of it than the lumps that fired well. Orrin and the west kiln master looked at the figures and said the two sets do not look different. It said that it had then told them nothing about the cracking. It said it could not tell which clay is good for a kiln and was not going to pretend to.

The diggers cheered, which the kiln masters did not forgive. The heavy digger said it proved what he had said, that his clay was finer and denser and not gritty. The kiln masters said a lump is not a basket. Orrin said nothing for a while, and then he said that if it was not the grit then it was something he could not see in a pan. He went home. The council did not alter the heavy digger's price. Two kiln masters took lower-pit clay again of their own accord, at the lower price.

**Month 3.** Rain came in, the pits on the downs filled, and the potters, who had long quarreled with the diggers over grit, were left with no grit to quarrel about and no other cause. Orrin began firing a small tile of each load's clay in a corner of his kiln before he filled it, to see whether the tile would crack when the load would, and the east kilns followed him. The kiln masters who had weighed a basket of every load kept doing it for the habit, and some stopped.

The first stale reading stick turned up at the end of the month, a netmaker's scale not brought in a season. The Tally-keeper cut the lapse, as her rule was, and showed the stick at the gate. The stranger looked at it and said nothing, and read the next scale. The notch rule held.

Some stallholders carved a small two-pans mark into the beam of their scales, after the mark the Tally-keeper cuts. The Stallreeve ordered it scraped off: the mark is the Tally-keeper's stick, he said, not your beam. It was scraped.

## Winter, months 4 to 6

**Month 4.** The wet came, with its hard spells. The ship owner of the channels, who had done well from southern goods in the summer and wanted the same again, went down to the landing at dusk, where the stranger sat by the channel folk's lamp, and put a thing to it in front of Bren and two boatmen. The thread is held and is in the report. In the town the ship owner said only that he had spoken to it and that it had listened.

The potters' master fired nothing that month. The ring had said the nest should be fired again, and she waited for the cold to ease, and said that no clay fired in a hard frost keeps faith with its own weight.

**Month 5.** A hard spell, ice on the shallows. The fever came into the short-stilted houses at the back of the bank and took the old and the very young, in the ordinary way, fewer than last year, because the Stallreeve had kept four stalls and two braziers under the hall's roof from the first frost and the old came there to sit. The tide-reckoner of the flats sent word four days ahead, by the same child, that the Alder would crest over the lowest paths. It crested at the height she gave and a few hours off the hour. The stalls moved, the boats were drawn up, and three families went to the mounds with their goats. The feeling went out of her hands for the season, as always. The Stallreeve told the line the word at the gatepost, saying the Alder would crest, and the stranger heard it and read the next scale.

The blue star went dim in the middle of a night in the dead of winter for about an hour and then was as before. The watch saw it, and the boatmen.

**Month 6.** The deepest of the cold and the leanest of the stores. The grain bins were lower than the Stallreeve liked, and not low. The dried fish held. The hill carriers' charcoal was nearly gone by the last week, and a few forges went cold for some days.

In the last week of the month two households of boatwrights asked the stranger to stand witness to the sale of a boat. It stood with two of the watch beside it, and said afterward what it had seen and heard, the price named, the hands shaken, the day, and that it would say no more. Neither side asked whether it thought the other meant it, and it did not say.

## Spring, months 7 to 9

**Month 7.** The flood season. The rains eased, the Alder rose, the weirs were mended. The tide-reckoner was asked by the Stallreeve to tell the height. She sent back that it would be an ordinary flood and she did not need her feet for an ordinary flood. The channels moved as they do and a reed island split and was quarreled over by two households. They asked the stranger, in the yard, who owned it. It said owning is not a weight, and that it would weigh what each household said was cut or drawn from it, if they brought it. They took it to the council.

The master of the potters fired the nest again, as the ring had said. She fired twenty-two pieces in one kiln and chose eleven from them by the gate's scale, bringing the pieces to the line like anyone and having each read and cut. Whether the new nest agrees with itself is not settled in this file; see the report, where it is written as an attempt. The first nest is kept in the hall under the Assay-master. The council has not asked for either to be weighed before the ring, and she has not asked.

**Month 8.** The spring flood came, ordinary in height. Sowing took five weeks of every hand that could hold a hoe. The boat people were gone for some days at the lowest tides of spring and came back. The blue star dimmed on a night in spring, for about an hour. The old said again that it was the drowned god, and the young said again that it came with the stranger, and the two sides went on saying it.

**Month 9.** The council sat after the sowing and, for the first time, brought the Weightward's seat to the ring. The clay head and the master of the potters had brought it forward, and the town knew it was coming. The Assay-master spoke for it, on his own terms: that the seat should be an office, not a gift, with a term set and the eleven's hand on it, and that his hand should sit beside the stranger's on the beam. The master of the weavers spoke against, for the first time: he said he weighs wool and knows what a man is when he weighs him, and had not weighed this one. The head of salt, the master of the smiths and the head of the hill goods spoke against as before. It carried, six to four, one absent: the open-water ship owner, out on the Gulf. The Stallreeve carried the eleven's word to the gatepost on the next market day, in front of the yard. That moment is held and is in the report.

After it, the council went home, and the town waited as a town waits on a council that decides little. The yard cried "Weightward" at the stranger a few days later, a boy first, and nobody answered.

## Summer, months 10 to 12

**Month 10.** Long and warm. The reed was cut early. A coaster of the southern harbor towns came up the main channel to the town's own landing, as happens some summers and did not happen last year. Her master is a woman of about forty. She traded at the landing for some days, took salt, pots and reed mats and paid in goods from the south coast, and asked the news of the town as such masters do, who bought what and what a pot fetched. She asked the Stallreeve where she might have her scale read and he told her the line. A man of her crew stood at the gatepost for most of one market morning watching the readings and went away without a scale. On the next market day she joined the line, before light, like anyone, and stood in it as it moved. At the head of the line she stood in front of the stranger. That thread is held and stops there. The town's side of it ends there.

The first boats from the Aldermark came down in the summer to trade, with grain, wool and some bronze. No one came from the king. Two traders had their scales read and went away with the story that the hall's own pieces had been found heavy, which the town was a little ashamed of and could not stop being told.

**Month 11.** Dry and warm. The channels ran low. The Stallreeve brought two disputes of weight to the gate in a week and ruled on the readings at the stalls, as he does. The hall's present pieces went on as the market's weight, and the old reckoning was asked for in a few bargains and cut on the stick each time.

**Month 12.** The grain came in, well enough. The salt pans ran. The blue star dimmed late in the summer on a night with no cloud, and the harvesters in the fields saw it. The council sat after the grain, as the ring had said, and took up the weight again. It could not decide which was the town's. It said it would wait on the Weigher's answer about the seat, and that the weight would stand where it stood. The ring decided nothing and went home.

The year turned. The first market of the new autumn is the next market.

## Why the numbers moved

See state.md. In short: an ordinary year with a wet winter. The market was louder than a year ago and no worse for it: the old quarrel about the bench had come out at last, and the town had a place to put it. The fever was lighter. The grain came in. Treasure moved little. The watch did not grow.
