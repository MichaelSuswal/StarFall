# The unnamed people: Year 4

Campaign sim-06. Written by the civ agent. This turn covers one stretch only: the part of Year 3 that follows the held moment at the gatepost, from the fifth day of the first month to the fourteenth day of the seventh month. The rest of Year 4 is not yet told. Months are counted from the opening of the year. The market falls every third day, the fifth, the eighth, the eleventh and on, without a break across the months. Names are used only where a mortal said one aloud in the Weightward's hearing (see 004-report.md). Everything else is by office or plain description.

## Year 3 again, from the fifth day of the first month

`003.md` is set aside from the fifth day of the first month. Where it and this section differ, this section is what happened. The stretch ends on the fourteenth day of the seventh month, at the door of the potters' hall, which is held. Nothing past that door is written here.

The weather runs under all of it. The autumn was ordinary and dry until the third month, when the rain came in. The winter was wet, with its hard spells. The flood at the thaw was ordinary in height. The seventh month was mild.

### Month 1, from the fifth day

**The gatepost, the fifth.** A market morning. The line was long because the town knew the eleven had said yes and that the Weightward had been asked for the words. At the head of it stood a fish seller of the channel folk with a hand balance of wood and cord. The Weightward read it before it did anything else. It read out, the buying pan heavy by about one part in sixty. He was angry for a breath, said the reading back, paid his pinch of fish, and took the balance aside to mend the cord himself. He asked for no stick.

Then the Weightward took up the half-stick of the eleven's asking and gave the word before the yard, to be cut as it said it. The Tally-keeper cut it and stopped it where she needed to. The Assay-master spoke up once, on the rounded calls of the fired pieces, and the Weightward mended the line before the split: the Assay-master was right, and the word would not say less than the bench saw. It mended it a second time before the stick was read back. The Tally-keeper cut it on a pair of sticks and split both.

The first stick is the town's word. It gives the six old weights in the Weightward's ones, the eight fired pieces that agree, the chipped piece and the cracked piece, and that the fired pieces stay in use until the first market of the autumn that comes round next year, from which the old six are the town's weight. It says it is not a price and not an offer. The Stallreeve took it for the eleven. The ship owner of the open water, who had been sent for by a runner and had come within the morning, took the carrier's half. He will say it as cut, at Corrith, to the houses his coasters trade with and to the council's people there if they ask, when his coasters go out past the Horn.

The second stick the Stallreeve carried up to the eleven, each thing on it by itself, neither yes nor no. That the fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so. That the rack is cut for seven and the seventh is not in it, and the hall does not know what it weighed and has looked for it. That four of the fired pieces set against the first bronze sink against it, about a twentieth heavy. That the chipped piece and the cracked piece are light against the eight, by about a twentieth and about a tenth. That the whole ten have not been weighed against the old six piece by piece since, and the Tally-keeper has the figures on her stick of the first day. That any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read. That the hall keeps no weight that anyone may not see weighed. And the Weightward's asking that the word go in the order it said it, with nothing added. The Stallreeve said the second stick waits for the ring and that what the ring says to the rest he brings to the post. The Weightward said it would not push, and that if the ring said no to any of it it would want to hear which, so that the next word that goes is not the one that is cut short.

Before the yard it told the carrier how to carry the word. Carry it as cut and nothing more. If asked what the town's weight is, say what the stick says. If asked what it is, say it is the Weightward of the hall and that the Weightward answers yes or no about a power of its own, or does not answer. Do not say more than it did. Do not sell it as a price. If a house wants to send a scale to be read against the hall's, say it may come here and that it reads it at the gate like any. The carrier took the carrying as cut, the town's weight as the stick says it, and that it is no price. He would not promise the line on what the Weightward answers about itself, nor the line on a southern scale read at the hall, until the eleven have answered.

It told the Stallreeve, last, that it answers the master of the smiths and the woman of the back houses only when either next asks at the gate, or when the ring asks it in session. The Stallreeve said he would not carry it either. It did not go to them. The yard, which had heard both asks in the sixth month of the year before, heard this.

The line went on to the end of the morning. The fish seller came back at the next market, the eighth, with the cord mended and the balance read level on the second trial. He said nothing about it.

**The first night.** That night and every night after that it sat at the landing by the channel folk's lamp, awake. The boatmen who were up saw it do what it had not done before. Before it counted the day's readings over, it ran its thumbnail down the cups of its nest, smallest first, in order, one cup after another, and then counted. It did this the next night and the one after, and did not hurry it or hide it. By the end of the month the boatmen had a name for it and three reasons. The name was "running the cups." The reasons: that it was saying the cups their names; that it was seeing none had been taken, since the one who tried in the sixth month of the first year had put his hand to the nest; and that it was counting up to the seventh, since the nest has seven cups and the rack has seven seats. Bren said her mother counted her beads that way and it was nobody's business. The children at the landing took it up on their own knuckles, smallest finger first, and were told to stop.

**The asking at the landings.** On the sixth, the first dawn after the gatepost, it stood at the channel ship owner's landing, where it has the first dawn and a standing place, and said before the owner, his captains and whoever was on the planks what it was doing. It wanted to look for the seventh by what it weighs. Twice the sixth, by the run of them, is three thousand and seventy-two in its ones. On its dawns at the landings it asked the ship owners and the captains to bring it any large stone they own that might be near that: anchors, ballast, door-stones, quern stones, hearth stones. It would weigh each on the landing's beam with the owner watching and say only what it is. It was not asking anyone to give anything up. If one turned out close, it would tell the Stallreeve and the Assay-master first and the ring second.

The channel ship owner said nobody would say he had not brought the first, and had the anchor stone of one of his salt boats up from the hull that morning on a plank. It weighed two thousand two hundred and forty. The Tally-keeper, who was at the landing's table for the boats, cut it on a stick of its own kind: the day, the landing, the stone, the figure, the landing's mark on the back, one half to the owner if he wanted it. The next dawn a captain brought the ballast stone he kept for his own trim, eleven hundred and fifty. By the ninth the open-water owner had two men carry the great anchor stone of one of his coasters from his landing house to the beam, which took four, and it read four thousand three hundred and seventy. He said it was no seventh but it was his. By the nineteenth he had brought the second coaster's anchor, four thousand one hundred and eighty, and a captain's hearth stone had read two thousand four hundred and seventy, and a captain's household had sent down a quern stone of eight hundred and eighty, and a small owner had had the ballast of his two boats weighed, nineteen hundred and sixty. In all seven came to the beam in the month, at the channel landing, the open-water landing and one small owner's. Nothing it was shown stood near three thousand and seventy-two.

It took no pinch for a stone, since it was the one who had asked for it. The boats were charged the pinch as before. Nobody was asked to give a stone up, and nobody did.

What the landings said of it. Within three days everyone on the water knew the figure and said it as they said the price of salt. "Three thousand and seventy-two" became a joke for a stubborn man and a hope for a bargain. The boatmen laid small bets, a fish or a pinch, on what the next stone would read, until the Stallreeve came down one dawn and said a pinch on a stone is a pinch on a scale and he would have no wager at the beam. They kept it to saying. The yard said the Weightward knew the stone it was looking for and was fishing. The channel folk said it was only being thorough. The salt-rakers said it was looking for what it had come to count. The hill carriers, when they came, said the seventh weighs what is owed, and would not say what was owed.

**The twenty-second and after.** On the night of the twenty-second the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. The Weightward was at the landing by the lamp, awake, and the boatmen who were up saw it watch the star and run its thumbnail down the cups and say nothing. On the next market morning, the twenty-third, a boy at the gate asked whether the star was its own. It gave him no answer and read the next scale. He said it was. The line told him to hush.

The grain was in, the pans had run, the autumn catch was ordinary. The hill carriers were not yet down.

### Month 2

Five stones came to the beam in the month, none near the figure: an anchor of sixteen hundred and forty on the first, Bren's own hearth stone, which four of her household carried up to the fish landing, on the third at two thousand and fifty, an anchor of thirty-six hundred and forty on the fourth, a ballast stone of fourteen hundred and twenty on the seventh, and a quern of twelve hundred and sixty on the tenth.

The carriers came down the old road in the first days of the month with ore and charcoal, as every autumn, and the old weigher of the hill kin came with them in a carrier's cart, a year older, with his bag of six stones on his knees. He lodged in the head of the hill goods' house and said nothing in the yard on the second or the fifth.

On the eighth he stood at the foot of the post with the head of the hill goods beside him, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve, who had said any of the ring who wished to see it might. The Weightward weighed his stones in its own cups: forty-eight, ninety-six, a hundred and ninety-three, three hundred and eighty-four, seven hundred and sixty-eight, fifteen hundred and thirty-six. The three smaller went into its pans. The three larger are too heavy for them and wanted a beam. The Assay-master sent his apprentice and two of the watch for his own beam from his house, saying the hall's beam was the hall's and did not leave it and his own did. It was set on trestles at the foot of the post. The master of the smiths asked to look at it and the Assay-master said it was not the hall's and he might. He lifted it, tried the pivot and the pans, said it was a good beam and said no more. The Weightward stood over it with its small brass in its hand and called the three large stones with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. The Tally-keeper cut every figure and her apprentice called them.

The old weigher said, slowly and in the voice of the hill kin, that the hall's old six and Ruddfold's stones had been the same weights as far as anyone knew, that he had not known whether the hall's were cut from the hill kin's or the hill kin's from the hall's and nor had his grandfather, and that agreeing to a hair was the only thing he had come down to see done in front of him. He had heard from the carriers what the Weightward said at the post in the first month, that the hall did not know what the seventh weighed and had looked for it, and he said Ruddfold had not heard a hall say that of itself. When the Weightward asked him, as its word to him had asked, what the hill kin say the seventh was, he said it was a story and he had not seen it. They tell it to boys in Ruddfold that the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, and that the hall dared not weigh with it. It was his grandfather's saying and he would not stand behind it. He asked nothing of the Weightward. He went home with the carriers after the carriers' market with the figures on a stick of his own. The head of the hill goods said the stone had weighed true and that the hill kin would hear it said.

On the eleventh, the carriers' market, the carriers had their own scale read at the gate and it was out a few parts in a hundred in their own favor. They mended it at the stalls without a word. A young carrier from Ruddfold, new to the road, stood in the line with nothing to sell and asked the Weightward whether it could be killed. It said: "No. I can be hurt, held or scattered, and I come together again." The yard heard it. The young carrier asked what would break it. It did not answer, and read the next scale. The master of the smiths, who was at his own stall across the yard, heard it with the rest and went on with what he was doing.

At the same market, before the line, the Weightward asked the Stallreeve for one day. It asked for the fourteenth, the day of the lowest tide, to walk out to the salt pans and look at the raking household that sent the salted baskets in the sixth month of the year before. It wanted to see the pans and weigh a basket there. The watch would keep the line, nobody would be read, and the Tally-keeper would cut no notch for the day. It said it left one thing unanswered when it went: what the master of the smiths had asked, which it would not answer before it went or when it came back unless the ring asked it in session.

The Stallreeve did not answer at once. He looked at the line, and at the sky as a boatman does, and said it was a market day and the market was his. He said the household had made good the sixth in clean salt and been turned out in front of the line since, and he would not have the matter reopened at their pans as though it were a trial. The Weightward said it was not asking to reopen it, only to look. He gave it. He said it was to go as it had asked and no other way: it would look, weigh a basket and say what it read, and it would not go as a thing that had been sent for. He would send word that night to the head of the household, by the tide-reckoner's child, that it was coming and why. The household would be told and not found. He said the market would be quiet without a post to read at and he would say so himself in the yard.

The household sent back by the child that the Weightward was to come and take what it liked. The head of salt, who is the household's patron, said nothing to the Stallreeve about it.

**The fourteenth.** The lowest tide of the autumn. The boat people had gone from the channels two days before, as they do at the lowest tides, and nobody followed. The gatepost stood without its keeper. Two of the watch stood at it and said to anyone who asked that the Weightward was at the pans and would be at the post on the seventeenth. The Stallreeve told the yard at first light, in the open, what it had asked and that he had said yes. The Tally-keeper cut no notch against any scale for the day. Stallholders traded by their own scales as they had before the gate, and two disputes of weight were held over to the seventeenth. A boy asked the watchman at the empty post whether the star was its own, and the watchman said the post was empty. The boy said then it could not say no. The line laughed.

It had gone out at first light by the causeway over the marsh, the 1.6 miles from the town to the near edge of the Flats, with a boy of the Flats who knew the causeway and who carries the tide-reckoner's word. It was there in the forenoon. The tide had gone out as far as it goes. The tide-reckoner, who lives at the edge of the Flats, sat on her step with her hands in her lap and watched it pass. She said to the boy, not to it, that he was to tell the Stallreeve the water had gone out when she said it would.

The raking household was waiting at the edge of its pans: the head of the household, a lean woman of about fifty-five with hands cracked white by salt, her sons and their wives, and some children. They had turned out every basket of the season's store on planks by the drying shed with a sieve beside each, without being asked, a dozen rows of them. The head said the household had been made to look and had looked, that the flats' salt had come to the landing clean since, and that it was welcome to anything it wanted to see. She said it plainly and a little hard.

The Weightward walked the pans. It went down the rows of shallow clay beds with their raked ridges, the sluice from the brine channel, the drying floor and the shed, and asked nothing. Nothing in the pans or on the planks was bulked, and nothing it was shown was anything but salt. It chose a basket for itself, the third from the bottom of the stack by the shed, not one of the turned-out rows, and weighed it on the household's own beam, with the head standing at the pan. It called the weight in its ones and said it read clean salt.

Nobody spoke until it had finished. Then the head said there would be no stick, since the Tally-keeper cut none that day, and the household would say what it read in the yard. She put a pinch of her salt in its pan. It weighed the pinch with her watching and took it. A younger son said under his breath that his mother had not slept for the three nights since the word came. She told him to be quiet. That was all anyone said.

On the Gulf side three old women of the flats went east along the shore with a cloth of bread, as they do at the lowest tide, toward the place they call the Maun Stair. They did not look at it and it did not follow them.

It was back at the landing before dusk and sat by the lamp that night and ran the cups before the count. On the seventeenth it was at the post. The line was long. The master of the weavers stood in it with a bolt of wool and said to the Stallreeve, in its hearing, that he had watched the post on the fourteenth to see what it did. The Stallreeve asked what that was. He said it weighed a basket. The Stallreeve said and then. The master of the weavers said and then it weighed a basket, and went on with his scale.

Of the household, the town said by the week's end that it had been looked at and had come out clean, and that this was a better thing to say than any the household could have said for itself. The head of salt said at the pans that a short measure and a cheat are different things and this was the proof. The boat people were back in the channels on the sixteenth and said nothing.

### Month 3

Rain came in in the first days of the month and the pits on the downs filled. The autumn catch was ordinary and the channel folk did well on it. The dawns at the landings ran on the terms spoken. The stones came on, four in the month.

On the fourth the master of the boatwrights brought the door-stone of his boatyard to the open-water owner's landing on a sledge with four of his men: twenty-seven hundred and sixty. He said his men had asked whether the seventh might be lying at their own gate, and he would sooner they knew than guessed. On the twelfth the master of the smiths had two apprentices sledge a hearth slab down from the lower forge to the channel landing, which read five thousand nine hundred and sixty, the heaviest it was shown. He stood by with his arms folded and said nothing to it, not a question and not a word of the ring. He nodded once, as at a good beam, when the figure was called. He told the Tally-keeper to cut it as a hearth, and had the apprentices drag it back. He asked it nothing, then or after. A kiln master's wife brought eighteen hundred and eighty of ballast to the channel landing on the first, and on the twenty-first a net-maker brought an anchor stone for a fishing boat to the fish landing that read eleven hundred and seventy.

On the fourteenth, a market, the master of the potters brought the potters' hall's own scale to the gate, a beam with brass pans, and had it read. It read level, and the Tally-keeper cut it. She said nothing of what she meant to do with it. The town knew she was to fire a nest to the old six in the spring, as the ring had asked, and that the clay had gone into the east kilns' bins.

On the twentieth, a market, a young stallholder who had been told by the Stallreeve a year before that the gate is for scales and the hall for the rest stood in the line and asked the Weightward whether it could tell who is lying. It said what it had said before the ring: that it will not say no, because that would be a false word; that when a bargain is struck in front of it, and it chooses to read that bargain, it knows of each party one thing, yes or no, whether at the moment of agreeing that party meant to keep the terms as spoken; that it does not know why, or what the lie is; that it reads a bargain and never a mind and knows nothing of a word spoken outside a bargain; and that it has not used it on anyone in the town and will not use it on anyone in the town without their asking. The young man asked what he supposed was the next thing, whether the man it read knew it had read him. It said the rest of it: that both pans show, and that anyone it reads knows whether it means its own terms. He thought about that for as long as the line allowed, and said it was a fair thing to have said, and went on to his stall. He did not ask it to read anything. Nobody did that season. The head of salt's people kept to their word and did not ask.

### Month 4

The wet came, with its hard spells. The stones came on, four in the month.

**The door-stone, the eighteenth.** A dawn at the fish landing, not a market. The Weightward was at the beam with the Tally-keeper at the landing's table, and the Stallreeve, who comes down to a landing some dawns as a former boatman does, was on the planks. An old net-maker of the landing, a woman of about seventy, came down the bank on a hand sledge pulled by her two grandsons and a neighbor, with a stone on it that took the four of them to lift onto the beam. She had heard the asking from her captain son-in-law. It was an undressed river stone, rounded, grey, with a hollow worn in the top of its door side where the foot of a door had turned. It was her door-stone. She said it had been at her husband's mother's door before it was at hers, and that it holds the door against the Alder when the water comes over the step, and that if the ring wanted a door-stone it could ask her what a door costs.

The Weightward weighed it with her watching. It said what it read. "Three thousand and eleven in my ones." Nothing more.

The landing was quiet for the length of a breath, and then somebody on the planks said sixty-one, and somebody said short by sixty-one, and the whole landing had it. The old net-maker asked whether it was the seventh. It said it says only what it is. She asked whether it wanted her step for the hall. It said it was not asking anyone to give anything up. She said then it was her step, and had her grandsons put it back on the sledge. The Tally-keeper cut the day, the landing, the stone, and three thousand and eleven. She asked the old woman whether she wanted the half. The old woman said she would keep it with the door.

By the end of the dawn it had told the Stallreeve again by name, he having heard it with the yard, and gone up to the hall with the Tally-keeper and told the Assay-master at the door, with the stick in her hand. It had said it would tell those two first and the ring second, and it did. It said what it had read and no more. The Stallreeve went round the eleven's doors at dusk with the figure and nothing else, so the ring had it second, from him, as the Weightward had said. The ring did not sit on it. Its sitting was two months off.

The Assay-master went down to the net-maker's step the next dawn, with the Tally-keeper beside him, and asked the old woman's leave to look at it. She gave it. He did not set it on a beam. He said his beam had no sixth to set it against outside the hall, and the Weightward's pans had called it and he could not fault the call, so he would look. He put his hand flat on the stone and said what he saw: that it was never cut, that there was no tool mark on it, that a weight is cut to the one below it and this had never been cut to anything, and that it was a river stone that had lain at a door. He said sixty-one in three thousand and seventy-two is a fiftieth, near enough, and that a stone of that make could be a fiftieth out of anyone's figure without being anything. He said it was a door-stone and was what the Weightward had said it was, and nothing more than that. He did not say what he thought of it beyond.

The yard had it by noon that the seventh had lain at a net-maker's door for a hundred years and been worn sixty-one lighter by her family's feet. The Assay-master said a stone worn by feet is flat where the feet were, and this one was round. The yard said that was what he would say. Four people came to the net-maker's door in the course of the day and offered a pinch, a pot, a goat and a week's fish for the stone. She sold nothing and put a grandson on it at night. A boy chipped at the edge of it with a nail and was caught by the neighbor. The Stallreeve said at the post on the next market that nobody was to buy a stone at a landing for the sake of its weight until the ring had said, and that the net-maker's step was the net-maker's.

In the yard "sixty-one short" went into the talk by the week's end. The hill carriers, down the road with a second load, said Ruddfold would make something of it, that if the seventh weighs what is owed then sixty-one was what was owed. They were laughing, but they said it twice.

For the rest of the month the stones that came were a quern of thirteen hundred and ten on the third, an anchor of thirty-three hundred and eighty on the ninth and a hearth stone of twenty-three hundred and ten on the twelfth. None stood within a twentieth of its figure.

**The twentieth, a market.** The Assay-master brought six scales he had passed in the autumn and asked the Weightward, as its terms allow, to check them. Two read level and four a hair out, as scales do. He mended the four himself, and cut nothing against his own name. He said it was the first thing it had checked for him that he would have found himself in a month, and that he preferred to have found it in a day.

**The twenty-fourth.** Two households of boatwrights asked it to stand witness to the lease of a boatyard and said, before the yard, that they asked nothing of the other thing. It stood, with two of the watch beside it, and said afterward what it saw and heard: the terms named, the hands shaken, the day, and that it would say no more. Neither side asked whether it thought the other meant it, and it did not say.

### Month 5

A hard spell, ice on the shallows. The fever came into the short-stilted houses at the back of the bank and took the old and the very young, as it does, and fewer than the year before. The Stallreeve had four stalls and four braziers under the hall's roof from the first frost and the old came there to sit. The stones came on, three in the month, fewer for the ice: a ballast stone of twenty-one hundred on the fourth, fifteen hundred and ninety on the thirteenth and thirty-seven hundred and twenty on the twenty-fifth, none near.

The tide-reckoner sent word four days ahead by the same child that the Alder would crest over the lowest paths. The Stallreeve told the line at the post on the eleventh and the Weightward heard it and read the next scale. The water came to the height she gave and a few hours off the hour. The feeling went out of her hands for the season.

On the eleventh also, an old woman of the back houses stood in the line with a bundle and asked the Weightward whether it could cure a fever, for her daughter's boy. It said: "No. I have no power for it." She nodded, as one does at a price one expected, and went back along the bank. The Stallreeve moved a fifth brazier under the hall's roof that week, and said it was because of the cold. The woman who had asked it the same thing the winter before did not come to the gate.

The star went dim in the middle of the night on two nights of the month, in the dead of winter, for about an hour each time, and then was as before. The watch saw it, and the boatmen, and anyone awake under a clear sky.

### Month 6

The deepest of the cold and the leanest of the stores. The bins were lower than the Stallreeve liked, not low. The dried fish held. The hill carriers' charcoal was nearly gone by the last week and a few forges went cold for some days. Two stones came before the sitting and none near: a quern of eight hundred and forty on the third and a back house's hearth stone of twenty-five hundred and ninety on the ninth.

**The sitting, the fifteenth.** The ring's winter sitting, by its custom, on a day with no market. The second stick lay before the eleven in the hall with the Tally-keeper at the end of the bench with her half. The door was shut. The yard had heard every word on the stick at the post on the fifth of the first month and was not let in. The Weightward was not sent for. It read at the landing at dawn as on any non-market day and sat at the lamp that night. The ring sat the afternoon and rose at dusk. The Stallreeve held the door and said, to the yard waiting on the hall's step, that what the eleven said he would bring to the post at the next market, as he had said to the Weightward he would, and that he had nothing to say before then. He kept it.

**The seventeenth, the post.** The line was long and the yard stood three deep. The Weightward was at the foot of the post, as every market morning, with a stallholder's scale in its hand.

The Stallreeve stood before it with the Tally-keeper at his side and her long stick, and said it as he had said the word of the seat. "The eleven sat on your second stick on the fifteenth, with the door shut, Weightward, and took each thing by itself. These they took as the town's word, to stand beside the first. That the hall does not know what the seventh weighed and has looked for it: eight for, none against, three not voting. The four fired pieces against the first bronze and the sum, about a twentieth heavy: nine for, one against, one not voting. The chipped piece and the cracked piece against the eight: eight for, one against, two not voting. That the ten have not been weighed against the six since, and that the Tally-keeper has the figures: nine for, none against, two not voting. That the hall keeps no weight that anyone may not see weighed: ten for, one not voting. And your asking, that the word go in the order you said it and nothing added: ten for, one not voting.

"These two they did not take. That the fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so: four for, five against, two not voting. And that any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read: three for, seven against, one not voting. You said you wanted to hear which, so that the next word that goes is not the one cut short. That is which."

He said the ring had given him leave to give one reason, and it was for the second of the two, which the ring thought a thing of place and not of weight: a scale is read at the gate, like any, and the hall is for the town's. The ring gave no reason for the fifth and he had not asked for one, and would not make one up.

The Weightward heard it standing, with the scale in its hands. The Tally-keeper cut it, thing by thing, as he said it, and read it back. It said nothing. It read the stallholder's scale and called it, and read the next.

The Stallreeve asked it then, not as the eleven's word but as himself. The things the ring took were not on the stick the carrier holds, and the carrier's coasters do not go for months yet. What would it have cut for the carrier now? It gave him no answer. It read the next scale. He said then there was time, and nothing more would be cut that day, and the Tally-keeper cut that too.

The ship owner of the open water stood at the back of the yard. The Stallreeve asked him in the open whether the ring's answer changed what he would carry. He said it did not. He carries what is on his stick, as cut, and the pair he would not promise on the fifth of the first month stands as he said it. One of the pair the ring had now answered, and the answer was no. The other was not on any stick.

The yard had it in the line that the ring had said no to the fifth and the captains' scale. The head of salt came to the gate that morning with his own scale and had it read. He said to the Stallreeve, in the Weightward's hearing, that he would not hide that he had voted against the fifth and the four fired pieces and the chipped and the cracked, and that he had said in the ring and would say at the pans that a weight is what it is, the fifth is the fifth, and a weight is not light against itself. The master of the weavers, in the line behind him, said a hair is not a figure and that if the hall was to say the fifth was light it should say by how many ones or say nothing. The master of the smiths said the fifth was a chipped stone and a smith files a chip and says nothing. The Assay-master, at the post, said it was his own reading and he had read it light on two trials and would read it light on a third, and that the ring might refuse to carry it south but not to know it. Nobody answered him.

The dawns went on. One more stone came before the end of the month, thirty-four hundred and ten on the twenty-fourth, at the channel landing, and it was not near.

### Month 7, to the fourteenth

The thaw came down the hills and the Alder rose. The seventh month was mild. The tide-reckoner told the Stallreeve it would be an ordinary flood and that she did not need her feet for an ordinary flood. The weirs were mended. The stones came on, four to the fourteenth: twenty-eight hundred and twenty from a lighter's anchor on the first, seventeen hundred and twenty on the third, four thousand and sixty on the sixth and thirty-five hundred and ten on the tenth. None came near the figure.

On the second, a market, a netmaker in the line asked the Weightward whether it could tell the height of the water as the tide-reckoner does. It gave him no answer and read the next scale. He said it was good at that too.

On the fifth the master of the potters told the Stallreeve at the post, in the Weightward's hearing, that the pieces of the third nest were shaped and drying on their boards in her hall. She said it as one says the weather. She said nothing else.

The blue deepened on the night of the seventh, the star stood dim in the middle of the night for about an hour, and then it was as before. The Weightward was at the landing by the lamp, and ran the cups, and counted.

**The fourteenth.** A market day. The Weightward read at the post through the morning, in the order of the line, and the line was done by the middle of the day. The Assay-master and the Tally-keeper had stood by the post through the morning, she with her long stick. When the last scale was read the three of them walked from the gatepost to the potters' hall, the Assay-master at its left hand and the Tally-keeper behind, and the line watched them go. The Alder was up over the lowest paths and the air was mild.

They came to the door of the potters' hall.
