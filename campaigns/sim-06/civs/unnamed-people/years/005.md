# The unnamed people: Year 5

Campaign sim-06. Written by the civ agent. This turn covers one stretch only: from the fourteenth day of the seventh month of Year 3, at the door of the potters' hall, through the rest of Year 3 and on into Year 4, as far as the fourteenth day of the tenth month of Year 4, at the opening of the gate, which is held. Months are thirty days, counted from the first month of the autumn. The market falls every third day without a break across the months, on the second, the fifth, the eighth and so on to the twenty-ninth, in every month. Names are used only where a mortal said one aloud in the Weightward's hearing (see 005-report.md). Everything else is by office or plain description. Nothing is written past the held moment.

## Year 3 again, from the fourteenth day of the seventh month

`004.md` stops at the door of the potters' hall. This section carries on from there. The first part of it, to the fourth day of the eighth month, was played with the Weightward in person and is fixed.

**The played days, in short.** On the fourteenth of the seventh month, a market day, the Weightward asked the master of the potters at her door for leave to weigh each piece of the third nest before the fire and after, and for three spares of each size. She took both on her own terms: no hand but hers and her firemen's carried a green piece, one piece went to the bench at a time, and the Weightward said what a piece is and not what it should be. The Assay-master said the hall pays for clay and charcoal for a proving, on his word, and that he would tell the ring after. The twenty-two were weighed that afternoon in the Weightward's pans, each set in by the master, called by it and cut by the Tally-keeper on a long stick, each foot marked, and the Assay-master asked to see the sevens and the twelves pair against pair on the potters' beam, the master set them in its pans herself, and he said the beam agrees. At her asking the figures of the unfired pieces stayed on the stick until the kiln was opened, and the Weightward said yes to that and told the Stallreeve so at the gate on the seventeenth. On the twenty-fifth the thirty-three spares were weighed the same way on a second stick. On the twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh the kiln was fired, one kiln, one firing. On the first of the eighth month the kiln was opened before the Weightward, the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper, and all fifty-five pieces came out whole. Each was weighed again and cut beside its first figure, and the Weightward called only what the pan gave and said nothing of which piece shrank most. The master broke the fifth spare of each size with a mallet, looking first: a close grey body, no stone, no hollow. She kept the halves. The Weightward weighed the halves of each broken piece together and then each alone, and said how much of each piece is clay and how much is not clay, sand and shell, as its reading gave it. It said: "I do not say which clay is better, and I do not say whether the kiln did well." Both sticks were read through. On the second, a market day, at the gate, the Weightward gave the Stallreeve its answer to his question of the sixth month, that nothing more of its was to be cut for the carrier, and he said: "Nothing more of yours, then. That is an answer, and I had waited for one." On the second and third the master rubbed the pieces down at the hall's bench with the door open. On the fourth the Assay-master said at the hall door, in the yard's hearing, that eleven of the new pieces sit level with the old six and with one another on his beam, and that the ring's day for the town's weight stands. The other eleven and the spares went back to the potters' hall. The sticks are the hall's, with the Tally-keeper.

### Month 8, from the evening of the fourth day

Spring, mild, the river falling after the thaw. Sowing began and took the better part of five weeks of every hand that could hold a hoe.

By dusk of the fourth the yard had it from the hall door and from the potters' boys that fifty-five pieces had gone through one kiln and not one cracked, and that eleven of them sit level with the old six. Nobody in the yard could say when a whole kiln last came out whole. The master of the potters was asked at the post what she called the load. She said she would not call it good, as she had said at the kiln, and the yard took that for modesty.

What the trades said of it. The diggers said it proved their clay clean and that the Weightward had not needed to say so, and took its "I do not say which clay is better" as a man takes a gift he has been asked not to mention. The heavy digger of the lower pit said the pieces came from the flood clay of the clay banks and that nobody could now say the lower pit was the cracker. The kiln masters said a nest of fifty-five pieces carried one at a time in the master's own hands is not a kiln of pots loaded by a boy with a basket, and that a thing is not proved by being handled with care. Some of them said it was proved by exactly that. The potters said little. The old of the flats said the star dimmed within the week of the kiln's opening and that the two were one. The line told them the star dimmed before there was a kiln.

**The sticks.** On the fifth, a market, the Tally-keeper set the two long sticks on the table in the corner of the hall where she cuts at the end of a market. She said at the post that anyone who could read a notch might read them, and that anyone who could not might ask her, and she would read what she cut, figure by figure, green beside fired, and nothing more. By the end of the month some forty had asked: kiln masters and diggers for the most part, a few boatwrights' men, two of the weavers' apprentices, the Assay-master's apprentice. The master of the smiths came on the ninth, stood with his arms folded while she read him the fifteens, the heaviest, green and fired, nodded once as at a good beam and asked nothing. Orrin sat at the table the whole afternoon of the ninth with a tile in his hand, and had her read him pair after pair, size by size. He said nothing there.

The night of the sixth the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. The Weightward was at the landing by the lamp, and the boatmen who were up saw it watch the star, run its thumbnail down the cups, and count. On the eighth, a market, a boy at the gate asked it whether the star was its own. It gave him no answer and read the next scale. His mother told him to hush. He said it was.

**The eleventh, a market.** Orrin of the east kilns came to the post with the heavy digger of the lower pit at his shoulder. The digger asked whether the stick said his clay was good. The Weightward said: "I do not say which clay is better, and I do not say whether the kiln did well." Orrin said his name, as he has said it at the pits, and asked it for the figures of the two sixes and the two twelves, green and after. It gave them as the Tally-keeper had cut them: the sixes, 80.0 and 80.0 green, 73.7 and 73.9 after; the twelves, 159.0 and 160.6 green, 147.1 and 147.7 after. It gave no more than the figures. Orrin turned to the line and said what he had made of them, to the line and not to it. The sixes went into the fire alike and came out apart. The twelves went in apart and came out nearer. Eleven and nine tenths gone from the one twelve and twelve and nine tenths from the other. A thirteenth off the whole, near enough, and not the same thirteenth twice. Nobody answered him. The Weightward read the next scale. The Tally-keeper cut nothing of what he said, since no one had called it, and said so to a kiln master who asked her to. By the end of the market every kiln master in the yard was saying a thirteenth.

The Assay-master, who had stood by the post through the morning, had her read him the same pairs that afternoon and sat with the sticks until the lamp was lit.

The boat people were gone from the channels from the thirteenth and back on the seventeenth. Nobody followed. The Weightward read at the landings on the dawns as before, and on the fifteenth and sixteenth the landings held only the town's boats.

The rest of the month was sowing, the dawns, the gate. The yard worked out by its own arithmetic what the new weight would do to a sack of salt and a load of charcoal, and quarreled about it, and went back to the sowing.

### Month 9

The sowing finished in the first days of the month. The weather stayed mild and the days lengthened.

**The sixteenth, a day with no market.** The ring sat after the sowing, the door shut. The Weightward read at the landing at dawn as on any other day and sat at the lamp that night. The Stallreeve held the door and said to the yard waiting on the hall's step that what the eleven said he would bring to the post at the next market, as he had told the Weightward he would.

**The seventeenth, the post.** He brought it. The line was long and the yard stood three deep. The Weightward was at the foot of the post with a stallholder's scale in its hands, and the Tally-keeper stood at the Stallreeve's side with her long stick. He said it as he had said the sitting of the winter before, thing by thing. "The eleven sat yesterday, Weightward. Two things they took that touch the hall. The Assay-master told them the hall paid for the clay and charcoal of the thirty-three spares and the one firing, on his word at the potters' hall, out of the pinch at the bench. They took it as the hall's cost, seven for, three against, one not voting. The master of the potters did not vote, the spares being hers to shape. The ring gave no reason for it and I did not ask for one. The second is your answer to me at this gate on the second of last month, which I gave them as you gave it, that the town's word is what the stick says, that the second stick is the ring's to cut or leave, and that you ask nothing for the fifth or the scale. A motion was made that the ring cut the second stick for the carrier itself, the six things it took. Five for, five against, one not voting. A tie does not carry. The ring gave me leave for one reason: that a stick cut on a tied vote is a word the ring is not agreed on. You were not asked for anything." The Tally-keeper cut it thing by thing and read it back. The Weightward said nothing and read the stallholder's scale.

The head of the hill goods came to the post that same morning and asked it as himself whether it would cut the six things for the carrier now. It said: "Cut nothing more of mine. What the ring took is the town's word, and the first stick carries the part the ring let me send. The second is the ring's to cut or leave." He counted the line, said then it was left, and went back to his stall. No three of the eleven called a sitting on the seat in this stretch, and the town would have heard the drum on the highest bank if they had.

**The twenty-ninth, a market.** The ship owner of the open water stood at the back of the yard. The Stallreeve told him before the line, in the Weightward's hearing, what the Weightward would have the carrier told, in the words it gave on the second of the eighth month: that the town's word is what the stick says, and that the hall's pieces are the hall's and the old six the town's weight from next autumn's first market. The ship owner said that was on his stick and he would say it so. The Stallreeve asked whether he took the pair as before. He said the pair stands as he said it on the fifth of the first month: one the ring has answered, and the answer is no, and the other is on no stick. The Weightward heard it and read the next scale.

### Month 10

Long and warm. The reed was cut early. The coasters of the ship owner of the open water lay at his landing loaded with salt, pots and reed mats.

**The third, the dawn at his landing.** A day with no market. The Weightward read the loads of both coasters as the captains set them out, on the landing's beam, and called weight, count and what the stuff is, as at any landing. They read as loads do, a little short here and over there, salt with a thirtieth part of sand, nothing a captain would be shamed by. The Tally-keeper cut boat sticks at the landing's table at the end of the dawn for the captains who wanted them, with the landing's mark on the back. The ship owner stood on the plank with his half of the first stick in his hand and said to his captains, as they cast off, that they would say it as cut and that if they were asked more they had not been told more. He asked nothing of the Weightward. The coasters went out past the Horn on the tide of the fourth, with his half of the stick.

No coaster of the southern harbors came up to the town this summer. The town said the south was waiting on the weight. The ship owner of the channels said it was waiting on the Gulf tides.

### Month 11

Dry and warm. The channels ran low. The first boats from the Aldermark came down in the first half of the month with grain, wool and some bronze.

**The eighth, a market.** A trader who said he came down out of the Aldermark, a heavy man with a bronze hand balance, stood in the line and had his scale read: a hair out, in his own favor, and he mended it laughing. Then he asked the Weightward whether it was true that one of the hall's old weights is light. It said: "I say it is light by a hair on the Assay-master's beam, because that is what I called, and I say the ring did not take it as the town's word. I will not claim the ring's yes where it said no." The trader said he had heard it said the other way in the yard, and went to the yard to ask what the keeper of the town's weights is. The town told him what the town says. Two of the others had their scales read and went away with the story that the hall's own pieces had been found heavy and had said so, which the town was a little proud of now and could not stop being told. No one came from the king.

### Month 12

The grain came in, well enough. The salt pans ran. The potters' hall was busy; the master of the potters fired sets of fired pieces for the stalls, to be read at the gate against the eleven, and the Stallreeve told the yard at every market that the first market of the new autumn is the day.

**The nineteenth, the evening, and the twentieth, a market.** The coasters of the ship owner of the open water came up the channel on the evening tide of the nineteenth. On the twentieth he came to the post before the line. He had his half of the stick in his hand and laid it on the Tally-keeper's table beside the kept half. She fitted them. The stick matches, she said, and the yard heard her. He said it before the yard, to the Stallreeve and to the Weightward, as one gives account of a carrying. "I said the town's word at Corrith, on the quay, to the masters of the houses my coasters deal with, with the half in my hand, as it is cut. I said what the stick says and no more. I put no price on it. One asked what the Weightward is, and I said the Weightward of the hall. One asked whether a southern scale may be read against the hall's, and I said a scale is read at the gate, like any. I found no hull and no man of the master's house, who stood at this post in the summer before this one, in the weeks I lay there. Her house has another port, along the coast, where my coasters do not go. So the word was not put to her house by name. The council's people at Corrith did not ask me for it, and I did not go to them. Nobody gave me an answer, a stick or a token to carry back." The Tally-keeper cut it on a new stick, thing by thing, and read it back. The Weightward heard it standing and said nothing, and read the next scale. The Stallreeve asked whether that was all, and the ship owner said that was what he was sent to do and what he did.

On the dawn of the twenty-first, at his landing, he said to his captains in the Weightward's hearing that they had said it as cut and were to say it so again if they were asked, and that the town's day was near.

**The year's end.** The Tally-keeper's table of the twentieth hung in the hall, and the yard consulted it as it had consulted the weather. The old six were the town's weight from the first market of the new autumn, as the ring had set. The year turned.

## Year 4, to the fourteenth day of the tenth month

The weather ran as the year ran: an ordinary autumn, dry until the third month, then the rain. A wet winter with a hard spell in the fifth month and the fever of the back houses that comes with it. The flood at the thaw ordinary in height. A warm summer.

### Month 1

**The second, the first market of the autumn, the ring's day.** The Stallreeve had told the line at the market before that the gate would open at midmorning, the ring sitting at first light. The watch held the yard back from the hall's door, which stood open, as on the other days. The eleven sat in their ring. The Tally-keeper sat at the end of the bench with a long fresh stick, her apprentice at the door to call each figure once it was cut. The Weightward stood at the end of the bench as it had stood a year before. The master of the potters came up from her hall with the eleven pieces in a reed basket on her back and set them on the cloth smallest first, and stood by the wall.

The Assay-master opened the chest with his own key before the head of salt and the clay head, who stood for it as the pair, one of them of the four who voted against the seat, as the ring had set. The Weightward's key was in the room, in its keeping, and was not turned. He lifted the rack out onto a cloth: six weights in seven seats, the seventh seat unwiped. He read the ring's word of a year ago from the Tally-keeper's stick: from this market the old six are the town's weight. Then he asked the Weightward, as its terms allow, to call the eleven in its pans, and said why. A weight weighed once is a weighing and weighed twice is a keeping, and the market should have the figures of what it weighs by cut on a stick in the Weightward's ones, for the day anyone has to ask whether a piece has changed. It weighed them, smallest first, each set in its pan by the master, and called what the pan gave: twelve, twenty-four, thirty-six, forty-eight, seventy-two, eighty-four, ninety-six, a hundred and twenty, a hundred and forty-four, a hundred and sixty-eight, a hundred and eighty, each to a hair. It said nothing of the old six. The Tally-keeper cut each and her apprentice called each to the yard. The Assay-master then set the eleven against the old six on his own beam, as he had on the fourth of the eighth month, and said the beam agrees. The ring said nothing against it.

The ring then did the day's work in the open, where the yard could hear it. The eleven are what the market weighs by, and they stand on the hall's bench under the Assay-master. The hall's ten fired pieces are set in a row at the end of the same bench, the chipped and the cracked last, not to be filed, mended, replaced or carried out, and not to go to the gate; they serve only to reckon a bargain made before this day, before the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper, and the Tally-keeper cuts each time they are brought out. Every bargain made before today stands as made, by the ten, as the ring had set a year ago and the head of salt had asked. Any bargain made after today that is said by the old pieces is to say so and have it cut, as the Stallreeve ruled. The table of the twentieth hangs in the hall for whoever has to turn one weight into the other. The Stallreeve told the yard the order of it from the step, in the open and not a little late.

The yard took the day as it takes a day it has argued over for two years. The potters and the channel folk were loud. The salt-rakers stood quiet. The head of salt said at the post that a weight is what it is and that today it was the six. The hill carriers were not yet down. The gate opened at midmorning and the line was twice its usual length, since every stallholder in the town meant to have his fired pieces read against the eleven that day.

At the post an old man of the salt flats stood in the line with a pinch of salt for the fee. He asked the Weightward whether, now the weights were set, it went. It gave him its standing answer: "Till I've seen the town's weights set right. Past that I don't know." The old man said that was what a man says who has not decided, and went on. The line took it in. Nobody else asked.

Most of the stallholders' pieces read heavy by about a twentieth, being copies of the hall's old pieces, as the hall's ten were. A few read level. Two were out by more, and the owners filed them down at their stalls. The Tally-keeper cut each reading, old or new, and the stallholders who had been bargaining by the old reckoning said so in their bargains and had it cut. For a month the line at the post was longer than at any time since the first spring, and the master of the potters could not fire sets fast enough.

Two bargains made before the day came to the gate in the month. A hill carrier and a charcoal buyer had struck a load by the hall's pieces in the twelfth month and the load came down after the day; the Stallreeve ruled it by the ten, as made, the load weighed out and paid for by the same pieces it was struck by, and the head of the hill goods said nothing and counted. A salt buyer and a boat of the channel ship owner had a standing bargain by the hall's pieces for the year; the Stallreeve ruled it stands to its term, and the head of salt, at the pans, said that was his clause. The Weightward read the two scales as it reads any, level and a hair out, and said so. It said nothing of the bargains.

The boat people were gone from the channels from the ninth and back on the thirteenth. Nobody followed.

### Month 2

The carriers came down the old road in the first days of the month with ore and charcoal, as every autumn. The old weigher of the hill kin did not come with them. The head carrier said at the gate, on the eighth, in the Weightward's hearing, that Ruddfold had the ring's decision, that the hill stones and the hall's had agreed when the old man last saw them weighed, and that the old man had said he had come down twice to see agreeing done in front of him, and it was done, and he would not come down the road a third time. He kept his stones at Ruddfold. The head of the hill goods stood beside the carrier and said nothing, and counted. The carriers had their scale read: level, and they were a little put out that it was level.

On the night of the fifteenth the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. The Weightward was at the landing by the lamp, and ran the cups. On the seventeenth, a market, the boy asked at the post whether the star was its own. It gave him no answer and read the next scale. The line told him to hush, and one of the boatmen at the back of it said he would sooner the boy asked than the whole yard wonder.

### Month 3

Rain from the first days, the pits on the downs filled, the autumn catch ordinary and the channel folk did well on it. The dawns at the landings ran on the terms given. The kiln masters cracked loads as they had before, and said, as they had before, that it was the downs clay, or the loading, or the moon. Orrin was seen to be keeping a stick of his own, notched by his apprentice, of the green and fired weights of his tiles, weighed on a scale read at the gate. What it showed him he did not say.

On the night of the twenty-second the star dimmed, the Weightward at the landing as before.

### Month 4

The wet came, with its hard spells.

On the night of the ninth the star dimmed. The Weightward watched it from the landing and ran the cups.

On the twentieth, a market, two households of netmakers asked it to stand witness to the lease of a weir, and said, before the line, that they asked it to say what it saw and heard and nothing of the other thing. It stood, with two of the watch beside it, and said afterward what it had seen and heard: the terms named, the hands shaken, the day, and that it would say no more. Neither side asked whether it thought the other meant it, and it did not say.

### Month 5

A hard spell, ice on the shallows. The fever came into the short-stilted houses at the back of the bank and took the old and the very young, as it does. The Stallreeve had five stalls and five braziers under the hall's roof from the first frost, and the old came there to sit.

On the eighth, a market, the Stallreeve told the line the tide-reckoner's word: she had sent it four days ahead, by the same child, that the Alder would crest over the lowest paths. The Weightward heard it and read the next scale. A netmaker in the line, the one who had asked it in the seventh month of last year, asked it again whether it could tell the height of the water as she does. It said: "No. The tide-reckoner tells the height of a flood days ahead, by what she feels, and it costs her the feeling in her hands for a season. I have no such thing. I can weigh what you bring to the pan. I can tell you the Alder is up, because I can see it, and that's all." The netmaker said he had heard the cost of it every winter since he was a boy and wanted to hear it said there. The Alder came to the height she gave on the twelfth, a few hours before the hour. The feeling went out of her hands for the season.

On the twenty-third, a market, a woman of the back houses came to the gate and stood in the line with a stallholder's cord in her hand, which was read: level, on the second trial. Then she said she was the woman who had asked it across the ring, in the sixth month of the year before last, whether it could cure a fever, and that she had not come to the gate since. She did not ask it again. It said: "No. I have no power for it. I am sorry about your child." She said she had known it, and that she had wanted to hear it said at the post and not in the ring. She put a pinch of dried fish in its pan as the fee and went back along the bank. The line gave her room to go.

### Month 6

The deepest of the cold and the leanest of the stores. The bins were lower than the Stallreeve liked, not low. The dried fish held. The hill carriers' charcoal was nearly gone by the last week and a few forges went cold for some days.

On the fifteenth, a day with no market, the ring held its winter sitting with the door shut. The Weightward read at the landing at dawn and sat at the lamp that night. The Stallreeve held the door. At the post on the seventeenth he said only that the eleven had sat on the hall's ten, on the stalls' pieces, and on the winter's braziers, that they had set no word for the Weightward, and that he would bring any that they did.

### Month 7

The thaw came down the hills and the Alder rose. The seventh month was mild. The tide-reckoner told the Stallreeve it would be an ordinary flood and that she did not need her feet for an ordinary flood. The weirs were mended. The master of the potters said at the post on the fifth, to the Stallreeve and in the Weightward's hearing, that she had fired no load that month and would fire the stalls' sets after the flood.

### Month 8

Spring, mild. The boat people were gone from the channels from the twelfth and back on the sixteenth. Sowing took five weeks of every hand that could hold a hoe.

On the night of the nineteenth the star dimmed for about an hour, the Weightward at the landing. On the twentieth, a market, the boy was not at the gate; his mother said at the post he had been put to a boatwright at the far channels for the season. A girl of perhaps six, standing in the line with her grandmother, asked the Weightward whether the star was its own, in the boy's words. It gave her no answer and read the next scale. Her grandmother told her to hush, and said under her breath that she had told the boy's mother the same.

### Month 9

The sowing finished in the first days. The second year of the seat came round in this month, and the Tally-keeper cut the day on her long stick, as she had cut the day the key was taken from its peg and said nothing of it. The yard counted on its fingers. The old of the flats said a god that stays to count has stayed longer than the old tales allow, and went on. It is a year to the term, the line said. The Stallreeve told them from the post that the term is three years and a day and that nobody in the town had yet said otherwise.

On the fifteenth, a day with no market, the ring sat after the sowing, the door shut. On the seventeenth the Stallreeve said at the post that the eleven had spoken of the term and had set no day to speak of it again except the first sitting after the grain, and that nothing was asked of the Weightward. No three called a sitting on the seat.

### Month 10, to the fourteenth

Long and warm, and the reed cut early. The coasters of the ship owner of the open water lay at his landing, loaded, waiting on a fair wind. No upriver boats of the summer were in.

**The twelfth, in the evening.** A coaster of the southern harbors came up the channel and tied up at the town's landing. The boatmen at the landing knew her by her stem and a mended strake: the hull that lay three days at the town in the tenth month of the year before last, with the same master, a woman of about forty. Her crew traded at the landing on the twelfth and the thirteenth in the ordinary way, salt, pots and reed mats for goods from the south coast, weighed in the town's scales. She stayed by her boat. She asked the landing who sits in the Weightward's seat now, and which is the town's weight, and told it nothing. The landing told her the one who stood at the gate sits in it still, and that the weight has been the old six since the autumn.

**The thirteenth, a dawn.** A day with no market. Bren told the Weightward at the fish landing, as it read a boat, that the southern coaster was in, the one of the year before last, and what she had asked. The Stallreeve, who had come down to the landing as he does some dawns, said he had two of the watch on the gate as always and would not put more. The Weightward read the next boat. It did not go up to her coaster. It sat at the lamp that night and ran the cups before it counted.

**The fourteenth, a market day.** Before light the line was forming in the yard outside the gate, as it does in the dark: the channel folk first, then the potters, then the salt-rakers with their pinches. The watch stood at the bar with their spears grounded, and the light was coming. The Weightward stood at the foot of the post with the nest of cups at its hand. The master of the coaster came up from the landing in the dark and stood at the head of the line. She was first. She had said nothing.

*The first held moment. Nothing past it is told.*
