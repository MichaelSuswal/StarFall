# The unnamed people: Year 3

Campaign sim-06. Written by the civ agent. First Year 2 told again, from the evening of the weighing in its first month to the year's end. Then Year 3, from early autumn round to early autumn. Months are counted from the opening of each year. Names are used only where a mortal said one aloud in the stranger's hearing (see 003-report.md). Everything else is by office or plain description. Where a thread is held for the Keeper it stops at its moment and is written in the report. What three of the town's people hold of the old keeper's loft and of the landing boatmen is the Keeper's sealed matter and is not written in this file.

## Year 2 again, from the evening of the weighing

`002.md` is not edited. Where it and this section differ, this section is what happened. Overtaken in it: that the Assay-master's question went unanswered and he waited; that the ship owner said in the town only that he spoke and it listened and that the Stallreeve had not heard of it; that the stranger did not touch the half stick of the seat; that the yard cried "Weightward" a few days after and had no answer; that the council after the grain said it would wait on the Weigher's answer about the seat. The four lines held in the Keeper's Year 2 section of the state file are answered below.

### Month 1: the evening of the weighing

The eleven rose at dusk, the yard went to its supper, and the Assay-master stayed in the hall with the stranger and shut the door. The lamp was left burning on the step. The watch at the gate, who could see the step from their post, counted the lamp to a long while, and said afterward that they had seen no one go in or out and heard nothing, which in that hall is usual.

When the door opened the Assay-master came out alone onto the step and stood there. Then he sent his apprentice at a run for the Stallreeve, who came up from the gate with a lantern and went in. In the hall, with the Stallreeve standing over him, the Assay-master laid the rack back in the chest and locked it with his own key. The key on the peg in the ring was not touched. The rack went in as it had lain, with the seventh seat left unwiped, and the Assay-master said it was to be left so. The stranger stood by the wall and touched nothing in the chest, and the Stallreeve, who is a man who watches hands, said afterward to nobody in particular that he had watched them.

Then the three went to the hall door. The stranger said to the Stallreeve, with the Assay-master beside it, what it had said to the Assay-master: that it is a god. It said it plainly, in the way it says a weight. It said it asks nothing for it, that if the ring wants it gone from the gate it will hear that and weigh its answer, that it will not run off and will not change its reading for anyone's sake. It told him what it had told the Assay-master and no more. The Stallreeve said: "A year at my gatepost for a pinch of barley. Well." He said the gate would be kept as it was and the watch would stand where it stood, and that he would say nothing in the yard: "A yard that hears that word on a market morning stops buying fish." He said he would carry the Assay-master's asking for a sitting round that night. Of the stranger's asking to say it standing at the foot of the ring with the yard let in, he said that was the ring's door and he would put it to them as it was said. He asked it nothing about what else there was to it.

The stranger also asked him to see that a word went by the next carrier up the old road to the old weigher at Ruddfold, and gave him the words. He said he would have the Tally-keeper cut them on a stick before the carriers came.

Of what passed in the hall with the door shut, the town knew nothing until the sixth month. The Assay-master said nothing false and nothing in the yard. Of what he said back to the stranger and what it said to him, nobody in the town but the Stallreeve had a word, and the Stallreeve had only what he was told at the door.

The Stallreeve went round to the eleven that night until the lamps were out, house by house, and said the same thing at each door: that the Assay-master asked for a sitting where a matter should be said, that the Weigher would say it first if the ring would hear it, that the Weigher asked to say it standing at the foot of the ring with the yard let in, and that he, the Stallreeve, would not name the matter and neither would the Assay-master, since it was the stranger's to say first. He said he knew how that sounded. The eleven took it as eleven take a thing they have not been told. The head of salt said a matter nobody will name in the ring is a matter the ring should name for him. The Stallreeve said it was the stranger's to say first. The head of salt said then the stranger was put above the ring. The Assay-master, sent for by the head of salt to answer it, said the Weigher had asked to stand at the foot of the ring, which is as low as a man can stand in it. The master of the smiths said a stranger's brass is a stranger's brass and he would hear what the brass had to say whenever he was told to. The head of the hill goods said nothing and counted the doors. The clay head said he would sit whenever the ring sat. The head of reed and fish said the Assay-master did not ask for sittings, so the matter was not small. Nobody would call a sitting that week. The ring had sat a whole day, had just set that the weight should come back at the first sitting after the grain, and had the old keeper's loft to look in. The asking went to the ring's winter sitting, which falls in the middle of the sixth month, and the Stallreeve carried that word back to the Assay-master and to the stranger at the gate on the next market morning, and said he would put the stranger's asking to the eleven as it was said.

### Month 1, the weeks after: the pair of them

From the evening of the weighing to the winter sitting the word stayed with two men. Neither told anyone and neither was ever alone with it, because each had the other.

The Assay-master took to coming down to the gate on market mornings, which he had not done before the weighing, and standing a little apart from the line with his arms folded. The first week he looked at its hands. The second he looked at its face, and the line noticed, and he noticed the line noticing and went back to its hands. After that he looked at what it did with a reading that went against someone who could hurt it, because it had told him to watch for that, and it had said it was not asking to be believed. He did not once say the word, not to the Stallreeve, not to his apprentice, not to the empty hall. In the hall, to the Stallreeve, he said "the stranger", and once, a long way into the autumn, "it", and then looked as though he had spilled a basket. At the bench he argued with it at the beam, as he had said he would, and was a little harder on it than before, and a little more careful of being right. He slept badly. His apprentice said he lit the lamp in the hall's small room before light and sat before the empty seat of the rack with the chest locked.

The Stallreeve looked at it as a former boatman looks at weather, in the face, for what it means to do. He found it the same each dawn, which comforted him, and he disliked being comforted by it. He kept the two of the watch at the gatepost, and kept them as he had, to keep the line and not to guard anything. He asked it nothing. He walked the stalls earlier than before. He said, when the head of salt came to him in the second month and asked straight whether the matter of the sitting was a threat to the market, that it was no threat to the market's measure, and let the head of salt hear the word he had chosen, and the head of salt heard it.

Once in each ten days the two of them sat in the hall in the evening with the door open and the lamp lit, as men do who have a thing between them. They said little of it. The Assay-master once said that the stranger's reading of the carriers' scale had been a few parts in a hundred out and that it had said so before the carriers asked. The Stallreeve said it had. That was a whole evening.

The clay head, who had been the loft's witness on the third day and had been told nothing of the other matter, asked the Assay-master in the second week why he had been the one asked. The Assay-master said: "Because you will say what you saw." The clay head said that was a reason that would do for a good many men and let it lie.

On the third day after the weighing three went up the old keeper's loft stair: the Assay-master, the Stallreeve and the clay head as witness, by the ring's agreement. They came down within the hour, quiet. The town saw that and said nothing it could use. What the three said afterward to the town was nothing. What they said to the ring at the winter sitting is in the Keeper's hands.

The town's own talk went round in the days after: that the Assay-master had asked for a sitting for a matter no one would name. By the second week the yard had settled on two stories. One was that the stranger meant to say yes or no to the seat. The other was that the Assay-master had found the seventh. Both were wrong, and the line at the gate said each to the other as a joke and then stopped joking.

### Month 2

The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides and came back. Nobody followed. The star dimmed on a night early in the month, as before.

The hill carriers came down the old road in the middle of the month with ore and charcoal, as every year, and had the old weigher's stick of figures from his own mouth. They had their scale read at the gate and it was a little out in their own favor and they mended it. The stranger's word for the old weigher went home with them on a stick cut by the Tally-keeper: that it would weigh his stones in front of any of the ring who wished to see it, at the gate, any market morning, for no fee but a pinch; that he should bring them in his own bag and it would call them in its own cups; that the old weights and his stones had agreed to the beam's tremble; and that it would like to hear what his people think the seventh was. It asked nothing more. The head carrier said it over twice at the gatepost to fix it, and the head of the hill goods stood by and heard it put, and said he would have the old man's answer by next autumn or not at all.

The Stallreeve went down alone to the landing a few days after the carriers' market and spoke apart with the boatmen below the hall, as he had said he would. The stranger was by the channel folk's lamp and saw him come and saw him go. What the boatmen answered is the Keeper's. Within the week he told the Assay-master in the hall, with the door open, what he had been told, which was what he would have told him sooner if he had had it sooner; the Assay-master said that the two of them would take it to the ring at the winter sitting and not before, and that the clay head should be told, since he had been up the stair. They told him. He said nothing for some time and then asked what the ring would do with it, and the Assay-master said he did not know.

On a day with no market in the last week of the month the stranger went out at first light with its small brass to the pits on the clay banks, and the Stallreeve kept the line and nobody was read. The diggers, the kiln masters and the potters came. The pan said what it said: every lump about a twentieth part sand and shell, the cracked lumps no worse than the sound, and the stranger said it could not tell which clay is good for a kiln and was not going to pretend to. The diggers cheered and the kiln masters did not forgive it. Orrin went home saying that if it was not the grit it was something he could not see in a pan.

### Month 3

Rain, the pits on the downs filled, and Orrin began firing a small tile of each load before he filled his kiln, and the east kilns followed him. The first stale reading stick turned up at the end of the month and the Tally-keeper cut the lapse and showed it at the gate. Some stallholders carved the two pans on their beams and the Stallreeve had them scraped.

In the third week Bren's household brought the stranger the first of a catch at the landing, as the channel folk feed their small gods. It said it took no gift. They did not argue, and took the fish back and ate it at their own table, and said among themselves that this was a small god that would not be fed, which is a thing they say of the oldest of them. They did not say it to the stranger.

### Month 4: the landing

The wet came, with its hard spells. At dusk the ship owner of the channels went down to the landing where the stranger sits by the channel folk's lamp, and put his bargain to it before Bren and two boatmen: that every boat that came to his landing from the south or went down from the north should be read before it was unloaded, at dawn, and what was in it said; that he would pay for the year, in salt, a measure for every boat; that he would have it at his landing and no other; and that he would have all that done in front of the Stallreeve. It said no to the landing and no other, and put its own terms back. It said: "What I won't do: be yours alone." At dawn the captain sets out the load, sack by sack or basket by basket, on the landing's beam or on its own, and it calls the weight, the count and how much is the stuff it is sold as; if he will not show, it says it was not shown; it reads at the landing of any ship owner who asks, on the same terms, on a day when it is not at the gate; his landing has the first dawn and a standing place; the fee is a pinch for a boat, from the captain, weighed, as at the gate, and anything more he can give to the Tally-keeper for her sticks and not to it; and if the two clash, the gate wins. Of the nod the ship owner would want, that his boats are better than the rest, it said it would not give it, and that if they were not he had better hear it from it before the buyer did.

He took the terms before Bren and the two boatmen. He said: "I came down here to buy a year of you, and you have sold me a morning and kept the rest." When the fee came down from a measure a boat to a pinch he said: "A pinch. I offered you a measure a boat and you beat me down to a pinch. Every man on this water will know by morning what I asked and what I got, so I had better be seen to like it." They shook hands on it.

It was set before the Stallreeve the same evening. He took it as he had taken the sticks: he added that the gate comes first, that no reading on a market morning is made anywhere but at the gatepost, and that a landing is its owner's ground, so that its line is the owner's to keep and not the watch's. Of the split sticks for boats that the stranger wished to ask for, he said: "That's her knife and her mornings. Ask her at the gate, the pair of you, and ask her what it does to her season's count."

They asked her at the gate on the next market, the stranger and the ship owner together, at the end of the line. The Tally-keeper heard them out and was a long time answering, and cut nothing while she thought. She said yes, on her own additions, as she had on the first sticks. A boat stick is cut at the landing's own table at the end of the dawn and not at the dawn, so that no boat waits on her stick. A captain who wants none gets none and his load is still called. A boat stick carries the landing's mark on its back, so that no one can take it for a stall stick and no one can hang it. One half goes to the captain and one to the buyer if he wants one, holding for that day only, and the landing's man holds neither. A pinch for the stick is the captain's, and the buyer's if he takes a half, and nothing is the stranger's. To the ship owner, who offered her a measure of salt a month toward her sticks, she said she would take it as the hall's and cut it on her own stick each month where anyone could read it, and not as hers. He said that was more trouble than he had meant to buy. She said it was exactly the trouble.

The Stallreeve asked her afterward what it did to her season's count. She said it cut a notch a boat and she had her apprentice for it and a lamp, and that she would tell him at the end of the season if her count held. It held.

Other owners of boats took the terms as they were offered. The ship owner of the open water, back from the Gulf for the winter, had his landing man ask the stranger at the gate in the fifth month for a dawn when his coasters were in, and was given the same terms and the next dawn after the channel landing's. Two smaller owners of the channels, with two and three boats, asked in the sixth month for a dawn a month. Bren's household asked at the fish landing in the same month and were given theirs, on the same terms, and found it cost them more in standing than in fish. Some captains would not show. The stranger said it was not shown and went on to the next boat, and chased no one. A load unshown sold a tenth lower at the first stall within the month, and by midwinter the captains of unshown loads were a short list and a known one.

What the water says of it, by midwinter: that it reads a hold like a net, counting what is in the mesh; that "I wasn't shown" is a thing to say of anything a man will not set out; that a captain who turns out a basket in front of the landing is a captain who has nothing to turn out against. The ship owner's rivals say it is his man, whatever the terms were, since it sits at his landing first. The ship owner says it is nobody's man and that this is why he sits it first.

### Months 4 to 6: the dawns

Through the winter the loads read as loads do: a little short here, a little over there, salt with a thirtieth part of sand in it, nothing a captain would be shamed by. The Stallreeve went down to the channel landing on several dawns, as a former boatman does, to see how a landing's line looks without the watch in it. He found it kept itself. The Assay-master came down once, early, and stood at the end of the plank table with his arms folded, and went away without saying why.

In the fifth month a hard spell, ice on the shallows. The fever came into the short-stilted houses at the back of the bank and took the old and the very young, in the ordinary way, fewer than last year, because the Stallreeve had kept four stalls and two braziers under the hall's roof from the first frost and the old came there to sit. The tide-reckoner of the flats sent word four days ahead by the same child that the Alder would crest over the lowest paths, and it crested at the height she gave and a few hours off the hour. The Stallreeve told the line the word at the gatepost, and the stranger heard it and read the next scale. The feeling went out of her hands for the season. The star went dim in the middle of a night in the dead of winter for about an hour and was then as before.

### Month 6: the salt

Early in the sixth month a boat of the ship owner's own came in from the flats at dawn with raked salt in baskets. The captain set them out basket by basket at the landing's head. The stranger read the first and called it, before the captain, the ship owner's landing man, and whoever stood on the landing: about five parts salt and one part not salt, sand and grit, under a clean top. It read the second and called it. It read the baskets one after another and called each as it read, and each was the same, and it said nothing of who or how. The landing was quiet but for the calls. Someone ran for the ship owner.

He came down before the baskets were done. He stood at the end of the plank table and heard the last four called. The Stallreeve was there, who had come down as he did most dawns, and the landing man, and a good many of the water. The ship owner said to the stranger: "Read the rest." It did. When it was done he said nothing for a time, and then told the captain to stand where he was. The captain had bought by the basket on the flats and had not turned one out. The ship owner had not known.

What the town found out, because it asked: the baskets came from one raking household on the flats that had a thin season. They had raked the last of the pans and topped each basket with clean salt. The captain had bought on their word and on the top. The ship owner did not know and said so before the water, loud, which a man does who has a name for knowing his boats.

The Stallreeve ruled it, since it was a quarrel between a buyer and a seller and that is the gate's. He ruled it short measure and not theft, since a thin season is a thin season, and said it was a thing the household had known and should not have done. The household made good the sixth in clean salt in two instalments, the first before the next spring tides and the second after, and the Tally-keeper cut it. The captain was docked half of the sixth from his share, and the ship owner bore the rest and said so himself in the open. From then, at the landing, the captains turned out every fifth basket in front of the line, whether anyone asked or not. The head of salt took the household's part and said at the pans that a short measure and a cheat are different things, and that the stranger had said so itself on the first morning it came. He was told this was so, and that it had also said it could not tell which this was. The household did not come to the gate for a month. When they did, they came with a sieve, and had their baskets turned out in front of the line before they were asked, and the flats' salt came to the landing cleaner that winter than it had in years. The salt-rakers said it was because they had been made to look. The head of salt said it was because they were proud.

The Assay-master heard it from the Stallreeve that morning. He said: "It told me to watch what it does when the true reading costs it." He asked what the ship owner had said, and the Stallreeve told him. The Assay-master said that a landing was what the stranger had to lose by it, and that the stranger had read the baskets. He said no more. The ship owner said in the hall a week after, before any of the ring who cared to hear it: "It read my own boat against me in front of the water, and I would sooner it read mine first than the buyer's. I told it so." The water said afterward that he had paid it to read his own boat first for a show. They were wrong about that, and did not stop saying it.

### Month 6: the winter sitting

It fell in the middle of the month, after the salt, on a day with no market, after the dawn reading at the landing. The Stallreeve had put the stranger's asking to the eleven at the gate the market before, as it was said, standing at the foot of the ring with the yard let in, so that nobody should hear it from a neighbor. They argued it for most of an afternoon the day before. The matter was still unnamed. The head of salt was against letting the yard in: a thing said before the yard cannot be unsaid, and a matter nobody will name before the ring should not be named before the market. The master of the smiths said the same, and added that a stranger who asks to speak before the yard is a stranger who means to speak to the yard, not to the ring. The head of the hill goods was for it, on the ground that the hill kin disliked things set in back rooms, and said so to the surprise of the ring. The Assay-master said that whatever it was, if the ring heard it in a shut hall the yard would hear it from a boy at the stalls by dusk, and that the Stallreeve had said more than once that he would sooner they heard it from the ring. The clay head and the master of the potters were for. The head of reed and fish said, dry, that she had never seen the Assay-master ask for the yard to be let in, and would like to see what he wanted it to hear. The ship owner of the channels, who had just had his own boat read against him, said he was for anything that was said in the open on that water. The ring let the yard in, nine to two, the head of salt and the master of the smiths against.

The door stood open and the yard came into the hall as far as it would hold, the rest at the door, with the Stallreeve and the watch holding the line there. The eleven sat in their ring. The Tally-keeper sat at the end of the bench with a long fresh stick. Her apprentice stood at the door and had nothing to call, because the thing was said to the whole hall and it was said once. The stranger stood at the foot of the ring, with the nest of cups at its hand and its small brass in a bag.

The Assay-master spoke first and only to say that he had asked for the sitting, that what the Weigher would say he had heard from it on the evening of the weighing in the hall and had asked it to say it here, and that he had nothing to add to it.

It said it first. It said that it is a god. It said what it had told the Assay-master: that others came down when it did and it has felt none of them; that it came down the old bank and does not know where from or whose child it is; that its scales tell it what is in the pans and what is weighed beside them, that it cannot give a false measure and can keep silent. It said there is more to it than the scales and that it will not list it. It said: "If you ask me a question about a particular power, I will say yes or no, or I will say I will not answer. I will not say no to a yes." It said the terms it had given for the weighing stand: "I weigh, and they decide." Of the seat it said: "I do not ask to be Weightward and I do not refuse it yet." It said it would answer for the seat when the loft had been looked in and the seventh had been looked for. The Assay-master asked it to say what it had said to him of the seventh. It said before the ring that it did not take the seventh, that it never touched the chest, and that it did not know the rack was cut for seven until the Assay-master lifted it out.

The Assay-master said then, standing, the one thing he had held back from the town for half a year: that it was said to him in the hall on the evening of the weighing, and that he asked. He said he would not say he believed it and would not say he did not. He said he had nothing to set against the word and had six calls his beam could not fault and a seventh denied before anyone asked. He said he had held one thing for a year because he did not know what it meant, and that he would not be alone with a second.

What the hall did. It did not make a sound for the length of a long breath, and then a great many people took the breath at once. Nobody cried out. An old salt-raker woman at the front said, not loud, "The Maun Stair." Someone at the back said it again. A child asked its mother whether it was the one that drowned, and the mother said hush, and the Weightward, who was still only the Weigher then, answered the child without being asked by saying again that it did not know where it came from or whose child it was.

The eleven. The head of reed and fish asked plainly, "What else are you?" It said what it had said to the Assay-master: that there is more, and that it answers yes or no about a particular power. She said that was an answer a good broker gives, and sat. The head of salt then asked the question the room had been waiting for someone to ask: whether it can tell when a man is lying. It said it will not say no, because that would be a false word. It said that when a bargain is struck in front of it, and it chooses to read that bargain, it knows of each party one thing, yes or no: whether, at the moment of agreeing, that party meant to keep the terms as spoken. It said it does not know why, or what the lie is, or what the party means to do instead. It said it reads a bargain and never a mind, and knows nothing of a word spoken outside a bargain. It said it has not used it on anyone in the town and will not use it on anyone in the town without their asking. The head of salt said, "Then no bargain in this town is safe from you." It said what it had said, and no more. The head of salt said that he would not ask and that his people would not, and that he would say so at the pans. The hall took that as a speech and, by the next market, as a rule.

The master of the smiths asked it across the ring whether it could be killed, and what it would take to break it. This is a question about a particular power, and it is written in the report for the Keeper. In this file the thread stops at the question. The master of the smiths said afterward only that he had asked a thing a smith asks of any metal, and that the ring's right to put it down was an edge he would hold to. A woman of the back houses, whose child had been in the fever of the fifth month, stood up from the middle of the yard and asked whether it could cure a fever. That too is written in the report for the Keeper, and the thread stops there. What the back houses did after is written below.

The master of the potters said, when the ring had gone round, that she had fired a nest that did not agree with itself and it had weighed her in front of the ring and she was not hurt, and that if that was a god it was a gentle one, and that she would bring the second nest to the gate and have it read like anyone. The master of the boatwrights asked whether its readings at the landing would stand as they were. It said they would. The master of the weavers said he weighs wool, and knows what a man is when he weighs him, and had not weighed this one, and would not say more until he had. The ship owner of the open water asked for his dawn, and was told he had it by the terms already given. The head of the hill goods said that the hill kin, who dislike being measured by anyone not their own, had been measured by their betters at last, and that he would send word up the old road with the first walker after the thaw. The ship owner of the channels said he had bargained with a god over a pinch of salt and did not like the bargain the less. The clay head said it had told him what it could not do, which he had not met before in a god.

The ring then sent the yard out. The Stallreeve cleared the hall and held the door. The stranger went out to the landing, where the dawn's work was waiting to be cut, and the Tally-keeper stayed with the ring. In the shut hall the three who had gone up the loft told the ring what they had seen there, and the Stallreeve told it what the boatmen had said, and the head of reed and fish asked the Tally-keeper plainly, now that the stranger had said before the ring that it did not take the seventh, whether she held anything of the seventh weight on any stick of the old keeper's last years. She answered plainly. What the three said, and what she answered, are the Keeper's and are not written here.

What the ring did on hearing it, in the shut hall. The head of salt moved that the stranger be asked to stand down from the gate until the ring had sat on what it was. The master of the smiths seconded. Nine were against, the two for. The Stallreeve said the gate was kept as it was and he would go on keeping it. The motion did not reach the stranger. The clay head moved that the seventh be looked for, in every way open to the town, since it had said it would answer for the seat when the loft had been looked in and the seventh looked for. It carried, ten to none, the head of salt not voting. The ring set the looking as follows: the rooms of the hall, three days, by the three who had gone up the stair with the Tally-keeper; the old man's house and whatever kin he had, by the Stallreeve and the Assay-master with the head of reed and fish; the boatmen of every landing asked who else lent a boat on the night; and the channel dragged at the lowest water of summer if the three found cause. Whether any of it finds the seventh is the Keeper's. The ring set its sitting on the seat for after the sowing, when the looking had had its season. It decided that nothing in the hall be filed, mended, replaced or carried out, as before. It decided nothing of the sealed jar of the old keeper's halves, which nobody raised.

What the yard did. It went to its supper in silence, and the next market morning the line at the gate was twice as long and nobody bought fish until noon, and then everybody did. The Stallreeve put eight of the watch at the post that morning instead of two, and sent six of them home by midday, and said that was the price of a quiet line. Bren said loud to the yard, as she says things, that she had fed it first and would feed it again, and that a god that eats her bream is no worse than a man that does. She did not feed it the first of a catch. She fed it at her table, as she had. The back houses did what the back houses do. The old of the flats took bread down to the Maun Stair at the next low tide, and left it, and came back. The salt-rakers said it had known about the seventh because it had asked who held the key, and did not say it in the yard. The potters said it was a gentle one. The hill carriers were not in town and heard of it in the autumn.

The woman who asked about the fever went home. Her child died in the last week of the month, in the ordinary way, with the cold. She said nothing at the gate. The back houses said what the back houses say, that a god of scales is a god of scales, and that you do not bring a sick child to a pan. They did not stop reading their scales at the gate. The Stallreeve moved two more braziers under the hall's roof the next winter and did not say why.

In the last week of the month two households of boatwrights asked the stranger to stand witness to the sale of a boat. They said, before the line, that they asked it to stand witness to the sale and to say what it saw and heard, and that they asked nothing of the other thing. It stood, with two of the watch beside it, and said afterward what it saw and heard: the price named, the hands shaken, the day, and that it would say no more. Neither side asked whether it thought the other meant it, and it did not say.

### Month 7

The flood season. The rains eased, the Alder rose, the weirs were mended. The tide-reckoner was asked by the Stallreeve to tell the height. She sent back that it would be an ordinary flood and she did not need her feet for an ordinary flood. A reed island split and two households quarreled over it. They asked the stranger in the yard who owned it. It said owning is not a weight, and that it would weigh what each household said was cut or drawn from it if they brought it. They took it to the council. The master of the potters fired the nest again, as the ring had said: twenty-two pieces in one kiln, eleven chosen by the gate's scale. It came out agreeing with itself to a hair. It is kept at the potters' hall. The first nest stays in the hall under the Assay-master.

In the same month the looking began. The three, with the Tally-keeper, went through the rooms of the hall over three days, and the Stallreeve and the Assay-master went to the old man's house, with the head of reed and fish for the sake of whoever they found there. The Stallreeve asked the boatmen of the landings what the Keeper's note has him ask, and a little more. What was found is the Keeper's, and the thread stops there.

### Month 8

The spring flood came, ordinary in height. Sowing took five weeks of every hand that could hold a hoe. The boat people were gone for some days at the lowest tides of spring and came back. The star dimmed on a night in spring for about an hour. The old said again that it was the drowned god, the young that it came with the stranger.

### Month 9: the gatepost

The council sat after the sowing. The clay head and the master of the potters had brought the seat forward and the town knew it was coming. The ring had sat on what the stranger had said in the sixth month. It voted knowing it. Six were for, four against, one away on the Gulf: the open-water ship owner. The Assay-master spoke for it, on his own terms, as before: an office with a term and the ring's hand on it and his own beside the stranger's on the beam. He added what he had not said in Year 2: that it had said it was a god before it was asked to take the seat, and had said it before the ring and before the yard, which a thing selling something does not do first, and that it had read the ship owner's own boat against him when the true reading cost it a standing place, and that it had said the ring could put it down at any sitting three called. He said a keeper the ring cannot put down is a lord, and that it had said that before the ring said it. The clay head said it had put out bread for it on the day of the weighing and been told it took no fee and no gift, and had put the bread away; that it had told the kiln masters it could not say which clay was good; that this was a thing he had not met before in a god. The master of the potters said it had weighed her nest and said so in front of everyone and she was not the worse for it. The head of reed and fish said her people had said for years the bench ran heavy against the fish and it had taken a stranger to say it aloud, and she did not mind who said it so long as someone counted. The master of the boatwrights said the landing readings had shortened his men's quarrels, and he was for. The ship owner of the channels said he had a stake and a grudge and a bargain, and that the grudge was against himself.

The four who spoke against did so for their own reasons, and said them plainly. The master of the weavers had not weighed this one and did not mean to give it a key until he had. He said a seat for a thing that does not sleep is a throne, whatever the term, and that the ring's right to put it down is a paper against a thing that does not sleep. The head of salt said what he had said before, that a store of worth is made by agreeing, and that a thing that can read a bargain should not keep the standards of the thing it can read. The master of the smiths said he still did not know what it would take to break it, and would not seat a thing he could not break. The head of the hill goods said the hall had waited forty years for a weight and had got a keeper, and that he would have had the weight first; and that a term of three years and a day ends in a way the ring would find hard to enforce on a thing it could not move.

The Stallreeve carried the ring's word to the gatepost on the next market day, in front of the yard, in the words the old file has. "The eleven ask the Weigher to take the Weightward's seat and keep the hall's standards, for a term of three years and a day, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam beside yours, and with the eleven's right to put the seat down at any sitting that three of them call." He laid the other half of the stick at the foot of the gatepost. The Tally-keeper stood by him with the cut stick; the Assay-master stood a little apart with his arms folded. The stranger was holding a scale. It read the scale first, a stallholder's, and called it: heavy on the buying side by about one part in thirty. It said so to the stallholder. Then it laid the scale down and took up the half stick. It said, first, that if anyone in the ring had not heard it from its own mouth, it said it now: it is a god. It said yes, before the yard, on its terms, spoken. It took the seat for three years and a day, as the eleven set it, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam beside its own, and with the ring's right to put it down at any sitting three of them call, and it did not ask that to be softened: "A keeper the ring cannot put down is not a keeper, it is a lord." It keeps the standards and does not set the town's weight: it calls what each piece is now and the eleven say which is the town's. It would not wait for the seventh to be found, because a hall without a keeper while it is looked for is a hall that keeps nothing. The rack stays as it is with the seventh seat unwiped, and if it turns up it turns up in front of the ring. The chest is opened only before two of the eleven and never by it alone. It takes the old keeper's key off its peg in front of them, and when its term ends the key goes back on the peg. It takes no fee for the seat. The hall's pinch at the bench is the hall's. The gate stays as it is, market mornings at the foot of the post, and the Stallreeve's rules on sticks and tokens stand. The Assay-master tests, as he has always done, and it checks what he has passed against its scales when he asks, not when it likes. And to the yard, last: "I am the Weightward, for a term. I am not their god, and nothing here is mine."

The Tally-keeper fitted her half to its half and said the terms back in its words. The Stallreeve said, loud enough for the line: "The eleven asked, and the Weigher has said yes on the eleven's terms. The rest it has said in front of you, and I'll carry it up as it was said. The key is taken in the ring, before the ring, and not before." The Assay-master said: "Expect to be argued with at the beam."

What the yard did when it had said it. The yard already knew, since the sixth month, what it was. It had not heard it say yes. A boy cried "Weightward" first, as he had a year before, and this time the whole line took it up, the channel folk and the potters first and loudest, and went on until the Stallreeve put up his hand. The line at the foot of the post did not lose its order. The salt-rakers stood silent. The hill carriers were not in town. The old of the flats said afterward that they had a saying, that a god has never stayed to count what it was owed, and here was one that had stayed and said that nothing in the town was its own, which was the more disquieting for being honest; they said it had not come to take but to count, and that what it counted would be paid. They were wrong, since it took nothing, but they said it for the rest of the year. Bren's household, who had fed it first, said they would go on feeding it at their table when it was hungry, as they fed anyone at the landing, and not as a small god, since it had said it was not theirs. The line that day was the longest of the year, and it read until dusk.

How the eleven took what it had added: they took it well. The terms it added were fewer and harder on itself than anyone had asked: that the rack stay unwiped, that it would not wait on the seventh, that it kept no fee, that the chest never opened by it alone. The master of the weavers said the unwiped seat was the thing he liked and would not say so again. The head of salt said the hall's pinch being the hall's saved the ring a quarrel it would have had. The master of the smiths said the key off the peg was the thing he would watch. Nobody called a sitting to put it down, and the three who had voted against it with the weavers' master counted the room and found no cause.

### Month 10: the key, the coaster, and the three questions

On a day with no market in the first days of the month the ring sat, and the key came off the peg. The eleven were in their ring, the Stallreeve at the door, the Tally-keeper at the bench's end with a fresh stick. The stranger stood at the foot of the ring and took the old keeper's key off its peg in front of them, and the Tally-keeper cut the day and that it was taken. It did not say what it did with the key after, and the town does not know where it keeps it. The Assay-master said the key was not the chest. The ring settled how the chest is to be opened before two of the eleven: any two who care to come, and one of any two to be one of the four who voted against, so that the six should not open it alone. The head of the hill goods and the master of the potters stood for the first time at the first opening, and nothing was opened at that sitting.

A coaster of the southern harbor towns came up the main channel to the town's own landing a few days later, as happens some summers and did not happen last year. Her master is a woman of about forty. She traded some days, took salt, pots and reed mats and paid in goods from the south coast, asked the news of the town. A man of her crew stood at the gatepost one market morning watching the readings and went away without a scale. On the next market day she joined the line before light and stood in it as it moved. At the head of the line she stood before the Weightward.

What the line saw and heard. The Weightward took her balance from her hand like anyone's, said nothing first, and read it against its brass pan by pan. It read level and said so. It took a pinch of barley. Then it asked her three things, quietly, so that the yard had to lean to hear, and told her why: it is keeper of the hall's standards for a term, and would know whether what the hall keeps can be read by anyone who comes up the channel. "What do the southern harbors read their scales against, Master?" She said: "Each house against its own. A house's weights are the house's." "Is there a measure in Corrith that the other harbor towns will take, or does each house keep its own?" She said: "The council in Corrith keeps a set for quarrels, and a house takes it when it must. Nobody swears it is what it was." "And what would a southern captain want to know about this town's weights before she signed to buy in them?" She said: "The thing your yard cannot tell me. Which is the town's weight: the old stones or the clay. Tell me that and I will tell every house on the coast, and that is a fair trade." It gave her no answer to that. She waited, and then nodded as at a price refused.

She asked who it works for. It said it is the Weightward of the hall for three years and a day, and the rest as it had said it. She said: "They say at the landing that you stood at this post and said you were a god. Did you?" It told her yes: it is a god. She looked at it a while and said: "Where I come from we say a god ashore is a landlord. You take barley." She said no more of it. She asked whether it would read a cargo at a quay of her coast if a house paid its passage. It said it does not leave the gate, and that her harbor may bring its scales here or send its own to be set against the hall's. She asked what the town means to do about its weights. It said that is the town's, and she is a guest at the gate. She offered no gift. She thanked it for the reading, took her balance and went down to the landing. She gave no name and was not asked for one. Her coaster stayed three more days and went down the channel.

What the town made of it. The yard took "a god ashore is a landlord" at once, and it was a saying by the next market and a joke by the one after. The line was short of breath the whole morning, because it had not known that the south keeps no common weight, and said at first that this was a mercy: that a coast where each house keeps its own would not be shocked at a twentieth. The head of the hill goods, who had been at the post, said it was a fair among thieves, and that the coaster's last question was the one the town could not answer. The Assay-master heard her last question from the line and went home and said nothing for the evening. The Stallreeve said it was a question every man in the yard had been asking himself for a year and a woman who sold salt had had the gall to ask it of the stranger.

In the same days the stranger asked the Stallreeve, at dusk at the gatepost, with the Assay-master standing by, once, quietly: whether the town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast with this captain, on what the hall's weights are and are not, in the plain terms it gave the eleven; and said it did not send it without their say. The coaster was at the landing with three days left. The Stallreeve said it was not his to say and that the ring had not said which was the town's weight; that a word sent down the coast before the ring had said was the town telling the coast it had none. He said: "Not this summer. Ask us after the grain sitting." The Assay-master said that the Weightward's terms were the ring's own, that it weighs and the ring decides, and that the answer to a southern captain who asks which is the town's weight is whatever the ring decides, and not before. No word went down with the coaster.

The stranger asked the Assay-master, once, quietly, in the hall's doorway at dusk after the coaster had gone, what the three of them had seen in the old keeper's loft. He told it. What he said, exactly, is in the report. He said no more than he had seen. He said the ring had had it since the winter.

### Month 11

Dry and warm. The channels ran low. The first boats from the Aldermark came down in the summer to trade, with grain, wool and some bronze, and two of the traders had their scales read and went away with the story that the town's own hall had been found heavy and had since said so, which the town was a little proud of now and could not stop being told. No one came from the king. On a no-market day in the second week the stranger said once to the diggers that it could come to the pits one more day if they wanted it, and that it still could not say which clay is good. It left it to them. The diggers wanted it, as they had been told it proved their clay clean. The kiln masters did not forbid it. Orrin came with lumps of both kinds and a tile fired from each, and the day was set as the last time. The pan said what it had said: every lump much alike, about a twentieth part sand and shell, the cracked no worse than the sound. It said so. The diggers cheered a little less than the first time. Orrin looked at the tiles and the figures together for some time, and said that the tiles had not told him anything either, and that he would go on firing them for the habit. The heavy digger sold at the lower price again. The quarrel kept.

In the last ten days of the month, at the lowest water, the ring's drag went down: the Stallreeve with twelve of the watch and the boatmen of two households, with drag hooks, weighted nets and long poles, in the reach below the hall's landing and the reaches either side of it. The ring had said that anything heavy and of stone that came up was to be carried to the hall's beam and put before two of the eleven and the Weightward, who had said it would tell what a piece weighed if it were brought. The thread stops at the drag. What it brought up is the Keeper's.

### Month 12

The grain came in, well enough. The salt pans ran. The blue star dimmed late in the summer, as it had every summer, on a night with no cloud. The council did not sit in this month on the weight, as the old file has it. It waited for its sitting at the first market after the grain, which falls on the first market of the new autumn. The year turned.

## Year 3

### Autumn, months 1 to 3

**Month 1.** The grain was in, the pans had run, and the first market of the new autumn was the day the ring had set a year before. The Stallreeve held the yard back from the hall's door with the watch and the door stood open so the yard could hear. The Tally-keeper sat at the end of the bench with a long fresh stick, her apprentice at the door to call each figure once it was cut. The eleven sat in their ring, the ship owner of the open water in the room this time. The Weightward stood at the end of the bench as it had stood a year before.

The weight came back to the ring as the ring had set. The Weightward offered, before the ring, to weigh the six again. The head of the hill goods said he would have it weighed, since the hill kin would ask whether it had read the same in a year. The head of salt said a stick cut once cannot be recut, and the Tally-keeper had cut it, and that was enough. The Assay-master said a weight weighed once is a weighing and weighed twice is a keeping, and that the Weightward's first duty was a keeping. The ring took the offer, seven to two, with two not voting. The pair of the eleven to stand for the chest were the head of the hill goods and the master of the potters, one of whom had voted against the seat, as the ring had set. The Assay-master opened the chest with his own key. The Weightward's key was in the room, in its keeping, and was not turned. The rack came out onto a cloth, six weights in seven seats, the seventh seat unwiped, and the ring looked at it again and said nothing for some time.

The three bronze pieces went into its pans and it called them in its own ones: forty-eight, ninety-six, a hundred and ninety-two. The three stone pieces went on the hall's beam with the Assay-master's hand on it and the Weightward standing over it: three hundred and eighty-four, seven hundred and sixty-six, fifteen hundred and thirty-six. The Assay-master checked each on his beam beside its calls. Each balanced two of the one below. At the fifth he read it light by a hair on the first trial, and said he had learned that one, and the Tally-keeper cut it. They read as they had read.

The ring then argued the day's work, which was the weight. Traders of the Aldermark had taken the shame of the heavy pieces upriver. The southern captain had asked which was the town's weight and been given no answer, and the whole town knew it. The second nest agreed with itself to a hair and with the hall's present pieces, which are heavy. The old six were the old six. The head of the hill goods moved that the old six be the town's weight and the hall's pieces be made again to them. The head of salt moved that the hall's pieces stand. The clay head, who had moved to wait, said that the third motion of a year ago had been that the nest be fired again and the seventh be found or given up, and that the nest had been fired and the seventh had been looked for in every way the town could think of, and he would not move to wait again, since the ring had been seen to wait by the whole south coast. The ring voted for the old six as the town's weight, six for, two against (salt and smiths), three not voting (the boatwrights, the weavers, the open-water ship owner). It carried the clause that the head of salt had asked for the year before and that every debt and bargain made before the change be reckoned by the hall's present pieces as made. It carried a year's notice: the old six are the town's weight from the first market of the autumn that comes round next year, and until then the market stands on the present pieces. The master of the potters said she would fire a new nest to the old six in the spring and have it read at the gate, and the ring asked her to. The Tally-keeper was to cut a table of the twentieth between the two, and the Stallreeve ruled that any bargain after the change that said it was by the old pieces should say so and have it cut. The seventh seat stays as it is. The six are the weight and the seat is held; if the seventh comes it comes before the ring.

The ring then took up what the Weightward had asked of the Stallreeve at the gatepost a year ago, whether the town wanted a keeper's word sent down the coast, and said yes, in the plain terms it had given the eleven, and said the ship owner of the open water would carry it when his coasters went out past the Horn. The Stallreeve carried the ring's word to the gatepost on the next market morning. That moment is held and is in the report.

The star deepened on some nights early in the month and stood dim in the middle of the night for about an hour, and then it was as before.

The ring spoke of one more thing and the town heard of it: the three who had voted against the seat with the weavers' master had counted the room, and had found no cause. The master of the weavers said he would call it when it first did a thing that was not a weight. The head of salt said that if that day came he would not need three. The matter did not come to a sitting.

**Month 2.** The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides of autumn and came back. Nobody followed.

The hill carriers came down the old road in the middle of the month with ore and charcoal, as every year, and the old weigher of the hill kin came down with them, a year older, in a carrier's cart, with his bag of six stones on his knees. He lodged in the head's house and said nothing in the yard for two market mornings. He had had the word by the carriers a year before and had sent back by the head's walker in the spring that he would come.

On the third market morning he stood at the foot of the gatepost with the head of the hill goods beside him, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve, who had said any of the ring who wished to see it might. The Weightward weighed his stones in its own cups: forty-eight, ninety-six, a hundred and ninety-three, three hundred and eighty-four, seven hundred and sixty-eight, fifteen hundred and thirty-six. The three smaller went into its pans. The three larger are too heavy for its pans and wanted a beam brought to the gate. The Assay-master sent his apprentice and two of the watch for his own beam from his house, saying the hall's beam was the hall's and did not leave it, and his own did. It was set on trestles at the foot of the gatepost. The master of the smiths asked to look at it and the Assay-master said it was not the hall's and he might. He lifted it, tried the pivot and the pans, and said it was a good beam, and said no more about it. The Weightward stood over it with its small brass in its hand and called the three large stones, and the Assay-master's hand was on the beam. The Tally-keeper cut every figure and her apprentice called them.

The old weigher said, slowly and in the voice of the hill kin, that the hall's old six and Ruddfold's stones had been the same weights as far as anyone knew; that he had not known whether the hall's were cut from the hill kin's or the hill kin's from the hall's, and nor had his grandfather; that agreeing to a hair was the only thing he had come down to see done in front of him. When the Weightward asked him, as its word had asked, what the hill kin say the seventh was, he said it was a story and that he had not seen it. He said that in Ruddfold they tell it to boys that the six weigh what is, and the seventh weighs what is owed, and that the hall dared not weigh with it. He said that was a thing for boys. He said it had been his grandfather's saying and he would not stand behind it. He asked nothing of the Weightward, and went home with the carriers after the carriers' market with the figures on a stick of his own. The head of the hill goods said the stone had weighed true and that the hill kin would hear it said.

The Assay-master did not like the hill kin's saying and said so to the Stallreeve in the hall that evening. The Stallreeve said it was a hill story. The Assay-master said the old man had been his friend of thirty years, that he had stood at the laying-out, and that he would not stand at a second. Nobody had asked him to. The ring did not move to look anywhere the saying pointed.

**Month 3.** Rain came in. The pits on the downs filled. The autumn catch was ordinary, and the channel folk did well on it. The Tally-keeper cut the first stale sticks of the year, and the notch rule held. The potters' master took the clay for the new nest to the east kilns and began sorting pieces by the gate's scale. The dawns at the landings ran on the terms spoken: a little short here and a little over there, salt with a thirtieth part of sand, captains who turned out their fifth basket without being asked.

### Winter, months 4 to 6

**Month 4.** The wet came, with its hard spells. The Assay-master asked the Weightward, as its terms allow, to check six scales he had passed in the autumn. Two read level and four a hair out, as scales do. He mended the four himself and cut nothing against his own name. He said it was the first thing it had checked for him that he would have found himself in a month, and that he preferred to have found it in a day.

The two households of boatwrights who had asked it to stand witness a year ago asked again for a lease of a boatyard, with the same words as before: that they asked it to stand witness and say what it saw and heard, and asked nothing of the other thing. It stood. The yard did not stop noticing that they said it.

The yard had decided about bargaining at the gatepost. Some stallholders moved their haggling a few paces from the post. Others moved it closer and said afterward that the sale was struck at the post, in front of the Weightward, and that it had read nothing, as an honor. The Stallreeve laughed at both. He said a young stallholder asked him whether he might ask the Weightward to read a bargain of his, only to be called honest. He told him the gate was for scales, and the hall was for the rest. Nobody asked the Weightward to read a bargain that year.

**Month 5.** A hard spell, ice on the shallows. The fever came into the short-stilted houses at the back of the bank and took the old and the very young, in the ordinary way, fewer than the year before, because the Stallreeve had kept four stalls and four braziers under the hall's roof from the first frost and the old came there to sit. The tide-reckoner called the winter water four days ahead, by the same child, right in height and a few hours off the hour. The Stallreeve told the line at the gatepost, and the Weightward heard it and read the next scale. The feeling went out of her hands for the season. The star went dim in the middle of the night on nights in the dead of winter, for about an hour each time, and then was as before, and the watch and the boatmen saw it, and anyone awake under a clear sky.

**Month 6.** The deepest of the cold and the leanest of the stores. The bins were lower than the Stallreeve liked, not low. The dried fish held. The hill carriers' charcoal was nearly gone by the last week and a few forges went cold for some days. The back houses brought no sick to the gate this winter. They said the braziers had done it, and the Stallreeve said so too.

### Spring, months 7 to 9

**Month 7.** The flood season. The rains eased, the Alder rose, the weirs were mended. The tide-reckoner told the Stallreeve it would be an ordinary flood and she did not need her feet for an ordinary flood. The master of the potters fired the new nest, twenty-two pieces in one kiln to choose eleven by the gate's scale, as the ring had asked, to the old six. Whether it agrees with itself and with the old first is not settled in this file; see the report, where it is written as an attempt. She brought each piece to the line like anyone and had it read and cut.

**Month 8.** The spring flood came, ordinary in height. Sowing took five weeks of every hand that could hold a hoe. The boat people were gone for some days at the lowest tides of spring and came back. The star dimmed on nights in spring, for about an hour each time. The old said it was the drowned god, and the young that it came with the Weightward, and the line said nothing, because by then the Weightward had said it watched the light and meant to learn its habits, and the line was used to it.

**Month 9.** The council sat after the sowing, a year to the market day since the gatepost. It sat on the nest: the master of the potters brought the eleven to the hall, the Assay-master set them against the old six, and the Weightward called them in its pans. What the nest was is the Keeper's. The ring did not sit on the seat. Nobody called a sitting to put it down.

### Summer, months 10 to 12

**Month 10.** Long and warm. The reed was cut early. The ship owner of the open water's two coasters went out past the Horn in the first days of the month, as every summer, with salt, pots and reed mats. What they carried in the way of a keeper's word is the Keeper's, since the word the ring asked for is held. No coaster of the southern harbor towns came up to the town's landing this summer. The town said the south was waiting on the weight, and the ship owner from the channels said the south was waiting on the Gulf tides, and he is nearer the mark.

**Month 11.** Dry and warm. The channels ran low. The boats from the Aldermark came down in the first half of the month to trade, with grain, wool and some bronze. Two of the traders had their scales read, and went away with the story. The traders had heard at home that the market on the levee had seated a keeper of its weights, and asked at the landing and in the yard what it was; the town told them what the town says, and the Stallreeve told them nothing. What they carried back is the Keeper's. No one came from the king. The Stallreeve said that a reeve of that king might come yet, to ask why a town needed a keeper of weights, and that he would say what he had always said: that the market is older than any lord.

**Month 12.** The grain came in, well enough. The salt pans ran. The council did not sit. The first market of the new autumn is the day the old six become the town's weight, if the new nest holds, and the town knew it, and the Tally-keeper had cut her table of the twentieth and hung it in the hall, and the potters' hall was busy. The year turned.

## Why the numbers moved

See state.md. In short: two ordinary years, each with a wet winter. The fever was lighter in each, because the Stallreeve had more braziers under the hall's roof. The market was louder than a year ago for the god at the gate and no worse for it. The old quarrel of the weight was settled on paper and not yet on the stalls. The treasure moved little. The watch did not grow.
