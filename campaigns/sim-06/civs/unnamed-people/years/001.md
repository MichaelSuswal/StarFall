# The unnamed people: Year 1

Campaign sim-06. Written by the civ agent. The year opens in early autumn and runs round to early autumn again; months are counted from the opening. Names are used only where a mortal said one aloud (see report-001.md). Everything else is by office or plain description.

## The first day, in brief

The stranger came into the town on a market day and read the hall's own pieces true at the hall door, in front of the yard. The Assay-master ruled the salt short on the strength of it and said aloud that the bench had been wrong in its ten. The salt-raker made good the short seventh that day and was not accused. The stranger then read scales at the gatepost until dusk, went up to the hall at the Assay-master's asking, and the door was shut. What was said there is in report-001.md.

## Autumn, months 1 to 3

**Month 1.** The yard filled on the second morning. The line began before light, the channel folk first, because Bren's people had told the landing. The stranger stood at the foot of the gatepost with its small brass pair and the nest of seven cups, read each scale in the order it stood, took the fee in grain, fish or a fired weight, and refused scrap silver again when a ship-owner's runner offered it for a place at the head. The runner stood in the line all day and was read at dusk, the last. The yard laughed about it for a week, and the laugh did the stranger more good than anything it said.

The Stallreeve came down on the third day, watched until noon, and put two of the watch at the gatepost to keep the line, not to guard the stranger, which was the Stallreeve's own phrase and a careful one. The Stallreeve took nothing from the yard and asked nothing of it that day.

The Assay-master did not carry the offer to the eleven at once. He told the Stallreeve on the second day, in the hall, with the door open. He brought it to the council on the ninth day, as his own, at the first sitting of the autumn. Eight of the eleven heard it with the face of people who have been asked to taste something. The clay head and the master of the potters wanted it that night. The master of the smiths said a stranger's brass is a stranger's brass. The head of the hill goods said nothing and counted the room. The council decided nothing, as it does, except that it would see how the stranger kept. The Assay-master had expected that and went home content.

The blue star deepened on some nights early in the month and stood dim in the middle of them for about an hour. Anyone awake saw it. The town had seen the thinning before, in other years, and said so. This year more of them said it aloud to each other in the line.

**Month 2.** The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides and came back. This had happened before. This year a good many people noticed it, because they were standing in a line at the gatepost with nothing to do but notice.

The hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way in the middle of the month, with ore and charcoal, as every year. Their head carrier went straight to the market and was met by a charcoal buyer who wanted the carriers' own scale read before he would pay. The carriers took it as an insult and nearly came to blows at the stalls. The Stallreeve took both scales to the gatepost himself, where the stranger read them in the order the Stallreeve set. The carriers' scale was true to a hair. The buyer's scale was out by about a tenth in the buyer's favor. The buyer paid the difference that day, in the open, before the carriers had finished being angry. It cost him a month of looks and cost the carriers nothing. The carriers went back up the road with a good opinion of the gate, and they took the story with them. From then the Stallreeve brought every dispute of weight between buyer and seller to the gatepost for a reading and ruled on it himself at the stall. He did not ask the stranger. The stranger did not object.

**Month 3.** Rain came in. The clay-diggers' pits filled on the downs. The potters, who have always quarreled with the diggers over grit, found a use for the true scale. A kiln-master of the east kilns, the first to do it, carried a fixed reed basket of each digger's clay to the gate and had the basket read. Most of the loads read alike. One digger's loads ran heavy by about one part in nine. By the end of the month five of the seven kiln-masters were weighing a fixed basket of every load before they would take it, on scales that had been read at the gate. The kiln-masters believe a heavy load is a gritty one. The diggers say it is the lower pit, finer and denser. The quarrel went to the council, where the head of the salt trade led the hearing, as the custom is, because salt is not a party to clay. The council settled it by a price in measure and not by an answer about the clay: the heavy digger sold his loads at a lower price, to any kiln-master who would still take them, and said he would sell to the east kilns no more. It is not settled which of them is right.

Some stallholders began hanging a small fired token on their scales that said "read at the gate". The Stallreeve left them be for a month.

## Winter, months 4 to 6

**Month 4.** The wet came, with its hard spells. The stallholders' tokens went up in number. The Stallreeve ordered them taken down: a token on a scale proves nothing a month later, he said, and a scale read true in autumn can be filed light by midwinter. All but two came down. The two kept theirs and were fined a small fish each. The Tally-keeper cut a notch on a stick for each. She had begun, on her own account and unasked, to keep a stick of the gate's readings: the date, whose scale, and whether the stranger had found it true or out, and by how much in its cups. She kept it in the hall's own manner, split down its length, one half kept in the hall and one half in her own house, so that nobody could change a notch. She told no one for some weeks. The Assay-master found the halves in the second month of winter and said nothing. He had been meaning to do the same.

The Assay-master began his own test. He had for years known of faults in a few scales in the town that were his to know: the bench itself, three of the stall scales he had passed with a shake of the head, and one at the clay hall he had never confessed to. He sent his apprentice to the gate, over the month, with fourteen scales to be read, a few of them with a small known fault set in by his own hand: a thumbnail of clay pressed under one pan, a thread of reed in a cord. The stranger read all fourteen. He kept the apprentice's report to himself.

**Month 5.** A hard spell, ice on the shallows, fever in the crowded reed houses. The fever took the old and the very young, in the ordinary way, in the houses at the back of the bank where the stilts are shortest. The Stallreeve moved four stalls under the hall's roof to keep the market open on the worst days, and the hall was crowded and warm and quarrelsome and nobody died of it.

Some nights in the dead of winter, the blue star went dim in the middle of the night for about an hour and then was as before. Fewer people were awake for it than in autumn, because of the cold, but the watch saw it, and the boatmen on the landing.

A high water was called by the tide-reckoner, who is old and lives at the edge of the Flats. She sent word to the Stallreeve four days ahead, by a child, that the Alder would crest over the lowest paths in the dark between one evening and the next. The stalls moved, the boats were drawn up, and three families went to the mounds with their goats. The water came half a day later than she said. It was the right height. The Stallreeve sent her salt and she took a small part of it. The feeling in her hands went out of them for the season, as always. She got her house cleared of snow by neighbors, and she thanked no one aloud.

**Month 6.** The deepest of the cold and the leanest of the stores. The grain bins on the bank were lower than the Stallreeve liked, but not low. The dried fish held. The hill carriers' charcoal was nearly gone by the end of the month, as it usually is, and a few of the forges at the back of the bank went cold for days.

In the dark of the last week of the month, a man bound by debt to a stallholder who sells in the yard's corner was sent by his master to take a cup out of the stranger's nest at the landing, where the stranger sits late. The man went. What came of it is for the Keeper (report-001.md). The town knows only what follows from each way.

## Spring, months 7 to 9

**Month 7.** The flood season. The rains eased, the Alder rose. The tide-reckoner was asked by the Stallreeve to tell the height. She sent back that it would be an ordinary flood and that she did not need her feet for an ordinary flood. She did not tell it. Her hands had begun to come back.

The weirs were repaired. The channels moved, as they do, and two reed islands joined and one split. The boatwrights were busy.

**Month 8.** The spring flood came, ordinary in height. Sowing took five weeks of every hand that could hold a hoe. The hill carriers' scale and the town's scale were read again at the gatepost, quietly, on the Stallreeve's order, because the carriers wanted a witness for the new year's trade.

The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides of spring and came back. A story went round that they had gone to the Maun Stair. Nobody had followed them.

The head of the hill goods sent a walker up the Old Rudd Way to Ruddfold, with a split stick and a few words: that the town's hall means to weigh its old weights before the eleven, and that the hill folk should send a witness with their own weights. He did it without telling the council. The Stallreeve learned of it from a carrier's wife and said nothing.

**Month 9.** The council sat after the sowing and voted on the Assay-master's offer. Seven for, three against, one not in the room (the ship-owner of the open water, who was out on the Gulf). For: the Assay-master, the heads of the clay and of the reed and fish, the masters of the potters, boatwrights and weavers, and the ship-owner from the channels. Against: the head of the hill goods, the master of the smiths, the head of salt. The head of salt spoke longest. Salt is the town's store of worth, he said, and a store of worth is made by agreeing what the weight is, not by finding out. The council did not answer him. It resolved that the old weights be weighed before the eleven, by the stranger's scale, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam, on a day the Assay-master and the Stallreeve would set, after the harvest, and that the Stallreeve would carry the invitation.

The master of the potters had by now fired a nest of eleven new clay standards, in one kiln so they shrank alike. They were not marked. They sat in the potters' hall until the old weights should be weighed.

## Summer, months 10 to 12

**Month 10.** Long and warm. The reed was cut early. The first upriver boats from the Aldermark came down to trade. They brought grain, wool and some bronze. No one came from the king. Two of the traders brought their own scales to the gate on a dare from the stalls and were read. The scales were out by a little. The traders laughed, paid in barley, and went away with a story.

**Month 11.** The Stallreeve carried the council's invitation to the gatepost himself on a market day, in front of the yard: that the eleven ask the Weigher to weigh the hall's old weights, at the first market after the grain is in, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. The Stallreeve had the Tally-keeper cut the words on a split stick first. The stranger heard it. It said nothing that anyone could report as an answer, and went on reading the next scale. The yard took this as it takes much of what the stranger does: as a fact to be argued over. The Stallreeve laid the other half of the stick at the foot of the gatepost and walked back up to the hall. The thread stops there; see report-001.md.

Ships of the southern harbor towns were in the Gulf. None of the larger ones came up to the town's landing this year. Their goods came up in the town's boats, and the town's boatmen weighed everything they carried in the town's scales, and the ship-owner from the channels did well out of it. A trader's mate from one of the coasters asked at the gate whether the stranger would read his captain's scale. It did, and told no one a word about it afterward.

**Month 12.** The grain came in. The salt pans ran. The last of the long light. The town was slow and tired and a little richer than it had been. The yard at the gate was shorter than in autumn, because most people had had their scales read, and longer on the market days. The stranger was still at the gatepost, and still read in the order of the line.

The year turned. The first market of the new autumn is the day the council set.

## Why the numbers moved

See state.md. In short: an ordinary year, with an ordinary winter fever. The market was less quarrelsome than it had been since last spring, because a place now existed where a dispute of weight could be settled quickly and in public. The treasure moved little. The hall spent a little of its scrap silver on the potters' standards and took in a little more from the fees of a busier market. The watch grew by four, on the Stallreeve's own word.
