## The talk at the hall door

The market day after the yes, at dusk, when the bench closed. The Assay-master stood in the hall's doorway with a lamp behind him and did not ask the stranger in; the stranger stood on the step with its brass. He said the door was the eleven's until they set the day, and that whatever it had to say it could say there. The yard had gone to its supper and the watch were at the gate. It said the five things of decision 5 to him and to no one else, in the order the file gives, and he answered each as below.

1. Why the eleven should take a stranger's scale. The god's words answer it (item 5, first line): "They shouldn't, on my word. Take the Weightward's own weights... I'm offering a measurement, not a standard." He heard it standing. He said it was the first time a seller had told him not to buy, and that he would carry the weighing as an afternoon's work, as it said, and the sum of it was only worth what his beam showed. He asked it, as a demand: "Then say that to the eleven on the day, in those words." Not answered by the god's words. On meeting silence he said, "Then I'll say it, and I'll say it was said to me," and he did not press.
2. Whether it is more than a man with good brass. The god's words answer it: "Yes. I'm not going to give you the rest before the weighing... I'll tell you what you ask after, and I won't lie to you about it." He was silent a long time. He did not ask what. He said, "After the weighing, then," and that he would hold it to the word "after". He said nothing of it to anyone later and carries it as his own; he will not tell the Stallreeve.
3. Who sent it. The god's words answer it: "No one. No king. I came down the old bank alone, and I have nobody behind me that I know of." He said, "That you know of," and that the clause was the careful part. He asked, "Where did you learn to weigh, then, if no one sent you?" Not answered by the god's words. On meeting silence he said, "That is a no or a not yet, and I have had both from you," and let it lie. He told the Stallreeve afterward only the first sentence and the clause.
4. Why it asked how the old Weightward died. The god's words answer it: "Because the seat has stood empty a year, and I wanted to know whether the quiet was an ordinary one. I'm sorry he was your friend. I won't ask again unless you tell me." He said nothing for the length of a breath, and said then that it had been a year this spring and that he had nothing to tell yet. He said he would tell it when he knew what there was to tell. He asked nothing here. He did not mention the rack.
5. What it wants of the hall after the weighing. The god's words answer it: "The weights set right and anyone able to check them... I'd call you the likeliest, but that isn't mine to say." He answered stiffly that he was not a keeper and had a beam, and that he would not have that said in the ring or at the gate while the old man's seat was a year cold, and that he owed it nothing for the saying. He said he would argue with it on the day, and that it should expect to be argued with at the beam. He asked, "You asked the Stallreeve who holds the key and where the weights are kept. Why?" Not answered by the god's words. On meeting silence he did not repeat it. He said the eleven would answer the Stallreeve what the Stallreeve had asked, and that they would answer it plain.

He went in and shut the door. The lamp was left burning on the step. He did not mention the clay nest, or the seat, or the king, to the stranger. What he told others of it: the Stallreeve, first sentence and clause of item 3 only; the eleven, at the sitting before the harvest, the offer of a measurement and not a standard, as his own report of it.

## The weighing

The day: the first market of the new autumn, month 1. The place: the hall, the eleven in their ring. Present besides: the stranger at the end of the bench, its small brass pair on a board and the nest of cups at its hand; the Assay-master at his bench; the Tally-keeper at the bench's end with a long stick, her apprentice at the door calling each figure to the yard once cut; the Stallreeve at the door with the watch holding the yard back; the master of the potters with the new nest in a reed basket; the old weigher of the hill kin beside the head of the hill goods; a few of the old of the hall. The door stood open. The gate was not kept that day, and the Stallreeve had told the line so the market before. The Tally-keeper's other sticks were not cut that day; no notch was cut against any scale for it, by the Stallreeve's ruling.

In order:

1. The Assay-master read the three terms aloud as the hall's rules for the day, as the eleven had taken them whole (eight to three) before the harvest. He opened the chest with the second key before the ring. The first key, which hangs in the ring by custom, was not turned and stayed on its peg.
2. He lifted the rack out onto a cloth. Six weights in seven seats; the seventh seat empty and clean where the others are dusty. He said aloud: he had found it so when he first opened the chest after the laying-out, and twice since, each time the same; he had told the Stallreeve the evening before, that the rack was not full and nothing else, and no one else. The head of reed and fish asked whether he had opened it alone. He answered alone, both times. The ring was silent. Said by him in the stranger's hearing, public.
3. The three bronze pieces, smallest first, in its pans. Called and cut: first 48; second 96; third 192. The Assay-master's check on his beam: the second balanced two of the first, the third two of the second, the second of each pair made with the sand bag the old keeper had taught him, brought equal to the lower piece on the same beam. Cut beside the calls. No difference.
4. The three stone pieces, on the hall's beam, the Assay-master's hand on the beam, the stranger standing over it with its small brass in its hand. Called and cut: fourth 384; fifth 766; sixth 1,536. His check: the fourth balanced two of the third. The fifth: at the first trial he read it level against two of the fourth. The stranger's call was 766. Both were cut. He asked for a second trial and read it light by a hair, and said aloud: "I read it level. I read it wrong. It is light." The Tally-keeper cut both of his readings against the one call; the call was not changed. The old chip at the foot was seen by all at the beam, and the stranger said nothing of it. The sixth balanced two of the fifth within the beam's tremble; he judged it no difference, and the call, 1,536, stood. Exactly one piece had two readings of his cut against one of the stranger's, and it is the fifth.
5. The Assay-master set four of the hall's small fired pieces against the first bronze, as the old keeper's practice is. The hall's side went down. He said so. He asked the Tally-keeper plainly what the stranger had called his eight agreeing pieces at the door a year ago; she read it from her stick: about twelve and a half each. The head of the hill goods did the sum aloud: fifty against forty-eight, about a twentieth. The Assay-master then said he had tested every scale in the town for thirty years against those pieces and that if they were heavy every scale he passed was passed heavy, and that he had not known it. The sum was done first by the head of the hill goods aloud, after the Assay-master had it in his head; the stranger did not say it and was not asked to.
6. The master of the smiths asked the stranger across the ring: "Weigher, where is the seventh? What did it weigh? Can you find it?" Not answered by the god's words. On meeting silence the ring looked at the Assay-master, who said the stranger weighs what is put in front of it and there was nothing there. The master of the smiths said, "It is good at that." Not a demand or a charge, so not held.
7. At midday the clay head put out bread and a jar of water. The god's words answer it (item 3): "I take no fee and no gift for the day, from the hall or from any of the eleven." The bread was put away.
8. The Assay-master asked, once the figures were cut: "What should the fifth be?" The god's words answer it (item 2): it tells the eleven what each piece is now; the eleven decide which is the town's weight. It said so in those words and no more.
9. The nest, brought by the master of the potters, eleven pieces unmarked, fired in one kiln so they would shrink alike. The ring agreed to weigh it, since it was there. Called and cut, smallest first: 13, 25, 37, 52, 75, 89, 98, 130, 150, 179, 181. The Assay-master's beam against the hall's fired pieces and against each other: pieces one, four, six, eight and ten came down against their count of the hall's pieces; pieces three, seven and eleven came up; two, five and nine level. Two of the second did not make the fourth: the fourth went down by two. Thirty-seven, the third, did not make the first and second together: light by one. The two sets of figures did not differ anywhere, so nothing was cut twice. The nest does not agree with itself, and sits about a twentieth heavy against the old weights as the hall's pieces do. The master of the potters said they were fired in one kiln so they would shrink alike. She asked that the nest be left in the hall under the Assay-master and not carried back through the yard. He agreed.
10. The old weigher of the hill kin, at the head of the hill goods' asking, set his own stones on the hall's beam against the old six on the terms the eleven had agreed: his readings cut, nothing called in ones. His stones agreed with the old bronze at the first and second to the beam's tremble, ran a hair heavy at the third, agreed at the fourth and sixth, and at the fifth went down a hair against the old fifth. He said the hill kin had sold by the hall's pieces and supposed them heavy for as long as the hall had had them, and had said nothing because a market is a market. He asked the stranger to weigh his stones in the ring. Not answered by the god's words (the terms cover the hall's pieces and the nest). Silence; the Assay-master said the hall's day was the hall's. The old weigher took the figures on a stick of his own while they were called; no one stopped him.
11. The ring argued. What each of the eleven said and did:
   - The Assay-master: opened the chest; said the six and the seat; misread the fifth and said so; said he had passed scales heavy for thirty years and had not known it; moved nothing. Told the ring what the stranger had said to him, "a measurement, not a standard," as his own report. Did not vote on the first ("six is not seven, and the fifth is chipped; I cannot give the town a standard with a seat empty"); voted against the second; voted for the third.
   - The head of reed and fish (a woman of about sixty, of the channel people, the eldest of the four trade heads): asked whether he had opened the chest alone. Said the channel folk had said for years the bench ran heavy against the fish. Voted for the first motion, against the second, for the third.
   - The head of salt: said "A twentieth, on every sack that has gone out of this town for forty years," and that a store of worth is made by agreeing the weight and not by finding it out. Moved the second motion. Asked that the sum not be called to the yard until the eleven had it, and was told the figures were already being called. Voted against the first, for the second, and for the third for its clause on debts.
   - The clay head: wanted the nest weighed; after it was weighed said nothing for some time. Moved the third motion. Offered the stranger bread at midday (item 7). Voted for the third, against the second, no vote on the first.
   - The head of the hill goods: brought the witness; counted the room; moved the first motion; said waiting is what the hall has done for forty years. Voted for the first, against the second, against the third.
   - The master of the potters: brought the nest; said little after it was called. Asked it be left in the hall. Did not vote on the first or second; voted for the third.
   - The master of the boatwrights: asked which weight he was to tell his men to use at the market the next morning. The ring answered, by the third motion, the hall's present pieces as before. Voted against the first, against the second, for the third.
   - The master of the weavers: counted the nest's figures against each other aloud and said they did not agree. Said little else. Voted against the first, no vote on the second, for the third.
   - The master of the smiths: asked the stranger the question of item 6. Said "A stranger's brass is a stranger's brass" again. Voted against the first, for the second; no vote on the third.
   - The ship owner of the channels: seconded the third. Voted against the first, against the second, for the third.
   - The ship owner of the open water, back from the Gulf and in the room: said he had been on the water the day they voted to ask and would be on the water the day they decided, and that he would weigh his cargo against any of the pieces. No vote on the first or second; voted for the third.
12. Three motions. First (the head of the hill goods): that the old six be the town's weight and the hall's fired pieces be made again to them. For two (the head of the hill goods, the head of reed and fish), against five (salt, smiths, boatwrights, weavers, the channel ship owner), four not voting (the Assay-master, the clay head, the master of the potters, the open-water ship owner). Fell. Second (the head of salt): that the hall's fired pieces stand as the town's weight and the old six be kept as a thing of the hall's past. For two (salt, smiths), against six (the hill goods, reed and fish, the clay head, the boatwrights, the channel ship owner, the Assay-master), three not voting (potters, weavers, open-water). Fell. Third (the clay head): that no weight be named the town's until the seventh is found or the ring has given it up and the nest has been fired again; that the market stand on the hall's present pieces meanwhile; that every debt and bargain made before the day be reckoned by them as made; that nothing in the hall be filed, mended, replaced or carried out; that the chest be opened only before two of the eleven; and that the matter come back at the first sitting after the next grain is in. For nine, against one (the head of the hill goods), one not voting (the smiths). Carried. Then, without a count, the ring agreed that the old keeper's loft, which custom keeps shut until a new keeper takes the seat, be looked in by three.
13. Decided: the ring did not decide which was the town's weight. It decided to wait, with the market on the hall's present pieces, debts reckoned as made, the six and the nest kept in the hall, and the loft to be looked in. It did not offer the seat that day. The clay head said it should be offered then; the Assay-master said a seat is given when what it keeps is whole, and the ring did not vote on it.
14. The yard, from the step: the Stallreeve told them in the open what the ring had done and what the four pieces had shown, and that he would sooner they had it from him than from a boy at the stalls. A boy cried "Weigher, will you be our Weightward now?" at the stranger when it came out. No decided words answer it, so it met silence (see Asked of the god). A boy is not speaking for the eleven, so it is not held.
15. Dusk: the eleven rose; the Assay-master stayed in the hall with the stranger and the door was shut. The held moment is below.

On the third day after, three went up the old keeper's loft stair: the Assay-master, the Stallreeve, and the clay head as witness. They came down within the hour. I write only that they went; the Keeper gives what is seen there. In the town the three were seen to come down quiet. After it the ring was told nothing in my files, and the town waited. I wrote nothing of what the three said at the ring's next sitting.

## Held

1. The Weightward's seat offered. Month 9, after the council sat following the sowing. The Assay-master had spoken for it on his own terms: an office, not a gift, with a term set and the eleven's hand on it and his own on the beam beside the stranger's. The master of the weavers spoke against for the first time; the head of salt, the master of the smiths and the head of the hill goods spoke against as before. Six for (the Assay-master, the clay head, the master of the potters, the head of reed and fish, the master of the boatwrights, the ship owner of the channels), four against, the open-water ship owner absent on the Gulf. The Stallreeve carried the eleven's word to the gatepost on the next market day. The moment: the yard in its line, the stranger holding a scale, the Tally-keeper beside the Stallreeve with the cut stick, the Assay-master a little apart with his arms folded. The Stallreeve says, in his own words: "The eleven ask the Weigher to take the Weightward's seat and keep the hall's standards, for a term of three years and a day, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam beside yours, and with the eleven's right to put the seat down at any sitting that three of them call." He lays the other half stick at the foot of the gatepost. Stop there. For the rest of the year the eleven have waited, the Stallreeve has said nothing more at the gate, and the matter of the weight was tabled again at the sitting after the grain, until the Weigher has said what it will say of the seat. The yard cried "Weightward" at it a few days after, a boy first; nobody acted as if it had been answered.
2. The Assay-master's question. Month 1, the evening of the weighing, in the hall. The eleven have risen and gone, the yard has gone to its supper, the door is shut and the lamp lit. The six lie on the cloth with the rack beside them and the seventh seat empty. The nest is by the wall in its basket. Present: the Assay-master and the stranger, nobody else. He says, in his own words: "It is after. You told me at this door there was more than a man in you and that you would tell me what I asked after. I am asking. What are you?" Stop there. For the rest of the year he has said nothing of it to anyone, asks nothing more, and carries on at the bench. I judge he would wait the year; he is slow, and the stranger's silence sits in his mind as a no that is also a not yet. I do not think he would act on his own to ask again, but he may, and if he does it is not mine to write.
3. A bargain proposed to the stranger. Month 4, at the landing at dusk, by the channel folk's lamp. Present: the stranger, Bren, two boatmen, the ship owner of the channels. It has been a good autumn for his boats and a better summer before it. He says in his own words: "Weigher, I run boats up and down. Every boat that comes to my landing from the south or goes down from the north, read it before it's unloaded, at dawn, and say what's in it. I'll pay you for the year, not in silver, a measure of salt for every boat, and I'll have you at my landing and no other, and that in front of the Stallreeve." Stop there. For the rest of the year Bren's household has said nothing, the ship owner has said only that he spoke and it listened, the Stallreeve has not heard of it, and no other ship owner has made the same offer.
4. The southern coaster's master at the head of the line. Month 10, a market day, before light the line formed and moved up. She is a woman of about forty, her crew of about a dozen hands and a mate. For some days at the landing she traded salt, pots and reed mats, paid in goods from the south coast weighed in the town's scales, asked the news of the town, who bought what, what a pot fetched. A man of her crew stood at the gatepost a whole market morning watching the readings and went without a scale. She joined the line before light like anyone and waited her turn. The moment: the stranger's hand has just come off the last scale before hers; she steps up to the foot of the gatepost; the yard behind her has gone quiet, because no coaster of the southern towns came to the landing the summer before. Stop there. I give her no words and the stranger none.

Of the three things the release names as held if they come, all three came: the eleven's offer of the seat (1), the Assay-master's question (2), and a bargain that would change its footing (3). The fourth is the coaster master's moment (4). I have no other unsure matter that I did not sort as answered or unanswered.

## How the god was met

**End of Year 1.**
- The Stallreeve took the yes and terms to the hall himself, at the evening. He heard from the stranger that his rule on the sticks was good and said the notch made it a rule.
- The Tally-keeper cut the mark two pans, level, at the head of her stick and has cut it at every level reading since. She cut the price as remitted and the first five sticks, and showed the stranger the first stale stick in month 3 of Year 2; it looked and said nothing.
- The master of the potters took the request to carry the nest up herself.
- Bren answered the question about the low tides plainly and for the household. The household decided it asked because it did not mean to go.
- The head of the hill goods did not argue the terms; he asked the witness's place and paid for it with his surety.
- The Assay-master met it at the door standing, carried the offer of a measurement to the eleven as his own report, and kept the "more than a man" to himself.

The head of salt spoke longest: a stick cut once cannot be recut, a store of worth is made by agreeing. He paid in his own trade, where the salt-rakers greeted him for a month with "read at the gate." ## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

**A. The potters' test tiles, from month 3.**
- Who tries: Orrin of the east kilns first, then three of the other six kiln masters by spring. Each fires a small tile of each load's clay in a corner of the kiln before filling, to see whether the tile cracks when the load would.
- What success means: the tile cracks when the load will, so kiln masters can set aside loads that will crack, and cracked loads fall by about the share set aside. What failure means: tiles do not predict, the cracking stays as it was, and the kiln masters blame weather or kiln.
- Circumstances from my record: practiced kiln masters and Orrin's reading of baskets last year (+10); a true scale and kilns in store (+5); the pits day showing no difference of sand and shell between the loads that fired well and the loads that cracked, so the cause is not that (no factor, a fact of the record); last year's sorting by weight failed, learned from (+5); winter firing and drying in a hard frost (-10); a lower-pit lump in each set (no factor).
- The rest of my year both ways. If it succeeds, the east kilns do well from month 6, the heavy digger sells at a fair price again from month 8 to a few kiln masters, and the quarrel of diggers and kilns quiets. If it fails, the kiln masters go back to weighing a basket or to cursing the wind, and the quarrel keeps; by month 9 the tiles are called Orrin's folly or Orrin's patience by who one asks.

**B. The potters' second nest, month 7.**
- Who tries: the master of the potters, fired as the ring said, twenty-two pieces in one kiln, eleven chosen by the gate's scale to agree with each other and with the hall's present pieces.
- Success: a nest of eleven that agree with itself to a hair. Failure: it is still uneven by a thirtieth and she fires again next spring.
- Circumstances: a practiced master with the true scale at the gate (+10 and +5); a failed first nest learned from (+5); fired twenty-two to choose eleven, a larger stock to pick from (+10); the hall's present pieces to match, heavy against the old, which is not her standard to set (no factor).
- The rest of my year both ways. If it succeeds, the nest is kept in the hall beside the first and she asks it be weighed at the sitting after the grain; if it fails, she says nothing and fires again.

**C. The loft.** Three went up on the third day after the weighing. What was seen is the Keeper's. From that moment the thread is stopped in all my files.

**D. The boatmen.** The Stallreeve asked, once, alone, a few days after the carriers' market in month 2. What he asked: who came to see the old Weightward in his last weeks, who he sent word to, whether any boat took anything for him. What a boatman answered is the Keeper's, and the thread stops at the question.

No other outcome in my year is uncertain and contested. The hill witness, the bonded man's master and his scale, the votes, the quarrels over the twentieth and the people's reactions are mine and I settled them.

## What turns on a power

1. **Every figure called.** I took every call as true: the six old weights, the nest, the diggers' lumps, every reading at the gate. If a call came out otherwise, the day's argument turns: the Assay-master's misread fifth, the sum of the four hall pieces, the nest's unevenness.
2. **The master's scale.** Settled by the Keeper as level; I wrote it as read and cut.
3. **The clay.** The pits day turned wholly on the pan's reading: no more sand and shell in the cracked lumps than in the well fired, about a twentieth. I wrote all six lumps (96, 91 clay and 5 not; 104, 99 and 5; 88, 84 and 4 from the well fired; and 100, 96 and 4; 92, 88 and 4; 108, 102 and 6 from the cracked) as the Keeper's note allowed. If any other reading was meant, the kiln masters' and diggers' reaction turns.
4. **The fee and its nights.** The town says its lamp burns all hours; decision 16 says it does not sleep; I wrote it as the town's saying and as the stranger's own account.
5. **The tide-reckoner.** She called the winter water four days ahead in month 5, right in height and a few hours off the hour, inside her priced knack. In spring she declined an ordinary flood. Her hands were numb through the winter.
6. **The blue star.** The town saw it dim on certain nights, by season, and has more to say of it than last year. I gave no count and no cause.
7. **The witnessing.** In month 6 it stood witness to a boat sale under decision 15 and said only what it saw and heard.

## Asked of the god

"Answered in full" means by the decided words only.

1. Day of the weighing, the master of the smiths: "Weigher, where is the seventh? What did it weigh? Can you find it?" Not answered by the god's words. Silence; the ring looked at the Assay-master.
2. Day of the weighing, the clay head: bread and water. Answered by item 3, in its words.
3. Day of the weighing, the Assay-master: "What should the fifth be?" Answered by item 2.
4. Day of the weighing, the old weigher of the hill kin: "Weigh my stones in front of the ring." Not answered by the god's words (the terms cover the hall's pieces and the nest). Silence.
5. Day of the weighing, a boy in the yard: "Weigher, will you be our Weightward now?" Not answered; silence; not held, since a boy is not speaking for the eleven.
6. Month 1, a stallholder: "How long will you stay?" Answered by item 10.
7. Month 1, a child: "Are you the drowned one's child?" Answered by item 10.
8. Month 2, a hill carrier: "Do you come from Hask?" Answered by item 10.
9. Month 2, the kiln masters at the pits: "Which clay is good for a kiln?" Answered by item 11 and the Keeper's clay note: it said it cannot tell and will not pretend.
10. Month 3, a potter: "Is this clay clean?" Answered by item 11.
11. Month 3, the first stale stick, shown by the Tally-keeper. It watched, as item 8 says it will, and said nothing.
12. Month 3, a stallholder: "May I say my scale was read at your gate?" Answered by item 10.
13. Month 4, a net-mender: "Reckon my debt for me, and strike it." Answered by item 13: it does not strike debts and will not say it can.
14. Month 4, the ship owner of the channels at the landing: the bargain. Held.
15. Month 6, two households of boatwrights: "Stand witness to this sale." Answered by item 15.
16. Month 7, two households at the reed island: "Who owns it? You say what is true." Answered by item 14.
17. Month 7, the master of the potters at the gate: the second nest's pieces brought to the line. Answered by item 9.
18. Month 9, the eleven by the Stallreeve: the seat. Held.
19. Month 9, a boy in the yard: "Weightward." Not answered; silence.
20. Month 10, the coaster master in the line: held; nothing written of her words.
21. Month 12, a salt-raker offering a pinch of salt as the fee. Answered by item 9.
22. Throughout, the yard: "Where do you sleep?" and similar. Answered by item 16.
23. Month 1, the evening: the Assay-master's question. Held.
24. End of Year 1, at the door: the Assay-master asked three things beyond the five answered. Marked above as not answered by the god's words.

## Names said aloud

- **Ruddfold**, said by the old weigher of the hill kin in the ring, month 1, to the ring and to the stranger.
- **Orrin**, said by the east kiln master himself, month 2, at the pits, to the stranger, in giving his fee. He said it already in Year 1.
- **Maun Stair**, said by Bren at the landing, late in Year 1, to the stranger, in answer to its question.
- **Alder**, said by the Stallreeve at the gatepost, month 5, to the line, in the stranger's hearing, giving the tide-reckoner's word.
- **Corrith**, said by one of the boatmen at the landing, month 10, to Bren in the stranger's hearing, of the southern coaster's home port, in ordinary talk of trade.
- No name for the town or the people was said in its hearing. No office holder's own name was said, nor the potters' master's, the head of the hill goods', or any of the eleven's, nor the old weigher's, nor the coaster master's. The town's nicknames for the stranger were not said to it: "Bren's weigher" (channel folk), "the Cupman" (salt-rakers), "the coat" (hill folk).
