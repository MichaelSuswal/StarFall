# The unnamed people: the people's record

Campaign sim-06. Written by the DM at the close of creation Day 3, 2026-11-24 (Pacific), from what the player said and agreed at the table. Read back to the player and agreed: "Yes, with no strikes." Stores, treasure, trade, strength and mood are the Keeper's to set on Night 3a. Where the town stands, and everything of the land and its past, is the world's.

## Who they are

Common mortal folk. The player gave no name for the people and none for their town: "The town stays 'the town' until a mortal names it in play. The people have no name for themselves that I will give, and they wait for one the same way." Both stay unnamed until play names them.

> I want them to be a river-and-reed people, and I want them mixed. They are about five thousand, descended from several small groups that were pushed together by the floods. Some are channel-dwellers who fish and cut reed, some are salt-rakers from the flats, some are clay-bank farmers and potters, and some are hill people who came down to sell ore and charcoal. They do not share one old kinship. What they share is that the market is the only place all four groups meet, so the market is the town's real center, and it is older than any lord. They are practical, a little suspicious, good with small numbers, and quick to notice when a friend gives short measure. I want them to be proud of being hard to cheat.

> Common mortal folk, like any other. The four groups differ in look and speech: the channel people are wiry and dark from the sun, the salt-rakers are weathered and quiet, the clay people are broad and stocky, and the hill people are tall and pale.

That floods pushed them together and that the market is older than any lord are the people's beliefs about themselves, for the Keeper to reconcile.

## How many, and how they live

About 5,000, the default. Nothing was argued about size.

> On the old raised banks, in houses of reed and clay on timber stilts or on mounds, joined by raised paths and flat-bottomed boats. They keep reed gardens and grain on the firm clay, nets and weirs in the channels, and salt pans on the flats. They fire pottery and cut reed for mats, roofing and baskets. From the hills they get ore and charcoal, and they work them with a bloomery at the pre-steel level you described. I am not naming any technology beyond that. For now, a measure is a thing carved and fired: standard clay weights, notched tally-sticks, and reed baskets of fixed volume.

The watch: "Sixty carry a spear in ordinary times, and the Reeve could call up to three hundred in a flood or a raid. Most of them are fishermen and rakers, and few are trained." Ruled at the table as ordinary, not priced.

## Their code

In the player's words:

- "They honor a debt paid on the day it falls due, and a witness who stays to the end of a bargain."
- "A person who runs from a debt is called a dry channel."
- "Even their worst thieves will not steal from a boat that is drawn up in a flood, or from a house with the door open and a lamp lit in the window. Those are the marks of someone sheltering, and they would not touch them."
- Practical, a little suspicious, good with small numbers, quick to notice short measure, proud of being hard to cheat. Ruled at the table as a temper, able to fail, not a guarantee. The player: "I do not want the guarantee against being cheated. It is a temper, and it can fail."

Debt-bonded folk exist among them: "Debt-bonded folk exist here, and I accept it." What the god means to do about it is in its conduct profile, not here; it is not the town's law at landing.

## Skills and culture

Fishing, reed-cutting, weirs and nets, salt-raking, farming on the clay, pottery, mats, roofing and baskets, boat-building, weaving, bloomery iron from hill ore and charcoal, and keeping counts by fired weights, notched sticks and fixed baskets. All ordinary, none priced.

What matters most at their market, as the player described it: "the flood-silt clay that their potters use. It matters more than anyone realizes, because a good pot is what everyone buys, and the quality of the clay decides whether a kiln load cracks. Right now each potter judges clay by feel and by rumor, and every kiln-master suspects the clay-diggers of mixing grit into the load."

## Priced edges and weaknesses

- The tide-reckoner's knack, a mortal's magic, priced at the table. Her own cost is the whole price; no weakness is asked of the people. See Their magic. Subject to the Keeper's reconciliation. Settled at creation. Ledger: pending, Night 3.
- Their being hard to cheat is a temper and not a guarantee. Not priced. Settled at creation. Ledger: pending, Night 3.
- A watch of sixty and a call-up of three hundred, few trained: ordinary. Not priced. Settled at creation. Ledger: pending, Night 3.
- A factor's rule, "no false measure in a sealed term", is an oath that can be broken, not a binding. Not priced. It is recorded on the power card. Settled at creation. Ledger: pending, Night 3.
- No default was refused for a step in any edge price. See Helpers.

## Their magic

One mortal with a knack.

> One person, and I want it plain. The tide-reckoner is an old woman of the salt flats who can tell the height of a flood several days ahead by what she feels in her feet. It costs her the feeling in her hands for a season after each telling. She cannot cure and cannot curse, and she can be wrong about a flood that is hours too late.

Priced by the DM: the cost she bears is the price. Terms the DM added and the player accepted with the read-back: she cannot teach it at the player's telling, and it ends with her unless play makes another. The player: "The tide-reckoner is fine as priced, and she can be wrong about the hour. I expect to get use from her and to pay for it." How common such workers are is the world's. No one else among the people was given a knack.

## What they believe

> They say there are small gods in every channel and bank, who must be fed with the first of a catch. There was once a larger one, and the story is that it drowned, and that is why the tide keeps coming. They say no god has ever stayed to count what it was owed. They do not know I am coming.

These are the people's beliefs, for the Keeper to reconcile.

## How they settle disputes

Between buyer and seller, the office the player calls the Reeve of the Gate. Everything else:

> The council of eleven in its ring, with the head of whichever trade is not a party to the quarrel as the one who leads the hearing. A death, a boundary or a marriage gone bad is argued before them and settled by a price in measure. I rule only on questions of weight, and I do not take the other cases unless the council asks.

The council, as designed: the heads of the four trades (reed and fish, salt, clay, hill goods), the masters of the potters, boatwrights, weavers and smiths, a ship-owner from the channels and one from the open water, and the chief assayer. At landing it is "a loose council that argues and decides little". Ships on open water are a wish, for the world to place.

## Helpers

Designed, not yet serving. The Weigher lands with no helpers in fact, and with no seat that is its own in fact. Recorded 2026-11-24, creation Day 3, with the player's yes: "I accept all four rulings, including the cost to my seat and helpers. I landed without being asked to, and I will have to win the town."

> The market stands, the house stands, and the hall is in it. It is a poor hall: a plank bench, a few clay standards cracked and mismatched, and a scale nobody trusts. The eleven already sit, as a loose council that argues and decides little. The three offices exist under other names, held by the people I described, and they know they are in office. They are waiting for something, but not for me.

The offices, and what stands at landing. The names below are the player's own for them; the town has its own names, which the player did not give.

- The Warden of the Hall. Stands under the town's own law, held by the chief assayer, who tests weights for the town and sits among the eleven. Designed to keep the standards and test the weights when the Weigher is away.
- The Reeve of the Gate. Stands under the town's own law. Runs the market: stalls, fees, disputes between buyers and sellers, the watch and the runners.
- The Clerk of the Seals. Stands under the town's own law under another name. Designed to keep the register of every sealed bargain and every licensed factor. No mortal feels a breaking: the Weigher tells the Clerk, and the register is where its knowing is matched to terms.
- The keeper of the standards. Vacant: "The old keeper of the standards died last spring and nobody has replaced them, which is why the hall is poor and the market quarrels." A separate office from the Warden's. The Weigher means to take it itself.
- Factors, "two or three to start". None exists at landing. The Weigher means to find and license them in play; a licence is given face to face and nobody holds one at landing.

How orders are designed to travel, once anyone takes them:

> By hand and by custom, since they have no better way. The Reeve's runners carry my rulings, written as notches on a stick and spoken aloud at the gate. There are tally-sticks split in two, one half kept in the hall and one half carried, so that no message can be changed without the halves failing to match. I would like to keep this as my first law: a word of mine is only a word of mine if the stick matches. Boats carry the sticks to the far channels, and a drum on the highest bank calls the town to the hall when a ruling has been made.

What it means: until each office is won in play, no one acts for the Weigher while it is away, its unplayed time runs on its standing conduct alone, and the town runs itself under its own council. The form is a bend, not a refused default. It bears on no edge and buys nothing.
