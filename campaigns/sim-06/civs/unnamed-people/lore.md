# What the people now say

Campaign sim-06. Kept by the civ agent. This is the people's own telling, first at the close of Year 1 and then, in the section at the foot, at the close of Year 2. It is what they say, not what is so. Where they explain a cause, they are wrong about something that matters, and the Keeper keeps the truth.

## The stranger

They call it the Weigher, because that is what it said it was and it did not argue the name. Other names are said among themselves and have not been said in its hearing: the channel folk call it Bren's weigher, because Bren fed it first. The salt-rakers call it the Cupman, for the nest of seven brass cups. The hill folk call it the coat.

What they say it is:

- The channel folk say it is a man from the sea towns who lost his house and is making himself a living honestly. They like this story and tell it to children.
- The salt-rakers say it is the old Weightward's kin come back for the hall, a story they like less and say more quietly. The stranger said it had not been in the town before that morning, and they put that aside.
- The hill carriers say it is a stranger of the stone towns to the east, a place they call Hask, and that its brass was made there. They have no reason for it beyond the brass, which is not of the town.
- The old of the salt flats say it came down the bank at first light the way the walkers came who sang the lists, and that it will sing the town's names next. Nobody knows what to make of this, and the line at the gate tells it to each other as a joke.
- A few say nothing and watch. The Assay-master is one of them. The Tally-keeper is another.

They say it refuses silver because it wants more than silver. They have not agreed what. The head of the hill goods says it is playing for the hall. The Stallreeve says it refuses silver so nobody can say the hall's neighbor sells measures for money, and that this is the most honest thing he has heard a stranger say, and is therefore the most suspicious.

They say it is never seen to sleep. The channel folk say its lamp at the landing burns at all hours, and that it sits up with it. This is the household of Bren's saying and has not been checked by anyone else.

They say it reads the scales and never touches them, and that this is the one thing about it nobody has argued: it reads a scale and gives the reading and goes on to the next.

## Their own rulers

The Assay-master is said to be honest, humorless and slow, which they take for a compliment. They say he lost an argument to a stranger in front of the yard and did not sulk. They disagree whether that is strength or age.

The Stallreeve is liked. They say he moved four stalls under the hall's roof in the worst of the winter and that no one died in the market. They say he takes every dispute to the gate, and some of them say he has handed the market's justice to a stranger. He says he has handed it a scale.

The Tally-keeper is said to be young for the office. They know she keeps a stick of the gate's readings. They do not know she keeps it in two halves.

The tide-reckoner is said to have called the midwinter water four days ahead, and to have been half a day out on the hour and right on the height. She says she is not a prophet. The town says she can be wrong about a flood that is hours too late and is still the best thing the flats have.

The eleven are said to argue and decide little. This year they voted, seven to three, and the town noticed.

## Their neighbors

The traders from the Aldermark were amused and a little uneasy, and the town noted that they went back up the river with a story. Some in the town say the king will send a reeve again, to take a share of a market that now has a famous gate. Nobody has seen one.

The hill folk of Ruddfold are said to be offended and curious in the same breath. The town's own hill kin say they have been waiting for something like the gate for a generation, and say it very quietly.

The water folk went from the channels at the lowest tides of autumn and of spring. The salt-rakers say they went to the Maun Stair to pay the old god's tide. The channel folk say they went to the sea mouth to meet kin from another water. Neither knows. The water folk came back each time and said nothing.

The southern ships came into the Gulf in summer and did not come to the landing. The town says the ships were afraid of a gate that reads true. The ship-owner from the channels says they were afraid of the Gulf tides. He is nearer the mark.

## Strange things of the year

The blue star dimmed on certain nights, early in the year and in the dead of winter. The town said so in the line at the gate. The old said it was the drowned god turning in its sleep, and that a god sleeping badly is a bad omen for a year of counting. The young said it came with the stranger, and some of the old agreed. It had happened before the stranger came, in other years, and the oldest say so. They are right about that and wrong about the rest: the town has no cause for it, and what it is they have not found.

The clay-diggers and the kiln-masters say the heavy loads are the gritty loads. The heavy diggers say it is the lower pit. Nobody knows. The town trusts the scale for the weight and has not yet decided what the weight means.

Hale the Raker's drum on the bank is still beaten at the flood. A child asked the stranger at the gate whether it had known him. It said nothing and read the next scale. The child said it must have known him. The line agreed.

## Sayings that came up this year

- "Read at the gate" has become a phrase for a thing that has been checked, and a joke for a thing that has not.
- "The stick matches" is said of a bargain that was fairly kept. The Tally-keeper did not start it, but she is blamed for it.
- "Dry channel" is still said of a runner from a debt. It was said more this year, because the line at the gate spent its hours talking about debt and measure.

## What the town does not say

They do not say what the old Weightward died of beyond the chill. The Assay-master has not said what he thinks. Nobody has asked him.

## Year 2: what they say at the close of the year

### The stranger

They still call it the Weigher. Some of the hall's old now say "the one who counts in ones," since it calls in its own ones and the whole yard learned the word in a day. The channel folk still say Bren's weigher, the salt-rakers the Cupman, the hill folk the coat. Nobody has found a better name and some say the town will have to give it one.

What they say it is, beyond last year's stories:
- The channel folk say it is a man who lost his house and found a better one, and that it said yes to the town and not to the hall. They have put it to the children: it asked after the boats at the low tide only because it was lonely.
- The salt-rakers say it knew about the seventh weight before it came, because it asked who held the key, and that a man who asks who holds a key knows what is in the chest. They say this quietly and are wrong: it asked after the key before the chest was opened and asked nothing of what was in it, and the Assay-master himself was the one who said "six."
- The hill kin say it is no stone-town man after all, for it weighed their witness's stones the way it weighed everything, by not being asked, and they say it with a certain rueful respect. They say the hall's weights have been heavy for as long as they have been the hall's and that only a stranger could say it aloud.
- The old of the salt flats say it counts the blue star's nights the way the old Weightward counted the tide. This is a thing the old of the flats say of anyone who sits up at night.

They say it will say yes or no to the seat when the ring has asked it, and that if it says no the hall will be a poorer house, and that if it says yes the eleven will be a poorer ring. They do not agree which. They say it is careful, and the Stallreeve says it is careful in the way of someone who has promised an answer and means to keep the promise.

They say it will not say which clay is good for a kiln, and take this as the one thing about it that is wholly like a man: it said it could not, and said so in front of the diggers. The kiln masters say it is the only one in the matter who did not pretend.

### Their own council and officers

- The Assay-master is said now to be the man who read the fifth wrong and said so. They say he stood at the beam with the stranger over him and did not look at it once, and that when the sum of the four came out he said he had passed every scale in the town heavy for thirty years and did not know it. They say that was either the bravest or the most stubborn thing a man has done in the hall. He does not say. They say he and the stranger argue at the beam like two men who have not yet decided whether they like each other.
- The Stallreeve is said to have told the yard the heavy pieces in the open, which some call honest and some say was a gift to buyers and sellers for a month of quarreling. He says he would rather they heard it from him. They say he went down to the landing alone one evening and that nobody knows why. They are right that he went and wrong about why: they say it was to see about a boat.
- The Tally-keeper is said to cut the day on her stick the way the old keepers cut a death: slow, in notches, and without a face. She is blamed for "the stick matches" and has stopped denying it.
- The eleven are said to have decided to wait, which is what they do, and to have said so in front of the yard, which is not. They say the ring was nine and one and one on the thing it decided, and that the one was the head of the hill goods, who said waiting is what the hall has done for forty years. The hill folk say he was right. The potters say he was right and wrong.
- The head of salt is said to have lost his temper at the weighing and gained a joke. He has been told "read at the gate" a hundred times since. He says he does not mind. Nobody believes him.
- The master of the potters is said to have carried the nest up on her back and been told in front of the ring that her pieces did not agree with themselves. She is said to have fired a second nest in the spring, and the potters' hall is quiet about it.
- The old weigher of the hill kin is said to have been the only man in the ring who did not look at the stranger. They say he looked at the empty seat. He went home before the carriers.
- The tide-reckoner is said to have called the winter water again, four days ahead, and been right in height and a few hours off in the hour. They say she has said nothing of the star.

### The weights, and the seventh seat

This is the great story of the year, and the town does not agree on it.

What they say they saw: that the rack is cut for seven and holds six. That the seventh seat was clean where the others were dusty. That the Assay-master said it plainly in front of everyone. That the stranger called each piece in its ones, each twice the last, but for the fifth, which is light by a hair and has a chip. That four of the hall's pieces do not make the first old one, and are heavy by a twentieth. That the potters' nest of eleven does not agree with itself. That the hill kin's stones agree, within a hair, with the old six.

What they say of the seventh, and each is a guess:
- The old Weightward took it with him, and was laid out with it, and the Assay-master has been too proud to say so. Some of the old say this and others say nobody would bury a weight of that size.
- A boatman stole it and sold it downriver. The channel folk say this, and are offended that anyone says it.
- The old Weightward never had a seventh, and the empty seat is a thing the first keepers cut for the day it would be made. The weavers' master says this and is told it is the weaver's wish to have a bench with nothing wrong in it.
- The hill folk say it was always the largest and never one anyone saw, because the hall dared not weigh with it.
- A few of the salt-rakers say the stranger took it. Nobody can say how, since it was not in the town the year the old man died, and since the stranger said nothing leaves the hall. The salt-rakers say that is exactly what a man would say. This is a thing said at the pans and not in the yard.
- The master of the smiths says nothing and asks everything.

They are all wrong about at least this much: the town does not know why the seat was empty, and the town has said five things about it so far, and the Assay-master has said none.

### Their neighbors

- The hill folk of Ruddfold are said to have the figures of the hall's weights cut on a stick in their hall and to be pleased and offended in the same breath. The town's own hill kin say the hall owes them a twentieth. The head of the hill goods says nothing of the twentieth and counts the room.
- Traders from the Aldermark went home with the story that the hall's pieces were heavy, and the town is ashamed of it and cannot stop telling it. They say a king's reeve will come now to take a share of a market that cannot keep its own weights. Nobody has seen one.
- The southern coaster that came to the landing in the summer is said to have had a master who asked the news and stood in the line like anyone. The town says no more of her, because it does not know what to say. Some say she came to see the gate.
- The water folk went at the lowest tides of autumn and of spring and came back. Bren's household told the stranger what the town says, and the town says the stranger asked because it means to go. It did not say so.

### The strange things of the year

The blue star dimmed again on certain nights in each season, and the town said more of it than last year, because the stranger sits at the landing by the lamp through every such night, awake, and the town has seen it watch. The old still say it is the drowned god turning in its sleep. The young still say it comes with the stranger, and some of the old have come round to it. The channel folk say it is the stranger's lamp seen from a long way. They are all wrong about at least this: the dimming began before the stranger came, as the oldest say, and nobody has found a cause.

The clay is the other thing. The diggers and the kiln masters met at the pits and the stranger said that the lumps that cracked and the lumps that fired well do not look different to its pans, and that it has told them nothing. The diggers say it proves their clay clean. The kiln masters say the cracking is in the drying, or the wind, or the moon of the loading, and some say it is the heavy digger's fault anyway. Orrin has begun firing a small tile of each load before he fills the kiln. They are wrong at least in this: nobody has found why the loads crack, and each of them has a cause that suits him.

### Sayings that came up this year

- "Two pans, level" has become a thing said of a bargain that is fair, and a joke for a thing that is merely quiet.
- "A hair light" is said of anything that is nearly right and not quite, and of a man who has said sorry.
- "Twice the one below" is said of a debt that doubles and of a family.
- "The seventh seat" is said of something everyone knows is missing and nobody names.
- "Read it wrong and said so" is the Assay-master's, and the town has taken it as a compliment, which he did not intend.
- "Wait and weigh" is the eleven's, said by the clay head, and has become what the yard says of the council's whole way of doing things.

### What the town does not say

They do not say what the Assay-master and the stranger said in the hall after the eleven rose. The Assay-master has not said, and nobody asked. They do not say what the three saw in the old keeper's loft, because the three have not told them. They do not say why the old Weightward's seat was clean.

## Year 2 told again, and Year 3: what they say at the close of Year 3

From the fifth day of the first month of Year 3 this section is set aside. The section for the stretch told again, below, and `years/004.md` tell that stretch as it happened, as far as it has been told.

Where this section differs from the sections above, this is what they now say. The Year 2 section above was written before the evening of the weighing was known to the town, before the winter sitting, and before the gatepost. It is what they said then.

### The stranger

They call it the Weigher still, and now the Weightward, since it said yes at the gatepost, and the yard has stopped cheering the word and started using it. The channel folk say Bren's weigher less than they did. The salt-rakers still say the Cupman. The hill folk say the coat, and since the autumn of Year 3 a few say the seventh that walked.

What they say it is, now that it has said it:
- The channel folk say it is a god that came into the town the way a man comes into a house, asking only for a table, and that it ate Bren's bream because it liked it. They have put it to the children. They are right that it asked for nothing and wrong that it needs feeding: it took no gift and said so.
- The salt-rakers say it came to count what the town owes, since it said nothing here is its own and a god that owns nothing can count everything. They stand a little further from the gatepost than they did and say it quietly. They are wrong in this: it takes a pinch and nothing else, and has said it will not read a bargain without being asked.
- The old of the flats say it is the drowned god's child come up the bank to see what the tide has done, and that the tide keeps coming because the town has not paid. They take bread to the Maun Stair at the low tide and leave it. It said it does not know whose child it is. They say that is what a child would say.
- The hill kin say the coat is the seventh that walked: the old weigher said at the gate that the saying in Ruddfold is that the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, and that the hall dared not weigh with it. He said it was a boy's saying and he would not stand behind it. The carriers have gone home with it anyway. They are wrong that anyone knows what the seventh was.
- The potters say it is a gentle one. The master of the potters said it, and the potters do not argue with her.
- The back houses say it could cure and will not. They say a god of scales does not bring a sick child to a pan, and that it said nothing when it was asked. They do not know whether it could, and nobody has asked it again.
- The boatmen say it sees through the top of a basket because it is a god. They also say "I wasn't shown," which is what it says of a hold it cannot see, and they say both without minding. They are wrong in that it reads what is set before it and said so.
- The yard says it can tell when a man is lying. It said it reads a bargain and never a mind, that it will not read one without being asked, and that it knows nothing of a word said outside a bargain. The yard has kept the first half. Some stallholders moved their haggling a few paces from the post, and some moved it closer and say it was struck in front of the Weightward, which is an honor and a joke.
- The line says a god ashore is a landlord and that this one takes barley.
- The Stallreeve says it is the same stranger it was, with a bigger word on it.

They say it is never seen to sleep, as before. They say the lamp at the landing burns, and now they say that the Weightward counts the day's readings over by it.

### Their own council and officers

- The Assay-master is said to have been the first to ask it a plain question and the first to be told the answer, and to have stood at the foot of the ring and said he would not say he believed it and would not say he did not. He is said to have said "I will not be alone with a second," and it has become what the yard says of a secret no one will keep alone. They say he comes to the gate in the mornings to watch it and that he was a little harder on it at the beam for a year. They say he lost his friend and has not said how he feels. They are right in most of it.
- The Stallreeve is said to have known for half a year before the town did, and to have said nothing, which some call honest and some, led by the head of salt, call half a year of lies by silence. He says he told nothing false. They say he walked the stalls earlier that autumn than before. They say that a man who keeps a thing that long should have to say why, and he has not.
- The Tally-keeper is said to be the one it trusts more than the ring. She cut the keys, the boat sticks, the table of the twentieth and the day it took the seat, and the yard says her stick is the town's second hall. She is blamed for "the stick matches" still and has stopped denying it.
- The eleven are said to have decided two things in two years and to be proud of neither. They say the ring let the yard in on the day it was said, nine to two, and that this was the best thing it did. They say the vote on the seat was six and four and that the four were not wrong. The master of the weavers is said to have called a seat for a thing that does not sleep a throne, and the town has not forgotten it.
- The head of salt is said to be the one who asked what the room wanted asked, and to be thanked for it and avoided. He said no bargain in the town is safe from it and was told it had said as much as it would. He is said not to have forgiven the joke of "read at the gate" and to have forgiven a year's worth of "a stick cut once."
- The master of the smiths is said to have asked whether it could be broken, and to have been answered, and to be saying nothing of how. He says the ring's right to put it down is an edge he will hold to.
- The ship owner of the channels is said to have bought a morning and a pinch. They say it read his own boat against him in front of the water and that he stood and said "read the rest." The water says he paid it to read his own boat first for a show. They are wrong about that, and have not stopped saying it. He says it is the best bargain he ever lost.
- The clay head is said to be the one who put bread out for a god and had it put away. The head of reed and fish is said to have called its answer a good broker's. The master of the potters is said to have called it a gentle one. The master of the boatwrights is said to have been for it because his men quarrel less.
- The tide-reckoner is said to have called two winters' water four days ahead and been right in height and a few hours off the hour both times. She says she does not need her feet for an ordinary flood. They say she has said nothing of the star.
- The old weigher of the hill kin is said to have come down a second time in a cart, a year older, to see his stones weighed, and to have said he had come to see agreeing done in front of him. They say he looked at the stranger a long time and said nothing. They are right.

### Their neighbors

- Ruddfold is said to have the saying of the seventh and the figures on a stick, and to be pleased. The town's own hill kin say the hall still owes them a twentieth. The ring said debts stand as made, and the head of the hill goods said nothing and counted.
- Traders of the Aldermark went home twice with the story, that the hall's pieces were found heavy and that the keeper said so, and now that it says it is a god. The town says a king's reeve will come, at last, to ask why a market needs a keeper of its weights, and that this time he will have to be answered. The Stallreeve says he would say what he has always said.
- The southern coaster's master is said to have stood in the line like anyone and asked the Weightward three things and been told it is a god, and to have called it a landlord. The line says she will tell every house on the coast that the town has two weights. The ring has decided, since, that it has one. The town says the south sent no coaster in Year 3 because it is waiting to hear. The ship owner of the channels says it is waiting on the Gulf tides.
- The water folk went at the lowest tides of autumn and of spring, in each year, and came back. The salt-rakers say they went to the Maun Stair to pay the old god's tide, and that they went because a god had come to the gate. The oldest say they went before it came. They are right about that.

### The strange things

The blue star dimmed on more nights this year than the last, in the first month, in the dead of winter and in spring. The old say it is the drowned god turning in its sleep, and now some say the god at the gate is counting it. The young say it comes with the Weightward. The channel folk say it is the Weightward's lamp seen from a long way. The line says the Weightward watches the light and means to learn its habits, and is used to that. They are all wrong in this: the dimming began before it came, as the oldest say, and nobody has found a cause. A boy at the gate asked whether the star is its own. It gave him no answer. He said it was. The line told him to hush.

The clay is the other thing. The diggers and the kiln masters met at the pits twice and the pan said the same twice. The diggers say it proves their clay clean. The kiln masters say the cracking is in the drying, or the wind, or the moon of the loading, and the tiles Orrin fires have not told him anything either, and he fires them for the habit. They are wrong at least in this: each of them has a cause that suits him, and none has found it.

### Sayings that came up in these two years

- "A god ashore is a landlord." Said now of any one who asks nothing and takes something. The coaster master's.
- "Read the rest." The ship owner's. Said of bearing a hard thing to its end.
- "I wasn't shown." What the Weightward says of a hold it cannot see, and what a man says of anything he has not been told.
- "Not alone with a second." The Assay-master's. Said of a secret nobody will keep alone.
- "Weigh and decide." From "I weigh, and they decide." Said of any honest division of labor, and as a joke of a man who gives the work to his neighbor.
- "A pinch for a morning." The ship owner's. Said of a bargain that costs little and is not small.
- "An edge to hold to." The master of the smiths'. Said of the ring's right to put a thing down.
- "The old six." The town's weight, now, by the ring's word, from the first market of the new autumn.
- "A twentieth" is said of any debt the town thinks the hall owes, and of any that the hall thinks the town owes it.
- "A god of scales." Said by the back houses, and not kindly.

### What the town does not say

They do not say what the three saw in the old keeper's loft. The three have told the ring and the ring has said nothing, which in a ring that argues is itself a story. They do not say what the old Weightward did in his last spring. They do not say what the Assay-master and the stranger said in the hall with the door shut, beyond what the Assay-master said before the ring. They do not say whether the seventh was ever a weight. They do not say whose child it is, because it does not know, and has said so.

## Year 3 told again, from the fifth day of the first month: what they say at the fourteenth day of the seventh month

Where this section differs from the Year 3 section above, this is what they say. The Year 3 section above was written before the word was given at the gatepost, and is set aside from that day. This section goes no further than the fourteenth day of the seventh month.

### The Weightward

They call it the Weightward. The names among themselves have not changed: the channel folk say Bren's weigher less than they did, the salt-rakers the Cupman, the hill folk the coat. None of them has been said to it.

What they say of the word it gave at the post:
- The channel folk say it told the south what the hall's pieces are and that this is the first time a market has done so. They say it with a straight face and are proud.
- The salt-rakers say it gave the south the hall's flaws in front of the whole yard. The head of salt says a weight is what it is and a weight is not light against itself. Some of the old say a god that says what the hall does not know is a god that knows what it does not know, which is the rarer thing.
- The hill kin say Ruddfold has not heard a hall say what it does not know of itself, and do not say whether they like it.
- The yard says the Weightward will carry the word south by the ship owner of the open water, and that the south will say the town keeps two weights until the first market of the new autumn, and then one.

They say it asked for a stone and nobody has found it. They say it knew the figure before it asked. They say it asks no one to give up a stone because it does not want to be the one who took it. The channel folk say it is only being thorough. The salt-rakers say it came to count what the town owes and is counting. The hill carriers say the seventh weighs what is owed.

What they say of how it answers:
- Asked whether it can be killed, it said it can be hurt, held or scattered and comes together again. The salt-rakers say scattered like brine on the pans and gathered like salt. The channel folk say torn like a net and mended. The hill folk say hung up like a coat and shaken out. The master of the smiths is said to have heard it across the yard and said nothing, and the yard takes that for a speech.
- Asked what would break it, it did not answer. The yard has settled on three answers for it. None of them is the same.
- Asked whether it can cure a fever, it said it has no power for it. The back houses say it was honest and some of them say that is worse. The old woman who asked has told her street.
- Asked who is lying, it said what it said before the ring, and when asked whether the man it read knows it, it said both pans show and the man knows whether it means its own terms. The young stallholder who asked says it is fair and does not know whether to be glad.
- It has not read a bargain this season and nobody has asked. The head of salt's people keep to their word. The yard has stopped dreading it and started to joke.

They say it runs the cups. At night at the landing, before it counts, it runs its thumbnail down the nest from the smallest, one cup after another. The boatmen say three things of it: that it is saying the cups their names, that it is seeing none has been taken, and that it is counting up to the seventh, since there are seven cups and seven seats. Bren says her mother counted beads that way. "Running the cups" is said now of anyone who counts before he speaks, and as a joke of a man who will not begin. The children do it on their knuckles and are told to stop.

They say it went to the pans. The household that sent the salted baskets turned out every basket unasked and it weighed one it chose itself and called it clean. The town says the household was looked at and came out clean and that this is a better thing than anything it could have said of itself. The old of the flats say it came to count and what it counted was paid. The raking household says it was looked at in daylight. The head of salt says a short measure and a cheat are different things and this is the proof. The master of the weavers is said to have watched the day to see whether it did a thing that was not a weight, and said it weighed a basket.

### Their own council and officers

- The Assay-master is said to have read the fifth light on his own beam on two trials and to be ready to read it light on a third. They say he said the ring may refuse to carry a thing south but not refuse to know it. They say he went to the net-maker's step and put his hand flat on the stone and said what he saw, and that this was the humblest thing he has done in the hall.
- The Stallreeve is said to have given the day at the pans on his own terms, that the household be told and not found, and to have forbidden a wager at the beam and the buying of a stone for its weight. He is said to have brought the ring's answer to the post on the first market after the sitting and to have named which things were refused when it asked, and to have given one reason of the ring's and not made up another. They say he asked it then what it would have cut for the carrier and got no answer, and that he said there was time.
- The Tally-keeper is said to have cut every stone at the landing's table. They say her stick is the town's second hall and that "the stick matches" is said now of a stone as of a bargain. She has stopped denying it.
- The eleven are said to have taken six things on the second stick and refused two. The yard knows the counts. The yard does not know why the fifth and the captains' scale were refused beyond what the head of salt, the master of the weavers and the master of the smiths have said in the open, and one reason the Stallreeve gave. The head of the hill goods is said to have said nothing and counted.
- The master of the smiths is said to have brought a forge slab of five thousand nine hundred and sixty and to have nodded once, as at a good beam, and to have asked it nothing, then or after.
- The master of the boatwrights is said to have brought his yard's door-stone to be read because his men asked whether the seventh lay at their gate.
- The ship owner of the open water is said to be a man who carries what is on his stick, as cut, and no more. He is said to have promised three of its instructions and not a pair. They say of the pair that one the ring has answered, and the other is on no stick.
- The master of the potters is said to have shaped the third nest over the winter, and to have said at the post in the seventh month that it is drying on its boards. She is said to have said it as one says the weather.
- The old weigher of the hill kin came down a second time, in the carrier's cart, and had his stones weighed at the gate on the eighth of the second month. He said what the hill kin tell boys. They say he did not stand behind it, and that he looked at the Weightward a long time and said nothing.

### The door-stone

They say a net-maker of the landing, a woman of about seventy, brought her door-stone to the fish landing on a hand sledge, and that it read three thousand and eleven in the Weightward's ones, sixty-one short of what it said it was looking for. They say the Assay-master looked at it and said it was a door-stone. The yard says the seventh lay at her door a hundred years and the family's feet wore sixty-one off it. The Assay-master says a stone worn by feet is flat where the feet were and this one is round. The yard says that is what he would say.

Four people have offered her a pinch, a pot, a goat and a week's fish for it. She has sold nothing. "Sixty-one short" is said now of anything that is nearly right and not it, and of a man who has almost kept his word. The hill carriers say sixty-one is what is owed, and laugh, and say it twice.

### Their neighbors

- Ruddfold is said to have the saying of the seventh and the figures on a stick, and to have heard from the carriers that the hall says what it does not know. The town's own hill kin say the hall still owes them a twentieth.
- The southern coast is said to be waiting for the word. The ship owner of the channels says it is waiting on the Gulf tides. The line says the ship owner of the open water has the stick and will say it at Corrith, and that the south will have the same story the town has, by autumn.
- The boat people were gone from the channels at the lowest tide of autumn, and came back on the sixteenth. Nobody followed. The old women of the flats took bread along the shore at the lowest tide, as they do, and the Weightward watched them go and did not follow. The salt-rakers say that is a good sign. The oldest say they went before it came, and are right about that.
- No one from the Aldermark was in the town this stretch. The Stallreeve says a reeve of that king may come yet to ask why a market needs a keeper of its weights.

### The strange things

The blue star dimmed on the night of the twenty-second of the first month, on two nights of the fifth month and on the night of the seventh of the seventh month, each for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then was as before. The old say it is the drowned god turning in its sleep. The young say it comes with the Weightward. The channel folk say it is the Weightward's lamp seen from a long way. The line says the Weightward sits at the landing by the lamp through every such night and watches the star and runs the cups, and is used to that. A boy at the gate has asked twice whether the star is its own. It gave him no answer. He says it is. The line tells him to hush, and the post, when it was empty on the fourteenth of the second month, gave him no answer either, which the boy took for a yes.

The clay is the other thing. The diggers and kiln masters have been quiet this stretch. The tiles are fired for the habit.

### Sayings that came up in this stretch

- "Running the cups." Said of a man who counts before he speaks.
- "Sixty-one short." Said of anything nearly right and not it.
- "Three thousand and seventy-two." Said of a stubborn man and of a hope for a bargain.
- "A hair is not a figure." The master of the weavers'. Said of anything offered as a weight that is only a feeling.
- "A weight is not light against itself." The head of salt's. Said of a standard nobody will admit is wrong.
- "Told and not found." The Stallreeve's, of the pans. Said of a thing done in the open.
- "It weighed a basket." The master of the weavers'. Said as a joke of a man who expected more of a day.
- "Looked at and came out clean." The raking household's. Said of anyone who has had to show everything and had nothing to hide.

### What the town does not say

They do not say what the seventh was, since nobody knows. They do not say what the stone at the net-maker's door was before it was a door-stone, since nobody knows. They do not say whose child the Weightward is, because it does not know, and has said so.
