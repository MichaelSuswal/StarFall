Year 4. The tenth month, the first month of summer in this country, the fourteenth day: a market day, first light, the gate not yet open. It is warm and dry, and the wind has stood foul for the open water for some days. The town has come through another winter, with a hard spell and the fever in the back houses, and an ordinary flood after it. Since the first market of the autumn the old six are its weight, and it weighs by eleven new pieces that you called in your pans. You are at the foot of the gatepost with the nest of cups at your hand, and the line is forming in the yard in the grey. Your body is whole. You have not slept, and nothing has been taken from you. No weight came into the air at any time. What follows runs from the kiln yard, in the spring of Year 3, as far as this gate, and stops here.

The weighing green and the kiln-opening stand as you played them, with every figure as you called it, and so do the days after, to the Assay-master's word on the fourth day of the eighth month and your answer to the Stallreeve at the gate on the second. Nothing in them was changed.

Two things in the kiln yard had no figure when you played that day, the first of the eighth month of Year 3, and the pans gave them. You weighed the halves of each broken spare together, then each alone, and said what the pans gave, and how much of each piece is clay and how much is not clay. The Tally-keeper cut what she heard. In your ones:

- The one: 12.3 whole. The halves, 6.2 and 6.1. Not clay, 0.7 in the whole, 0.4 and 0.3 in the halves.
- The two: 24.7 whole. The halves, 14.8 and 9.9. Not clay, 1.3 in the whole, 0.8 and 0.5 in the halves.
- The three: 36.9 whole. The halves, 19.3 and 17.6. Not clay, 2.0 in the whole, 1.0 and 1.0 in the halves.
- The four: 49.3 whole. The halves, 34.3 and 15.0. Not clay, 2.7 in the whole, 1.9 and 0.8 in the halves.
- The six: 73.5 whole. The halves, 40.1 and 33.4. Not clay, 4.0 in the whole, 2.2 and 1.8 in the halves.
- The seven: 86.2 whole. The halves, 52.8 and 33.4. Not clay, 4.7 in the whole, 2.9 and 1.8 in the halves.
- The eight: 98.5 whole. The halves, 65.8 and 32.7. Not clay, 5.3 in the whole, 3.5 and 1.8 in the halves.
- The ten: 122.8 whole. The halves, 71.5 and 51.3. Not clay, 6.7 in the whole, 3.9 and 2.8 in the halves.
- The twelve: 147.7 whole. The halves, 93.6 and 54.1. Not clay, 8.0 in the whole, 5.1 and 2.9 in the halves.
- The fourteen: 172.1 whole. The halves, 87.8 and 84.3. Not clay, 9.3 in the whole, 4.7 and 4.6 in the halves.
- The fifteen: 184.6 whole. The halves, 97.8 and 86.8. Not clay, 10.0 in the whole, 5.3 and 4.7 in the halves.

The halves together give what each piece gave whole. What is not clay is sand and shell, spread alike through the body, with nothing gathered in one half more than the other. It is the reading you had at the pits in the second month of Year 2. It says nothing of which clay is better or of how the kiln did, and you said so.

By the evening of the fourth day the yard had the Assay-master's word from the hall door. Fifty-five whole was a thing each trade accounted for in its own way. From the fifth day the Tally-keeper kept both sticks on her table and read them aloud to anyone who asked, figure by figure, and nothing more. About forty asked in the month. On the night of the sixth day the blue light dimmed, and you saw it from the landing. On the eighth day, a market, a boy at the gate asked whether the star is yours. Your decided words do not answer that. You said nothing, and the line told him to hush. On the ninth day the master of the smiths stood at the Tally-keeper's table, had the fifteens read, nodded once and asked nothing.

The eleventh day was a market. A digger of the lower pit asked whether the stick says his clay is good. You had given words for that, and you said them: you do not say which clay is better, or whether the kiln did well. A kiln-master of the east kilns said his name at the post, Orrin, and asked for the figures of the two sixes and the two twelves, before the fire and after. You gave them from the Tally-keeper's cuts. He worked the loss out aloud, to the line and not to you: a thirteenth off the whole, and not the same thirteenth twice. You read the next scale. Nothing of what he said was cut. The yard has "a thirteenth" by heart.

About the middle of that month the boat people were gone from the channels for some days, as at every lowest tide of spring.

On the sixteenth day of the ninth month the ring sat with the door shut, and you were not sent for. On the seventeenth, at the post, before the yard, the Stallreeve brought what the ring let him bring. The hall's paying for the spares was taken as the hall's cost, seven for, three against, one not voting. A motion that the ring itself cut a second stick for the carrier, of the six things it took in the winter, did not carry: five for, five against, one not voting. He gave one reason, that a stick cut on a tied vote is a word the ring is not agreed on. He asked you nothing. The same morning the head of the hill goods asked you, as himself, whether you would cut the six things for the carrier now. You answered in your words of the second day: nothing more of your own, and the second stick is the ring's to cut or leave.

On the twenty-ninth, a market, the Stallreeve told the ship owner of the open water, before the line and in your hearing, what you would have the carrier told, in your words. The ship owner said that was on his stick and he would say it so, and that the pair of things he would not promise stands as he said it in the first month.

On the third day of the tenth month, a dawn with no market, you read the loads of both coasters at the open-water landing. They went out past the Horn on the tide of the fourth, the carrier with his half of the first stick. No southern coaster came up to the town that summer.

On the eighth day of the eleventh month, a market, an upriver trader who said he was of the Aldermark asked whether one of the hall's old weights is light. You answered in the words you had given for a captain who asks about the fifth.

In the twelfth month the grain came in, and the potters' hall fired sets of pieces for the stalls. On the evening of the nineteenth the coasters came back up the channel. On the twentieth, a market, the ship owner of the open water laid his half-stick on the Tally-keeper's table. She fitted it to hers and said it matches. He gave his account before the yard, to the Stallreeve and to you. He said the town's word at Corrith, on the quay, to the masters of the houses his coasters deal with, as cut and no more, and put no price on it. One asked what the Weightward is, and he said the Weightward of the hall. One asked whether a southern scale may be read against the hall's, and he said a scale is read at the gate, like any. He found no hull and no man of the house of the master who stood at this post two summers before. Her house has another port, along that coast, where his coasters do not go, so the word was not put to her house by name. The council's people at Corrith did not ask him for it. Nobody gave him an answer, a stick or a token to carry back. You said nothing.

That is Year 3 to its end. Seen from the landing, the blue light dimmed on six nights of that year: one in the first month, a pair in the fifth, a pair in the seventh and one in the eighth. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the light stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. The boat people left the channels at the lowest tides twice in the year, in the second month and the eighth.

Year 4 opened with the ring's day. On the second day of the first month, a market, the Assay-master opened the chest before the yard, read the ring's word, and asked you to call the eleven new pieces in your pans so that the market has a figure for each. You called them: 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 84, 96, 120, 144, 168 and 180, each to a hair. The Tally-keeper cut them. From that market the old six are the town's weight and the eleven are what the market weighs by. The hall's ten fired pieces stand on the hall's bench and serve only to reckon bargains made before that day.

An old man of the flats asked whether you go, now the weights are set. You gave your standing answer: until you have seen the town's weights set right, and past that you do not know.

The line was twice its length all that month. Most stallholders' pieces read about a twentieth heavy, being copies of the hall's old pieces. A few read level, and a pair were worse and were filed down. A pair of bargains made before the day came to the gate, and the Stallreeve ruled both by the ten, as made. You read the scales and said nothing of the bargains. From about the ninth day to the thirteenth the boat people were gone from the channels.

In the second month the hill carriers came down. Their scale read level. The old weigher of the hill kin did not come. The head carrier said at the gate that Ruddfold has the ring's decision, and that the old man had seen agreeing done and would not come down a third time. On the night of the fifteenth the blue light dimmed. On the seventeenth a boy asked again whether the star is yours, and you said nothing.

The third month was rain. The kilns cracked loads as before. The blue light dimmed on the night of the twenty-second.

It dimmed again on the night of the ninth day of the fourth month. On the twentieth a pair of netmaker households asked you to stand witness to the lease of a weir. You stood, and said what you saw and heard. You used no power on it.

The fifth month brought a hard spell, and fever in the back houses. On the eighth day, a market, the Stallreeve gave out the tide-reckoner's word. A netmaker, the one who asked in the spring of Year 3, asked again whether you can tell the height of the water as she does. You had given words for that on the played day, and you said them. The Alder crested on the twelfth at the height she gave. On the twenty-third the woman of the back houses who asked across the ring in Year 2 came to the gate. She said who she was and that she had not come since, and asked nothing. You said what you had decided for her: no, you have no power for it, and you are sorry about her child.

In the sixth month the ring sat on the fifteenth with the door shut. On the seventeenth the Stallreeve said at the post that it had set no word for you.

Then the thaw, and an ordinary flood.

In the eighth month the boat people were gone from the twelfth day to the sixteenth. The blue light dimmed on the night of the nineteenth. On the twentieth a girl of about six asked, in the boy's words, whether the star is yours. You said nothing. The boy has been put to a boatwright at the far channels for the season.

In the ninth month the second year of the seat came round. The line talked of a year to the term, and the Stallreeve said the term is three years and a day. The ring sat on the fifteenth with the door shut. On the seventeenth he said at the post that it had spoken of the term, had set no day to speak of it again but its first sitting after the grain, and asked nothing of you.

On the evening of the twelfth day of the tenth month a southern coaster came up the channel and tied up at the town's landing. The boatmen knew her: the hull that lay three days at the town in the tenth month of Year 2, with the same master. On the thirteenth, a dawn with no market, Bren told you at the fish landing that she was in, and that her master had asked the landing who sits in the Weightward's seat now and which is the town's weight, and had told the landing nothing. The Stallreeve said he had a pair of the watch on the gate as always and would put no more. You did not go to her boat. The ship owner of the open water's coasters lay loaded at his landing, waiting on a wind.

Today, before light, she came up from the landing in the dark and stood at the head of the line.

You are at the gatepost, at first light, with the gate not yet open. The line is forming in the yard in the grey. The watch stand at the bar with their spears grounded. You are at the foot of the post with the nest of cups at your hand. The Stallreeve is at the gate and the Tally-keeper is at her table. The master of the southern coaster stands at the head of the line, a brass balance in a leather case under her arm, with one of her crew a pace behind her. She is the woman of about forty who stood here in the tenth month of Year 2, had her ship's balance read level, and named a price you could not pay for the town: which is the town's weight. She has not given her name, her house or her port. No weight is in the air. She has said nothing yet in your hearing.

(oog) Where the story stands against the clock. The clock has run on through Year 5, and the story stands in the summer of Year 4, on the fourteenth day of the tenth month. A year is told only as far as its first held moment. Both scenes you prepared for the spring of Year 3 are played, so the night took your thread from the evening of the fourth day of the eighth month of Year 3 to that year's end and on into your Year 4, which had never been told, and stopped it at the first moment held for you, this gate. So your own Year 5, and your town's, has not begun, and none of it is told. The world beyond the town has gone on through Year 5, and none of that has reached you.

(oog) Some plain things.

One question asked of you is not answered, and it is the one I will put to you first on the next played day: "Is the star yours?" It has been asked five times, by a boy at the gate in the first month of Year 3, by the same boy of the empty post in the second month, again on the eighth day of the eighth month of Year 3 and the seventeenth day of the second month of Year 4, and by a girl of about six on the twentieth day of the eighth month of Year 4. No decided words of yours answer it, so each time it met silence. Nothing is read into that. "What would break you" was refused by your own word and is not waiting.

Answered in the stretch by words you had already given, as told above: the digger, the kiln-master's figures, the head of the hill goods, the upriver trader on the fifth, the old man of the flats, the netmaker on the height of the water, and the woman of the back houses. Your words on the height of the water stand as you gave them, the line on what the tide-reckoner's telling costs her included: her cost is common talk at the gate, and you have heard it there through four winters.

What you asked the Stallreeve to have the carrier told, he was told. The ring cut nothing more.

Your word down the coast, as you now know it from the carrier's own account at the post: said at Corrith on the quay, as cut, in the tenth month of Year 3. It was not put to the coaster master's house by name, and no answer was given him. Nothing has come to you from anyone outside the town.

A ruling, for the figures of clay and not clay in the broken spares. It is a reading of the ruling of Year 2 and no widening of it: of a fired piece you know its weight, and how much of it is the fired clay and how much is not, sand, grit, shell. It tells nothing of how the piece fired, how it will wear, or what the fire took. What was not clay before the fire is not clay after it. This is on your card as a dated entry.

For the record: in the stretch you read no bargain and no person, set no seal and gave no licence. Each scale you tried and found level is a true scale for as long as it holds.

The sky. Year 3 is now told whole, and the count for it is six nights, as above. Of Year 4 you have seen the blue light dim on the nights of the fifteenth of the second month, the twenty-second of the third, the ninth of the fourth and the nineteenth of the eighth. That year is not told to its end, so I give no count for it.

Your map did not change. You went nowhere you had not been.

No painting this morning. No painting is approved for this run, so the request is held and nothing was painted.

(oog) The clock is closed after this night.
