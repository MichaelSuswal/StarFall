## Year 2 again: what was played, as the town saw it

### The first month: the evening of the weighing
- In the hall, with the door shut, the Assay-master asked what it is. It told him: it is a god. The rest of what it told him, and its answers to his three questions, are in its decisions file, item 1, in its own words. He alone heard it.
- What he said back: that he would not say he believed it and would not say he did not; that he had nothing to set against the word and had six calls his beam could not fault; that it had denied taking the seventh before anyone asked. Of the seventh he said: "Twice the sixth, by the run of them, if the old man's set ran true to the top." That is his own reckoning from the six figures.
- It asked him to keep it between them until he had decided what the ring should hear. He would not: "I held one thing for a year because I did not know what it meant, and today the head of reed and fish asked me before the ten of them whether I was alone when I found it. I was. I will not be alone with a second." He said he would say nothing false and nothing in the yard, that the Stallreeve would hear it that night, and that he would ask for a sitting where it is said: by the stranger first if it comes, he adding only that it was said to him in the hall on the evening of the weighing and that he asked.
- At its asking he sent for the Stallreeve and locked the rack in the chest with his own key, with the Stallreeve standing over him. The key on the peg was not touched. The empty seat was left as it was, unwiped. The stranger touched nothing in the chest.
- The stranger told the Stallreeve itself, at the hall door, with the Assay-master beside it: its decisions file, item 2. The Stallreeve said: "A year at my gatepost for a pinch of barley. Well." He said the gate is kept as it was and the watch stands where it stood, and that he says nothing in the yard: "A yard that hears that word on a market morning stops buying fish." He said he would carry the Assay-master's asking for a sitting round that night. Of the stranger's asking to say it standing at the foot of the ring with the yard let in, he said that is the ring's door and he would put it to them as it was said. He asked it nothing about what else there is to it.
- It also gave a word for the old weigher at Ruddfold, to go by the next carrier up the old sunken road: its decisions file, item 8.

### What came of the asking for a sitting (settled; not yours to change)
The Stallreeve carried the asking round that night. He named no matter, since the thing was the stranger's to say first, and the Assay-master would not name it either. The ring had sat a whole day, had just set when the weight comes back, and had the loft to look in; nobody would call a sitting that week for a matter no one would name. It went to the ring's winter sitting. From the evening of the weighing to that sitting the word stayed with the Assay-master and the Stallreeve. Neither told anyone, and neither was alone with it. How each of them carried it through the autumn and the first of winter, and how each looked at the stranger at the gate, is yours.

### The fourth month: the landing
- The ship owner of the channels made his offer as your Year 2 file has it. The stranger refused "my landing and no other" and put terms back, which he took, before Bren and a pair of boatmen: its decisions file, item 3. He said: "I came down here to buy a year of you, and you have sold me a morning and kept the rest." And when the fee was made a pinch: "A pinch. I offered you a measure a boat and you beat me down to a pinch. Every man on this water will know by morning what I asked and what I got, so I had better be seen to like it." They shook hands on it.
- It was set before the Stallreeve the same evening. He added: the gate first, and no reading on a market morning anywhere but the gatepost; a landing is its owner's ground and its line is the owner's to keep, not the watch's. Of the split sticks for boats he said: "That's her knife and her mornings. Ask her at the gate, the pair of you, and ask her what it does to her season's count."
- Yours: what the Tally-keeper answers when the stranger and the ship owner ask her at the gate (item 9 has the stranger's terms for such sticks; if she says no, it leaves it). What the other owners of boats do, and whether any asks for a dawn at a landing of his own. What the water says of it.
- From then on, at dawn on days with no market, the stranger reads boats at his landing on the terms spoken: the captain sets the load out sack by sack or basket by basket, on the landing's beam or its own, and it calls weight, count, and how much is the stuff it is sold as. What it is not shown, it says it was not shown. It takes a pinch from the captain, weighed.

### What the dawn readings found (settled)
Through the winter the loads read as loads do: a little short here, a little over there, salt with a thirtieth part of sand in it, nothing a captain would be shamed by. One did not. Early in the sixth month a boat of the ship owner's own came in from the salt flats with raked salt in baskets, and basket after basket the stranger called about five parts salt and one part not salt, sand and grit, under a clean top. It called each basket as it read, before the captain, the ship owner's landing man and whoever stood on the landing, and said nothing of who or how, as it never does. What your people can find out, if they ask: the baskets came so from one raking household on the flats that had a thin season; the captain bought by the basket and did not turn one out; the ship owner did not know. What the ship owner, the captain, the rakers and the water do about it is yours.

### The sixth month: the ring's winter sitting (the outcomes marked settled are not yours to change)
- It falls in the middle of the sixth month, after the salt and before the boat sale.
- Settled: the Stallreeve puts the stranger's asking to the eleven, and the ring lets the yard in. The door stands open and the yard is let into the hall as far as it will hold, the rest at the door. How the eleven argued it, and who was against, is yours.
- The stranger comes and says it first, standing at the foot of the ring: that it is a god, and what it told the Assay-master, as its decisions file gives in items 1 and 6. It tells them the terms it gave for the weighing: it weighs, and they decide. Of the seat it says: "I do not ask to be Weightward and I do not refuse it yet." It says it will answer for the seat when the loft has been looked in and the seventh has been looked for. If the Assay-master wants it said, it says before the ring that it did not take the seventh, never touched the chest, and did not know the rack was cut for seven until he lifted it out.
- The Assay-master adds only that it was said to him in the hall on the evening of the weighing, and that he asked.
- Settled: someone of the ring or the yard asks it outright whether it can tell when a man is lying. Who asks, and how, is yours. Its answer is item 7 of its decisions. In substance it says: it will not say no. When a bargain is struck in front of it, and it chooses to read that bargain, it knows of each party one thing, yes or no: whether, at the moment of agreeing, that party meant to keep the terms as spoken. Not why, not what the lie is, not what the party means to do instead. It reads a bargain and never a mind, and it knows nothing of a word spoken outside a bargain. It has not used it on anyone in the town, and it will not use it on anyone in the town without their asking. Write that as said; add nothing to it.
- Any other question about a particular power is answered yes or no, or it says it will not answer, and its decisions give no more than that. So: where a mortal asks plainly whether it can do a particular thing, write the question in your report under `## Asked of the god`, marked `for the Keeper: yes, no, or no answer`, and write on from there both ways, briefly. A question that names no particular thing ("what else are you?") gets the same words it gave the Assay-master: that there is more, and that it answers yes or no about a particular power.
- Yours: everything the eleven and the yard say and do on hearing it, at the sitting and after. One of the eleven may be away, as your files have it. The fixed points below are where the town has to arrive.

### The ninth month: the gatepost
- Fixed, as your Year 2 file has it: the council sat after the sowing; six for, four against, one away on the Gulf; the Assay-master spoke for it; the Stallreeve carried the offer to the gatepost with the half-stick, in the words your file has.
- New, and fixed: the eleven voted knowing what it had said in the sixth month. Why six of them still wanted it in the seat, with a term, the Assay-master's hand on the beam and their own right to put the seat down, and why four did not, is yours.
- The stranger first read the scale it had in its hand: heavy on the buying side by about one part in thirty. It said so to the stallholder.
- Then it took up the half-stick and said yes before the yard. Its words are its decisions file, item 4, whole. It said first that if anyone in the ring had not heard it from its own mouth, it said it now: it is a god. To the yard, last: "I am the Weightward, for a term. I am not their god, and nothing here is mine."
- The Tally-keeper fitted her half to its half and said the terms back in its words. The Stallreeve said, loud enough for the line: "The eleven asked, and the Weigher has said yes on the eleven's terms. The rest it has said in front of you, and I'll carry it up as it was said. The key is taken in the ring, before the ring, and not before." The Assay-master said: "Expect to be argued with at the beam."
- Yours: what the yard did when it had said it; how the eleven took the terms it added; when the ring next sits and the key comes off the peg before it. The stranger takes the key only in the ring, before the ring, and puts it back when the term ends. The chest is opened only before a pair of the eleven and never by it alone.

### The tenth month: the coaster's master at the gate (settled; this is what the line could see and hear)
The stranger took her balance from her hand like anyone's, said nothing first, and read it against its brass pan by pan. It read level and it said so. It took a pinch of barley. Then it asked her three things, quietly, so that the yard had to lean to hear, and told her why it asked: it is keeper of the hall's standards for a term, and would know whether what the hall keeps can be read by anyone who comes up the channel.
- "What do the southern harbors read their scales against, Master?" She said: "Each house against its own. A house's weights are the house's."
- "Is there a measure in Corrith that the other harbor towns will take, or does each house keep its own?" She said: "The council in Corrith keeps a set for quarrels, and a house takes it when it must. Nobody swears it is what it was."
- "And what would a southern captain want to know about this town's weights before she signed to buy in them?" She said: "The thing your yard cannot tell me. Which is the town's weight: the old stones or the clay. Tell me that and I will tell every house on the coast, and that is a fair trade." It gave her no answer to that. She waited, and then nodded as at a price refused.
- She asked who it works for. It said it is the Weightward of the hall for three years and a day, and the rest as its decisions file gives, item 5.
- She said: "They say at the landing that you stood at this post and said you were a god. Did you?" It told her yes: it is a god. She looked at it a while and said: "Where I come from we say a god ashore is a landlord. You take barley." She did not say more of it.
- She asked whether it would read a cargo at a quay of her coast if a house paid its passage. It said it does not leave the gate, and that her harbor may bring its scales here or send its own to be set against the hall's.
- She asked what the town means to do about its weights. It said that is the town's, and she is a guest at the gate.
- She offered no gift. She thanked it for the reading, took her balance, and went down to the landing. She gave no name and was not asked for one.
Her coaster stayed three more days and went down the channel. Give her no other words to the stranger. Her talk with anyone else in the town stays the ordinary talk of trade. What your people make of the exchange is yours.
