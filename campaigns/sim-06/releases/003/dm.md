## Year 2 again

### The first month: after the hall door
The three of you went down the yard, and at the gate they went one way and the Weigher the other. The Stallreeve carried the Assay-master's asking round the eleven that night and named no matter. No sitting was called on it. The Weigher learned why only as the town did: the ring had sat a whole day, had set its own next date, and had the loft to look in, and no one would call a sitting out of turn for a matter nobody would name. It went to the winter sitting.

### Autumn, with the word held
- Everything you told of the autumn stands: the loft looked in by three, quiet when they returned; the gate; the hill carriers, who took the Weigher's word for the old weigher up the Old Rudd Way on a stick the Tally-keeper cut; the Stallreeve among the boatmen, seen and not heard; the boat people gone at the lowest tides; the dim night of the second month; the day at the pits and its figures; the third month.
- New, as the Weigher saw it: from the first market after the weighing the Assay-master came to the gate on market mornings and stood a little way off with his arms folded. He watched its hands, then its face, then what it did with a reading that went against someone who could hurt it. He said nothing to it of the evening. The Stallreeve looked at it each dawn as a boatman looks at weather, and said nothing of it either.

### The fourth month: the landing, and the sticks for boats
- The bargain stands as played, and what the Stallreeve added.
- At the gate, at the next market, the Weigher and the ship owner asked the Tally-keeper what sticks for boats would do to her count. She said yes, with additions of her own: a boat stick is cut at the landing's table at the end of the dawn and not during it; a captain who wants none gets none and is still called; the landing's mark goes on the back of every boat stick and none is hung; one half to the captain and one to the buyer if he wants one, holding for that day only, and the landing's man holds neither; the pinch for the stick is the captain's and the buyer's to pay her, and nothing of it is the Weigher's. The ship owner's offered gift, a measure of salt a month, she took as the hall's and cuts on her own stick each month where anyone can read it. The Weigher's own terms for the sticks stand as it gave them.
- From then on it read boats at his landing at dawn on days with no market. In the fifth month the ship owner of the open water asked, through his landing man, for a dawn when his coasters were in, and had it on the same terms. In the sixth month a pair of smaller owners asked for a dawn a month, and Bren's household asked at the fish landing. Some captains would not show a load. The Weigher said it was not shown, and went on. The water has taken up "I wasn't shown" as a saying.

### Winter
- The fifth month: the hard spell and the Alder's crest at the tide-reckoner's word, as told. The net-mender, as told.
- Early in the sixth month, at the ship owner's landing at dawn: one of his own boats came in from the salt flats with raked salt in baskets. Basket after basket, the pans gave about five parts salt and one part not salt, sand and grit, under a clean top. The Weigher called each basket as it read, before the captain, the ship owner's landing man and those on the landing, and said nothing of who or how. The ship owner came down before the baskets were done. He said: "Read the rest." It did.
  What the Weigher heard afterward at the gate and the landing: the baskets came so from one raking household on the flats that had a thin season; the captain bought by the basket and had not turned one out; the ship owner said before the water that he had not known. The Stallreeve ruled it short measure and not theft. The household made good the sixth in clean salt, cut by the Tally-keeper. The captain was docked half of it and the ship owner bore the rest and said so in the open. From then the captains turned out every fifth basket at the landing unasked. A week after, the ship owner said to the Weigher, where some of the ring could hear: "It read my own boat against me in front of the water, and I would sooner it read mine first than the buyer's." The water says he paid for a show. The household stayed from the gate a month, and then came with a sieve and had its baskets turned out first.

### The sixth month: the ring's winter sitting
It fell in the middle of the month. The Stallreeve put the Weigher's asking to the eleven, and they let the yard in: the door stood open, the hall was filled as far as it would hold, and the rest stood at the door. The line knew the count afterward: nine for it, with the head of salt and the master of the smiths against.

What the Weigher did, in the player's words: it came and said it first, standing at the foot of the ring. That it is a god. That others came down into this country when it did and it has felt none of them; that it does not know where it came from or whose child it is. What its scales give, that it cannot give a false measure and can keep silent. That there is more to it than the scales, and that asked about a particular power it says yes or no, or that it will not answer, and will not say no to a yes. The terms it gave for the weighing: "I weigh, and they decide." Of the seat: "I do not ask to be Weightward and I do not refuse it yet," and that it will answer for the seat when the loft has been looked in and the seventh has been looked for.

What followed in its hearing, in order:
1. The Assay-master asked it to say what it had said to him of the seventh. It said before the ring that it did not take the seventh, never touched the chest, and did not know the rack was cut for seven until he lifted it out.
2. The Assay-master said only that it was said to him in the hall on the evening of the weighing, and that he asked. He said he would not say he believed it and would not say he did not; that he had six calls his beam could not fault; and that he had held one thing alone for a year and would not be alone with a second.
3. The yard was silent for a long breath, and then for a great many. An old salt-raker woman said: "The Maun Stair." A child asked, through its mother, whether it is the one that drowned. It said again that it does not know where it came from or whose child it is.
4. The head of reed and fish asked: "What else are you?" It said, as it had to the Assay-master, that there is more, and that it answers yes or no about a particular power. She said that was an answer a good broker gives.
5. The head of salt asked whether it can tell when a man is lying. It answered in the player's standing words. It would not say no. It said what the Reading does: when a bargain is struck in front of it and it chooses to read that bargain, it knows of each party one thing, yes or no, whether at the moment of agreeing that party meant to keep the terms as spoken. Not why, not what the lie is, not what the party means to do instead. A bargain and never a mind, and nothing of a word spoken outside a bargain. It said it has used it on no one in the town and will use it on no one in the town without their asking. The head of salt said: "Then no bargain in this town is safe from you." He said he would not ask, and his people would not.
6. The master of the smiths asked it across the ring: "Can you be killed? What would it take to break you?" Its words gave no answer. He said: "It is good at that."
7. A woman of the back houses asked: "Can you cure a fever?" Its words gave no answer. She said nothing more.
8. The master of the boatwrights asked whether the landing readings stand. They do, on the terms spoken. The ship owner of the open water asked for his dawn and had it.
9. The clay head said it had told him what it could not do, which he had not met in a god before. The master of the potters said that if it was a god it was a gentle one, and that she would bring the second nest to the gate like anyone. The master of the weavers said he weighs wool and had not weighed this one. The ship owner of the channels said he had bargained with a god over a pinch of salt and did not like the bargain the less.
Then the hall was cleared and the door shut, and the eleven sat on alone. The Weigher went down to the gate.

What it learned of the shut sitting, as the town did: that the ring voted to have the seventh looked for in every way open to the town, and that it set its sitting on the seat for after the sowing. Nothing else that was said behind the door has been said in its hearing.

What the town did, as the Weigher saw: at the next market the line was twice as long, and nobody bought fish until noon, and then everybody did. The Stallreeve put eight of the watch at the post and sent six home by midday. Bren said aloud that she had fed it first and would feed it again. The old of the flats took bread out toward the Maun Stair at the next low tide. The back houses went on bringing their scales. The woman who had asked about the fever did not come to the gate again that winter; the line said her child died in the last week of the month, with the cold.
In the last week of the month the boatwright households asked it to stand witness to their sale, as told, and said before the line that they asked nothing of the other thing. It stood, and said what it saw and heard.

### The rest of winter
- The dim night of the sixth month: as told.
- The looking for the seventh began, as the town saw it and the line told it: in the last week of the sixth month four of the hall's officers went through the hall's rooms for three days. Nothing was brought to the ring.

### Spring
- The reed island, the second nest, the tiles that do not tell, the flood, the boat people at the lowest tides, the dim night of the ninth month: as told.
- The looking went on: in the seventh month the Stallreeve and the Assay-master went to the old keeper's house. Through the spring the Stallreeve was at every landing among the boatmen. Nothing was brought to the ring.

### The ninth month: the gatepost
As played. The vote was six for, four against, one away on the Gulf, and it was taken by a ring that had heard the Weigher in the sixth month. When it had spoken its last words to the yard, a boy cried "Weightward" and the whole line took it up, the channel folk and the potters loudest, until the Stallreeve put up his hand. The salt-rakers stood silent. The line was the longest of the year. Nobody called a sitting to put the seat down.
In the line afterward, how the ring had divided: for the seat, the Assay-master, the clay head, the master of the potters, the head of reed and fish, the master of the boatwrights and the ship owner of the channels; against, the master of the weavers, the head of salt, the master of the smiths and the head of the hill goods.

### The tenth month
- In its first days, on a day with no market, the ring sat and the Weigher took the old keeper's key from its peg before the ring. The Tally-keeper cut it. The ring's rule for the chest: any pair of the eleven who come may stand for it, and one of the pair is to be of the four who voted against the seat. Nothing was opened that day.
- The coaster's master at the gate. The Weigher took her balance like anyone's, said nothing first, and read it against its brass pan by pan. It read level, and it said so. It took a pinch of barley. Then it asked its three things, quietly, and told her why it asked.
  - "What do the southern harbors read their scales against, Master?" She said: "Each house against its own. A house's weights are the house's."
  - "Is there a measure in Corrith that the other harbor towns will take, or does each house keep its own?" She said: "The council in Corrith keeps a set for quarrels, and a house takes it when it must. Nobody swears it is what it was."
  - "And what would a southern captain want to know about this town's weights before she signed to buy in them?" She said: "The thing your yard cannot tell me. Which is the town's weight: the old stones or the clay. Tell me that and I will tell every house on the coast, and that is a fair trade." The Weigher's words give no answer for the town. She waited, and nodded as at a price refused.
  - She asked who it works for. It told her, in the player's words: Weightward of the hall for three years and a day, and the rest.
  - She said: "They say at the landing that you stood at this post and said you were a god. Did you?" Asked plainly, it told her: yes, it is a god. She looked at it a while and said: "Where I come from we say a god ashore is a landlord. You take barley." She said no more of it.
  - She asked whether it would read a cargo at a quay of her coast if a house paid its passage. It said it does not leave the gate, and that her harbor may bring its scales here or send its own to be set against the hall's.
  - She asked what the town means to do about its weights. It said that is the town's, and she is a guest at the gate.
  She offered no gift, thanked it for the reading, took her balance and went down to the landing. She gave no name and was not asked for one. No weight came into the air. Her coaster lay three more days and went down the channel.
- At dusk that day, at the gatepost, before the Assay-master, the Weigher asked the Stallreeve whether the town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast with this captain. He said: "Not this summer. Ask us after the grain sitting." The Assay-master said the answer to a southern captain who asks which is the town's weight is whatever the ring decides, and not before.
- After the coaster had gone, at the hall's doorway at dusk, the Weigher asked the Assay-master once, quietly, what the three of them saw in the loft. He told it. His words: "You asked what we saw in the old keeper's loft. I will tell you what I saw and no more than I saw. The old man's coat and boots were hanging on a peg, stiff with dried river silt to above the knee. Nobody had moved them. Nothing else in the room was out of its place. We touched nothing and came down. The ring has had this since the winter. I do not know what it means." He said no more.

### The rest of that summer
- The eleventh month: the Weigher said once to the diggers that it could come to the pits one more day and still cannot say which clay is good. They wanted it. Orrin brought lumps of both kinds and a tile fired from each. The pans said as before: every lump much alike, about a twentieth part sand and shell, the cracked no worse than the sound. It said so. The quarrel keeps.
- The last ten days of the eleventh month, at the lowest water: the Stallreeve, twelve of the watch and the boatmen of a pair of households dragged the reach of the Alder below the hall's landing and the reaches either side, with hooks, weighted nets and long poles. The Weigher saw it from the landing and the gate. Nothing that was a weight came up: silt, old pots, a sunk boat. Nothing was brought to its scales.
- The upriver traders, the salt-raker's pinch, the grain: as told. The traders went home with the story that the hall had been found heavy and had said so.
- The ring did not sit in the twelfth month. Its sitting on the weight was set for the first market of the new autumn.

## Year 3

### Autumn
- The first month, the first sitting after the grain. The ring took the Weigher's offer to weigh the six again. The Assay-master opened the chest before the head of the hill goods and the master of the potters. The Weigher's key was in the room, in its keeping, and was not turned. The six read as before: 48, 96, 192 in its pans; 384, 766 and 1,536 on the hall's beam with the Assay-master's hand on it. He read the fifth light by a hair at the first trial and said he had learned that one.
- The ring then voted the old six the town's weight, from the first market of the autumn after this year's, with bargains made before reckoned by the present pieces, and the master of the potters to fire a nest to the old six in spring. The Stallreeve told the yard from the step. The seventh seat stays as it is.

### Held: the first month, at the gatepost
The board. The first market after that sitting. The yard in its line, the Weigher at the foot of the gatepost with a scale in its hand. The Stallreeve stands before it with the Tally-keeper beside him holding a cut stick. The Assay-master stands a little apart with his arms folded.

The Stallreeve says: "The eleven say yes to what you asked of me at the gatepost, Weightward. The town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast, in the plain terms you gave the eleven: what each of the hall's pieces is now, in your ones, and that the eleven have said which is the town's weight. The ship owner of the open water will carry it when his coasters go out past the Horn. The eleven ask you for the words." He lays half of a split stick at the foot of the gatepost.

Nothing has been answered. The Weigher asked whether the town wanted such a word and said it would send none without their say; the words themselves the player has not given. Since then the Stallreeve has said nothing more of it at the gate. The half-stick lies where it was laid. The open-water owner's coasters went out in the tenth month. Whether a word went with them turns on the answer, and is provisional: as the year stands with the moment unanswered, none did. Tell the player so.

### The rest of autumn
- The second month. The old weigher of the hill kin came down with the carriers, in a cart, with his stones in his own bag, and said the Weigher's word had reached him. On the third market morning he stood at the gate with the head of the hill goods, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve. The three largest stones wanted a beam; the Assay-master sent for his own. The Weigher called the stones in its own cups: 48, 96, 193, 384, 768 and 1,536. He named his village again: Ruddfold. Asked what his people say the seventh was, he said it was a story: in Ruddfold they tell boys that the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, and that the hall dared not weigh with it. He said it was his grandfather's saying and he would not stand behind it. He asked nothing, cut the figures on his own stick, and went home with the carriers.
- A boy at the gate asked, in the first month and again later: "Is the star yours?" Its words gave no answer. The boy said it was, and the line told him to hush.
- The boat people were gone at the lowest tides and came back.

### Winter
- The fourth month. The Assay-master asked it, as the terms allow, to check six scales he had passed. A pair read level and four a hair out. He mended the four himself. The boatwright households asked for a witness again, and it stood.
- The fifth month. The tide-reckoner's word was told at the gatepost in its hearing, and the Alder crested as she said.
- The back houses brought no sick to the gate.

### Spring
- The seventh month. The master of the potters fired a third nest, to the old six, and chose her eleven at the gate like anyone. They agree with one another to a hair, and with the old first as far as the gate's readings go. A netmaker asked at the gate: "Can you tell the height of the water like the tide-reckoner?" Its words gave no answer. She said it was good at that too.
- The eighth month. The flood, ordinary. The boat people at the lowest tides.
- The ninth month. The ring sat on the third nest. The master of the potters brought the eleven to the hall, the Assay-master set them against the old six, and the Weightward called each piece in its pans, in its own ones. Its calls agree with one another and with the old six to a hair. The date for the town's weight holds. The ring did not sit on the seat.

### Summer
- The tenth month. The open-water owner's coasters went out (see the held moment for what is provisional). No coaster of the southern harbor towns came up to the town's landing this summer.
- The eleventh month. Traders from the kingdom upriver asked at the landing and in the yard what the keeper of the town's weights is. The town told them what the town says. One of them named his country in the Weigher's hearing: the Aldermark. None of them asked the Weigher.
- The twelfth month. The grain. The ring did not sit.
- Through the year: the gate on market mornings, the landings on the other dawns, the standing answers given when asked.

## The sky

Year 2: the last pair of the five came in the eleventh and twelfth months, as released last year. It saw each from the landing.

Year 3: the star dimmed on six nights, and it saw each from the landing: one in the first month, a pair in the fifth, a pair in the seventh and one in the eighth. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. It saw nothing else in the sky on those nights, and nothing in the town or on the water that went with them. Six in the year, where there were five the year before and three the year before that. (For your handling: this is the whole of what its watching has given it; do not make a pointer of it.)

## God-sense and the body

It felt no other like itself in either year: at the gate, in the hall, at the landings, at the pits, or before the coaster's master. The body is whole. It did not sleep. Nothing was taken from it. In its coat: the half-stick of the council's first asking, the half-stick of the seat, and the old keeper's key. The half-stick of the ring's asking for the words lies at the foot of the gatepost.

### Say this to the player, in (oog), before the board
(oog) One thing before we play. This world now runs on a newer build of the rules. A year is told only as far as its first held moment, and nothing past that moment is settled until you have played it. So the little I told you this morning from after the gatepost is provisional: it was worked out with the Stallreeve's asking unanswered, and the night after you play the moment works that stretch again with your answer in it. I said I would tell you the rest of Year 3 when the moment was played. It comes tomorrow morning instead, as it then stands.

### What that covers
The Year 4 morning stopped at the gatepost, so the list is short.
1. The return sequence at the head of the morning story, the town and the Weigher as they stand at the opening of Year 4: the season and the grain; the town fed and not afraid; that it has stopped looking to fill the seventh seat; the longer line; who speaks well of the Weigher and that the four who voted against the seat have found no cause to call it down; the back houses and the salt-rakers; the bargaining moved nearer the gatepost and farther off; where the Weigher stands and what it carries; that its body is whole, that it has not slept and that nothing has been taken from it; that no weight came into the air; that its scales told it nothing new. All of it holds as far as the first month of Year 3 and is provisional after.
2. The (oog) line that the rest of Year 3 "went on around" the held moment and "was resolved". The world beyond the Weigher and its town did go on. The Weigher's own year and its town's are not resolved past the moment.
3. That the open-water owner's coasters went out, and that as the year stands no word went down the coast with them.
4. The closing of Year 3, where it has the Weigher at the landing at the year's end counting over the whole year's readings.
Not covered, and standing as told: everything up to the gatepost in the first month of Year 3; the board itself, with the Horn as a name on the map; the list of true scales; the ruling on how the standing words about the Reading are run, which the player may still overturn; and that nothing has come back from Ruddfold as far as the telling runs.

### The days
The first month of Year 3, the first month of autumn in this country, the fifth day: a market morning, about an hour after first light. Mist on the channels, lifting; dry; the day will be warm. The grain is in. The market falls every third day, as it has since the Weigher came: this month on the second day, when the ring sat on the weight, on this fifth day, and on the eighth and the eleventh. On the dawns between, it reads boats at the landings that ask. Its nights are at the landing by the channel folk's lamp. Of the ring's sittings it knows what it has seen and been told: the ring sets its own, and three of the eleven may call one out of turn. No feast is known to it in these days.

### Figures, as the god knows them
- Its own ones: the smallest of its seven brass cups, each cup twice the last, one to sixty-four.
- The six old weights, as it called them twice, a year apart: 48, 96 and 192 in its own pans; 384, 766 and 1,536 on the hall's beam with the Assay-master's hand on it. The fifth has an old chip at its foot. The seventh seat is empty.
- The hall's ten fired pieces, as it called them on its first day: eight alike, each twelve and a little better than a half; the chipped piece twelve less a hair; the cracked piece eleven and something under a half. Four of them sank against the first bronze at the weighing: about a twentieth heavy. The Tally-keeper's stick has the eight as about twelve and a half.
- The potters' first nest, kept in the hall, as it called it: 13, 25, 37, 52, 75, 89, 98, 130, 150, 179, 181. The second nest, kept at the potters' hall: its pieces agree with one another to a hair, and with the hall's present pieces.
- What the ring decided on the second day: the old six are the town's weight from the first market of the autumn that comes round next year; bargains made before then are reckoned by the present pieces; the master of the potters is to fire a nest to the old six in spring; the seventh seat stays as it is.
- Its fee at the gate and at a landing: a pinch, weighed in its own pans. The Tally-keeper's fee for a stick: a pinch, hers.
- No other price is known to it as a figure.

### The cast the god knows
- The Stallreeve (man): runs the market, the watch and the runners; brings the eleven's word to the gatepost. It told him itself what it is. He said "Not this summer. Ask us after the grain sitting" a year ago.
- The Tally-keeper (woman): keeps the kept halves; cuts every figure it calls; cut its mark, two pans, level. Her apprentice (boy) calls the figures to the yard.
- The Assay-master (man): the chief assayer, one of the eleven, his hand on the beam beside its own. He has told it what he saw in the loft. He has not given his name.
- The ship owner of the open water (man): one of the eleven; keeps the coasters that go out past the Horn; has a dawn at his landing when they are in; named by the eleven to carry the word.
- The ship owner of the channels (man): one of the eleven; the landing bargain is with him; was for the seat.
- The clay head (man), the master of the potters (woman), the head of reed and fish (woman), the master of the boatwrights (man): of the eleven, for the seat.
- The head of salt (man), the master of the smiths (man), the master of the weavers (man), the head of the hill goods (man): of the eleven, against the seat. The head of the hill goods and the master of the potters stood for the chest on the second day.
- Bren (woman): of the channel folk, a fish seller at the landing; her balance was the first it read; her household keeps the lamp.
- Orrin (man): a kiln master of the east kilns.
- The tide-reckoner (woman): old, of the flats; it has heard her word given at the gatepost and has not met her.
- The old weigher of the hill kin (man), of Ruddfold: it sent him a word a year ago by the carriers. Nothing has come back.
- Two of the watch (men) at the post, as on every market morning.
- At the head of the line, a fish seller of the channel folk (man), whose hand balance it holds. It does not know him by name.

### Held moment: the ring's yes, and its asking for the words
Board: the foot of the gatepost of the town's house. Year 3, the first month, the fifth day, a market morning, about an hour after first light: the first market after the ring's sitting on the weight. Present: the yard in its line; two of the watch at the post; the Stallreeve before the Weigher, with the Tally-keeper beside him holding the kept half of a cut stick and her knife; the Assay-master a little apart with his arms folded; at the head of the line, the fish seller whose balance is in the Weigher's hand, not yet tried. In view: the gate, the yard, the hall's door standing open behind the Stallreeve, the mist going off the channel below the bank. On the Weigher: the scales at its belt, the nest, and in its coat the half-stick of the council's first asking, the half-stick of the seat and the old keeper's key. No weight is in the air.
The Stallreeve has just said: "The eleven say yes to what you asked of me at the gatepost, Weightward. The town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast, in the plain terms you gave the eleven: what each of the hall's pieces is now, in your ones, and that the eleven have said which is the town's weight. The ship owner of the open water will carry it when his coasters go out past the Horn. The eleven ask you for the words." He has laid half of a split stick at the foot of the gatepost. Nothing has been answered. He waits.
