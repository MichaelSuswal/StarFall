## Year 3 again, from the gatepost to the potters' door
### Autumn, the first to the third month
- After the fifth day the line ran as before. The yard had heard the word given and mended and was proud and ashamed of it together.
- Every night at the landing the Weigher ran its thumbnail down the cups, smallest first, and then counted. The boatmen saw.
- From the sixth day it asked at its landing dawns for large stones. They came, a stone or two at most dawns: anchors, ballast, querns, door-stones, hearth stones. It weighed each with the owner watching and said what it was. The Tally-keeper cut a stick for every one, unasked. The Stallreeve forbade wagers at the beam. The figures are in the people's report, in a table.
- The eighth day: the fish seller came back with a mended cord. His balance read level.
- The night of the twenty-second day the star dimmed, as on the earlier nights, seen from the landing. The next morning a boy at the gate asked, "Is the star yours?" No decided words answer it. The line told him to hush.
- The second month. The hill carriers came down, and the old weigher of the hill kin with them. On the eighth day his six stones were weighed at the gate before the head of the hill goods, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve, the three largest on the Assay-master's own beam brought down: 48, 96, 193, 384, 768 and 1,536. Asked what his people say the seventh was, he gave a saying of his grandfather's, that the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, and would not stand behind it. He went home with the figures on his own stick.
- The eleventh day: a young carrier asked whether it can be killed. It answered in the player's words. He asked what would break it. It did not answer.
- The same day it asked the Stallreeve for the fourteenth, the lowest tide. He gave it, on his own terms: the household would be told the night before, and not come upon.
- The fourteenth day, a market day: the Weigher walked out to the Flats, 1.6 miles over the marsh, and was back before dusk. The post stood empty with a pair of the watch at it. Nobody was read and no notch was cut. At the pans the raking household turned out every basket before it was asked. The basket the Weigher chose and weighed was clean salt, and nothing it was shown there was bulked. The head of the household put a pinch in its pan. Told to it after: a boy asked the empty post whether the star was its own, and the watch had no answer to give.
- About those days the boat people were gone from the channels, as at every lowest tide of autumn, and came back.
- The third month: rain. The potters' hall's own beam came to the gate and read level. A young stallholder asked whether it can tell who is lying, and then whether a man it read would know. Both were answered by its decided words.

### Winter, the fourth to the sixth month
- The eighteenth day of the fourth month, at the fish landing: an old net-maker's door-stone read 3,011. The Weigher's own reckoning for the seventh, which the landings have by heart, is 3,072. It said what the stone read and nothing more. She asked whether it was the seventh and whether it wanted her step for the hall; it said only what the stone is, and that it asks no one to give anything up. It told the Stallreeve and the Assay-master first, and the Stallreeve carried the figure to the eleven that dusk. The Assay-master went to look and said, in the Weigher's hearing afterward, that the stone was never cut. Four people tried to buy it. The Stallreeve forbade buying a stone at a landing for its weight. The yard says "sixty-one short". No other stone of the thirty read within a twentieth of its figure.
- The twentieth day: the Assay-master asked it to check six scales he had passed. A pair read level and four a hair out.
- The twenty-fourth day: a pair of boatwright households asked it to stand witness to the lease of a boatyard. It stood, and said what it saw and heard. No power was used.
- The fifth month: a hard spell, and fever in the back houses, fewer than the year before. An old woman of the back houses asked at the gate whether it can cure a fever, for her daughter's boy. It answered in the player's words. The woman who asked at the winter sitting of Year 2 has not come to the gate. The star dimmed on a pair of nights this month, seen from the landing.
- The fifteenth day of the sixth month: the ring's winter sitting, the door shut. The Weigher was not sent for.
- The seventeenth day, at the post, before the yard: the Stallreeve brought what the eleven said to the second stick, thing by thing, and the Tally-keeper cut it.
  - Taken as the town's word: that the rack is cut for seven and the seventh is not in it, that the hall does not know what it weighed and has looked for it; the four fired pieces against the first bronze and the sum, about a twentieth heavy; the chipped and the cracked piece against the eight; that the ten have not been weighed against the six since, and that the Tally-keeper has the figures; that the hall keeps no weight that anyone may not see weighed; and that the word go in the order said, with nothing added.
  - Not taken: that the fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so; and that any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read.
  - He gave the counts, which are in the people's report, and one reason, for the captain's scale: a scale is read at the gate, like any, and the hall is for the town's. For the fifth he was given no reason and would not make one up. The Assay-master said at the post that the fifth is light by a hair on his own beam and that he would read it so again.
  - Then he asked the Weigher, as himself: what would it have cut for the carrier now, since the things the ring took are not on the stick the carrier holds? Its decided words do not answer that. It met silence. Nothing more has been cut. He has not asked again.
  - The ship owner of the open water, asked in the open, said it changes nothing he carries.
- The master of the smiths brought a hearth slab to the beam in the third month and asked the Weigher nothing, then or since.

### Spring, the seventh month to the fourteenth day
- The thaw. The Alder is rising to an ordinary flood.
- The second day: a netmaker at the gate asked, "Can you tell the height of the water as the tide-reckoner does?" No decided words answer it. He said it was good at that too.
- The fifth day: the master of the potters said at the post that the third nest is shaped and drying on its boards.
- The night of the seventh day the star dimmed, seen from the landing.
- The fourteenth day, a market day: the Weigher read the line through the morning. When the last scale was read it walked to the potters' hall with the Assay-master at its left hand and the Tally-keeper behind, carrying a long stick. The line watched them go. They came to the door. The story stops there.

### The days
The seventh month of Year 3, the first month of spring in this country, the fourteenth day: a market day, early afternoon, the gate done. Mild. The Alder is up over the lowest paths. The market falls every third day, as it has since the Weigher came: next on the seventeenth and the twentieth. On the dawns between it reads boats at the landings that ask. Its nights are at the landing by the channel folk's lamp. The ring has set no sitting that the Weigher has been told of. The coasters go out in the first days of the tenth month. No feast is known to it in these days.
