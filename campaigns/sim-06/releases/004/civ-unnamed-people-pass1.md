## What your people meet, by month
- The weather, all through. An ordinary autumn, dry until the third month. Winter wet, with its hard spells. The flood at the thaw ordinary in height. The seventh month mild.
- Month 1, from the fifth day. The Weightward keeps the gate on market mornings (the market falls every third day: the fifth, the eighth, the eleventh and so on, without a break across the months) and reads boats at the landings on the other dawns. From now it asks the ship owners and captains at its landing dawns, in the words of item 8, to bring any large stone they own to the landing's beam. Settled: they bring them. A stone or two comes to the beam at most landing dawns through the stretch, about thirty in all: anchor stones, ballast, querns, door-stones, hearth stones. It weighs each with the owner watching and says only what it is. They read as working stones do, anywhere from about 800 to about 6,000 of its ones, most between 1,500 and 4,000. Who brings what, and what the landings say of the asking, are yours.
- Month 2. The hill carriers come down, as every autumn. The boat people are gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides of autumn. The lowest tide falls on the fourteenth day, a market day. That is the day the Weightward asks the Stallreeve for, in the words of item 9: to walk out to the salt pans, the watch keeping the line, nobody read, no notch cut. Whether he gives it is yours; he is your officer and it is his market. If he gives it: from the town to the near edge of the Flats is 1.6 miles over the marsh, and it is there and back within the day. It looks at the pans of the raking household whose baskets were found bulked in the sixth month of Year 2, and it weighs a basket there. Settled: the basket it weighs is clean salt, and nothing it is shown at those pans this season is bulked. What the household says and does when the Weightward walks onto its pans is yours. If he does not give it, it does not go, and says nothing further of it.
- While it is away from the gate, and at any time: a question put to it that its decided words do not answer meets silence and stays pending. Anything asked at the gatepost on the fourteenth day of the second month is asked of an empty post; the watch has no answer to give for it.
- Month 4. Settled: among the stones brought to the beam this month is a door-stone that reads 3,011 of its ones. Its own reckoning for the seventh, which it has said aloud at the landings, is 3,072. It says what the stone reads and nothing else of it. By its decision, item 8, it tells the Stallreeve and the Assay-master first, and the ring second. The stone is an undressed stone that has lain at a door; whose door, how long, and what the owner says of it are yours. What the Stallreeve, the Assay-master, the ring and the yard make of a door-stone that reads sixty-one short of the Weightward's figure is yours. It asks no one to give it up. No other stone brought in the stretch reads within a twentieth of its figure.
- Month 6. The ring's winter sitting, by its custom. The second stick comes before the eleven there. Settled, thing by thing, as the eleven voted:
  - Taken as the town's word: the seventh seat, that the hall does not know what the seventh weighed and has looked for it; the four fired pieces against the first bronze and the sum, about a twentieth heavy; the chipped and the cracked piece against the eight; that the ten have not been weighed against the six since, and that the Tally-keeper has the figures; that the hall keeps no weight that anyone may not see weighed; and the asking that the word go in the order said, with nothing added.
  - Not taken: that the fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so; and that any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read.
  - The counts, who spoke for and against each, and what was said, are yours. The Assay-master speaks for each thing that is a plain figure; that is fixed in his temper. Whether the yard is let in, and whether the Weightward is called to the sitting, are yours. Settled: the master of the smiths does not ask it again in this stretch, at the gate or in session, and the woman of the back houses does not come to the gate.
  - The Stallreeve brings what the eleven said to the post, as he said he would, at the first market after the sitting, and says which things were not taken, since the Weightward asked to hear which. He does not give the eleven's reasons unless they gave him leave to; that is yours.
  - What is then done about a second word for the carrier is not settled by anything the Weightward has decided: it said only that it wants to hear which things are refused, "so that the next word that goes is not the one that is cut short". If the Stallreeve or the eleven ask it what is now to be cut for the carrier, that asking meets silence and stays pending. Nothing more is cut in this stretch. The coasters do not go until the tenth month, and nobody is pressed.
- Month 7. The thaw and the flood. The master of the potters has shaped the third nest over the winter, to be fired to the old six, and it stands drying on its boards in the potters' hall. On the fourteenth day, a market day, when the gate is done, the Weightward walks to the potters' hall, and the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper go with it. Stop there, at the door. What it asks her, what she answers, the weighing and the firing are not yours to write.

## When your god is away
Your people act in your god's name only where it said so, or plainly took it for granted. Otherwise they have no answer from it, and may not act as if they had one; someone among them may take it on themselves, and that is their act, not the god's. A question put to your god while it is away meets silence and stays pending, unless its standing words or conduct profile answer it. Your people know only that they were not answered.

## The Weightward's conduct in this stretch
- The gate on market mornings, the landings on the other dawns, its standing answers, its nights at the landing by the channel folk's lamp. At night there it runs its thumbnail down the cups of its nest in order, smallest first, before it counts the day's readings over. It does this every night it sits there. Anyone at the landing at night may see it; what the boatmen make of it is yours.
- It reads no bargain and no person. It sets no seal. It gives no licence.
- Asked whether it can tell who is lying, it says what it said before the ring. Asked further about that, it now has words: that both pans show, and that anyone it reads knows whether it means its own terms (item 11).
- Asked at the gate by anyone whether it can be killed: "No. I can be hurt, held or scattered, and I come together again." Asked what would break it, it does not answer, unless the ring asks in session. Asked at the gate whether it can cure a fever: "No. I have no power for it."
- Sort everything else a mortal brings to it as `engine/rules/encounters.md` section 6 gives, and say which in your report: answered by its decided words; met with silence and pending; or held. Hold any offer, demand or bargain that would bind it or change its footing, anyone of note from outside the town who seeks it out, and anything you are unsure of: write the moment it reaches the Weightward, in the mortal's own words, and stop that thread there.
