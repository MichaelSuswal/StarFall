## The end of Year 1: what was played, as the town saw it
- At the gatepost, before the yard, the stranger took up the half-stick and said yes: it will weigh the hall's old weights before the eleven, at the first market after the grain is in, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. It spoke its terms to the yard, and the Stallreeve carried them up. They are in its decisions file, item 2, in its own words, with two more it added for the eleven: the weighing is done in the hall and nothing leaves the hall; every figure is cut, and where its reading and the Assay-master's differ, both are cut.
- It asked the Stallreeve where the old weights are kept and who holds the key. He said it is the eleven's to tell and that he would put it to them.
- It named a mark for the Tally-keeper: two pans, level, nothing else. She cut it at the head of her stick of the gate's readings.
- It said to the yard that it has not said yes to being Weightward and will say after the weighing, not before.
- Of the bonded man taken at the landing in the winter: it said it will not claim a price from a bonded man, and asked the Tally-keeper to cut the price as remitted if his master's scale reads level at the gate within the month. If the scale is not brought, or reads out, the price lies unclaimed as before. She said she would cut it as said and that the council would hear of it.
- The same day it read a stallholder's scale out, the selling pan heavy by about one part in forty in her own favor, and said so aloud. She took up her cord in the yard and it read level on the second trial.
- It gave its standing answers where the line could hear: how long it stays, whose child it is, the fee (salt will do, a pinch of any of the four), and that a stallholder may say a scale was read on a day and what the reading was, and may not say it is true. They are in its decisions file, items 9 and 10.
- At the next market it asked the Stallreeve's leave, and the Tally-keeper with him, for split reading-sticks: one stick for a scale, the day and the reading cut on it, one half the owner's and one hers, holding for that day and no longer, lapsing if the scale is not read again within a season, her fee a pinch from the owner and nothing to the stranger. The Stallreeve gave leave on his own rule: no stick is hung on a stall or a scale, and if one hangs they all come down. The Tally-keeper took it on with her own addition: the end cut at a season, and a notch for each market a scale is not brought. Five sticks were cut that day. No mark of the stranger's is on them.
- It thanked the Tally-keeper and asked her to change nothing.
- At dusk of that market day, when the bench closed, it went up to the hall door with its brass.

## Year 2: the weighing
The day: the first market after the grain is in, in the first month. The place: the hall, before the eleven in their ring. The stranger comes as it promised and does exactly what its decisions file gives, items 1 to 3, and no more.
- The pieces. The sealed note says what is brought out, what each piece is, and what the stranger calls for each. Read it before you write the day. What the note says the hall saw on the day may go in your public files. What it marks as not for public files stays out of them.
- How it weighs. A piece small enough for its pans is weighed in its pans. A piece too heavy for them goes on the hall's own beam with the Assay-master's hand on it, the stranger standing over it with its scales in its hand, and it calls that piece the same way. It calls each figure aloud, in its own ones. It says what each piece is now. It names no piece the town's weight, guesses nothing, and says nothing of how any piece came to be as it is.
- The Assay-master's side, the Tally-keeper's cutting, the ring of eleven, the yard outside, the witness from the hill kin: yours. An old weigher of the hill kin did come down the sunken road ahead of the carriers, with a bag of his own stone weights, at the hill-goods head's asking, and is in the town for the day. Who he is and what he does is yours.
- The potters' new nest. The stranger asked the master of the potters to bring it to the hall. Whether the eleven want it weighed that day is theirs. If they do, the stranger weighs it the same way; the sealed note gives what it calls.
- What the eleven decide when the weights are down is theirs, and so yours. They decide which is the town's weight, or fail to.

- A request, plea or question from the town that its decided words do not answer: it meets silence, your people react as they would, and you list it under `## Asked of the god`.
- Held. Three things are held if they come, and anything else you are unsure of. For each, write the moment it reaches the stranger, in the mortal's own words, with who is present and what has just happened, and stop that thread there. Give the stranger no answer and no gesture that is one.

  2. The Assay-master asking, after the weighing, what the stranger is or anything else it told him he might ask "after". It gave him its word that it would tell him. It does not tell him this year.
  3. Any offer, demand or bargain that would bind the stranger or change its footing in the town.
- For the rest of the year after a held moment: the town goes on, and that thread does not move. Those who asked wait, as a town waits on a council that decides little. Do not have anyone act as if an answer had been given either way. If you judge that your people would not wait out the year, say so in your report under `## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper` and write the year as if they waited.

## Year 2: the day at the diggers' pits
After the weighing, not before, the stranger goes for one day to the diggers' pits on the clay banks, in the town's own ground, out and back within the day. Before it goes it asks the Stallreeve whether the watch will keep the line and the Tally-keeper keep her sticks; what they answer is yours, and it goes only if the line is kept. Which day is yours to set with Orrin.
- What it does there is item 12 and item 11 of its decisions: it weighs what the diggers bring up, in front of the diggers and the kiln-masters, and says what the pan says. It asked for three lumps from loads that fired well and three from loads that cracked; whether they are brought, and by whom, is yours.
- What the pan says, settled by the Keeper: of each lump it calls the weight, and how much of the lump is clay and how much is not (sand, grit, shell, root). Every lump it is given reads much alike: a little sand and shell in each, about a twentieth part, a hair more or less from lump to lump. The lumps from loads that cracked carry no more of it than the lumps from loads that fired well. So, in its own stated words, the two sets do not look different, it has told them nothing about the cracking, and it says so. It cannot tell one clay from another clay, and it does not say which clay is good for a kiln.
- What the diggers, the kiln-masters and the potters make of that, and do about it, is yours.

## Year 2: the world, as your people met it
- The weather. An ordinary year. The grain came in well enough. The autumn catch was ordinary. Winter was wet with its hard spells. The spring flood was ordinary in height. Summer was warm.
- The hill carriers came down the old sunken road in the middle of autumn with ore and charcoal, as every year, some weeks after the weighing.
- The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides of autumn, in the second month, and again at the lowest tides of spring, in the eighth, and came back.
- Boats from the kingdom upriver came down in summer to trade, as most summers. No one came from its king.
- In the tenth month, the first of summer, a coaster of the southern harbor towns came up the main channel to the town's own landing, as happens some summers and did not happen last year. Its master is a woman of about forty. She traded at the landing for some days, asked the news of the town as such masters do, and on a market day she joined the line at the gate. That thread is held: write what the town saw of her and her crew up to the moment she stands at the head of the line in front of the stranger, and stop it there. Write nothing she says to the stranger and nothing it says to her. Her own words anywhere in the town are the Keeper's; give her none beyond the ordinary talk of trade.
- The star dimmed on certain nights this year, as it has in other years, and on more nights than last year: the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. It came in the second month, in the dead of winter, in spring, and late in the summer. Anyone awake under a clear sky saw it. What your people make of such nights is theirs. Give no count of them in anything you write; the Keeper has the count.
- No sickness beyond the ordinary, no flood loss, no raid.

## What is yours to decide, and what is not
Yours: everything your people do, think, say and feel, every mortal among them, how each office holder meets the stranger and whether each takes its word, their work, stores, births and deaths, quarrels and stories. Mortals have their own will. They do not know the stranger's plans; they meet what it does, as it does it.
Not yours:
- An outcome that is uncertain and contested. Do not settle it. Write it in your report as an attempt: who tries what, when, what success and failure would each mean, and every circumstance from your record that bears on it. The Keeper gives the odds and rolls. Write the rest of your year both ways where it turns on that outcome, briefly.
- What the stranger's scales know. The Keeper has settled every reading this year needs: the pieces in the hall (the sealed note), the clay (above), the master's scale (above). Any other scale brought to the gate reads as an ordinary scale of your people would: most a little out, some level. The stranger says what it finds.
- Anything about another people or about land beyond what your knowledge file gives. Give no distance or travel time that is not in your files.
- The name of your people or their home, and the name of any office holder your record leaves unnamed: these stay unnamed unless a moment in your year has a mortal say a name aloud in the stranger's hearing, and then you write who said it, when and to whom.
- Why the old Weightward did what he did, or anything else your people do not know. You are not told it and you do not reason toward it. Each mortal says only what that mortal knows, when asked or when moved to, by the temper your people files fix.
