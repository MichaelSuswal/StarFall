## The end of Year 1

### The talk at the hall door, at dusk
The Assay-master stood in the hall's doorway with a lamp behind him and did not ask it in. He said the door was the eleven's until they set the day, and that whatever it had to say it could say there. The yard had gone to its supper. It said its five things to him, in order, in the player's words.
1. On why the eleven should take a stranger's scale. He said it was the first time a seller had told him not to buy, and that he would carry the weighing as an afternoon's work, worth what his beam showed. He said: "Then say that to the eleven on the day, in those words." Its words gave no answer. He said, "Then I'll say it, and I'll say it was said to me."
2. On whether it is more than a man with good brass: "Yes", and the rest after the weighing. He was silent a long time and did not ask what. He said, "After the weighing, then," and that he would hold it to the word "after".
3. On who sent it. He said, "That you know of," and that the clause was the careful part. He asked, "Where did you learn to weigh, then, if no one sent you?" Its words gave no answer. He said, "That is a no or a not yet, and I have had both from you."
4. On why it asked how the old Weightward died. He said nothing for a breath, then that it had been a year this spring and he had nothing to tell yet, and that he would tell it when he knew what there was to tell.
5. On what it wants of the hall after. He answered stiffly that he was not a keeper and had a beam, that he would not have that said in the ring or at the gate while the old man's seat was a year cold, and that he owed it nothing for the saying. He said it should expect to be argued with at the beam. He asked, "You asked the Stallreeve who holds the key and where the weights are kept. Why?" Its words gave no answer. He said the eleven would answer the Stallreeve what the Stallreeve had asked, and plainly.
He went in and shut the door, and left the lamp burning on the step.

### The rest of the month, and the twelfth
- The Stallreeve heard from the Weigher that his rule on the sticks is a good one. He said the notch made it a rule.
- The master of the potters, asked to bring the new nest to the hall and not to the gate, said she would carry it up herself.
- Bren's household, asked quietly and once what they would be willing to say about the low-tide trips: Bren answered for them, with the others standing by. They would say what the town says: that the water folk go out at the lowest tides and come back; that the salt-rakers say it is to the Maun Stair and the channel folk say it is to meet kin; and that none of them has ever followed or knows. They would say it again to anyone who asked as quietly, and would tell the Stallreeve first if the question came from anyone else.
- In the third week of the twelfth month the bonded man's master brought his scale to the gate, mended. The Weigher tried it and it read level. The Tally-keeper cut the price as remitted. He said it was a cheap scale in the first place.
- The eleven's answer on the old weights came by the Stallreeve: they are kept in a chest in the hall, in the room under the old keeper's loft; the Assay-master holds the second key; the old keeper's own key hangs in the ring, by the custom that a dead keeper's key hangs until a new keeper takes it.
- Before the harvest the eleven took the Weigher's terms whole, as the hall's own rules for the day. The Stallreeve brought the word: eight to three.

## The weighing: the first month of Year 2

The first market after the grain was in. The hall, the eleven in their ring, the door open, the Stallreeve at the door with the watch holding the yard back. The Tally-keeper sat at the bench's end with a long stick, and her apprentice called each figure to the yard once it was cut. The master of the potters had the new nest in a reed basket. An old weigher of the hill kin sat beside the head of the hill goods with a bag of his own stones. The gate was not kept that day and no notch was cut against any scale for it.

What the Weigher saw and did, in order:
1. The Assay-master read the three terms aloud as the hall's rules for the day. He opened the chest with his own key before the ring. The key on the peg was not turned.
2. He lifted a wooden rack out onto a cloth. The rack is cut for seven weights. It held six. The seat for the largest was empty, and clean where the other seats were dusty. He said aloud that he had found it so when he first opened the chest after the laying out, and twice since, each time the same; that he had told the Stallreeve the evening before that the rack was not full, and no one else. The head of reed and fish asked whether he had opened it alone. He said alone, both times. The ring was silent.
3. The three smallest pieces are old bronze. The Weigher weighed each in its own pans and called it: 48, 96, 192, in its own ones. The Assay-master checked each on his beam against the one below; his readings were cut beside its calls. No difference.
4. The three largest are cut stone and too heavy for its pans. Each went on the hall's beam with the Assay-master's hand on the beam, the Weigher standing over it with its scales in its hand, and it called each the same way: 384, 766, 1,536. On the fifth the Assay-master first read it level against two of the fourth. Both were cut. He asked a second trial, read it light by a hair, and said aloud: "I read it level. I read it wrong. It is light." Both of his readings stand on the stick beside the one call. The fifth has an old chip at its foot, which everyone at the beam could see. The Weigher said nothing of it.
   What The True Measure gave it of the six: plain old work in bronze and stone, each reading as any weight reads; nothing in them that does not read true.
5. The Assay-master set four of the hall's small fired pieces against the first bronze, as he said the old keeper's practice was. The hall's side went down. He asked the Tally-keeper what the Weigher had called his eight agreeing pieces at the door a year ago; she read it from her stick: about twelve and a half each. The head of the hill goods did the sum aloud: fifty against forty-eight, about a twentieth. The Assay-master said he had tested every scale in the town for thirty years against those pieces, and that if they were heavy every scale he passed was passed heavy, and that he had not known it. The Weigher was not asked to say the sum and did not.
6. The master of the smiths asked it across the ring: "Weigher, where is the seventh? What did it weigh? Can you find it?" Its words gave no answer. The ring looked at the Assay-master, who said the stranger weighs what is put in front of it and there was nothing there. The master of the smiths said, "It is good at that."
7. At midday the clay head put out bread and a jar of water. The Weigher said, in its own words, that it takes no fee and no gift for the day. The bread was put away.
8. The Assay-master asked, "What should the fifth be?" It said, in its own words, that it tells the eleven what each piece is now and the eleven decide which is the town's weight.
9. The ring had the new nest weighed, since it was there. Eleven unmarked pieces, called smallest first: 13, 25, 37, 52, 75, 89, 98, 130, 150, 179, 181. The Assay-master's beam agreed with every call. The nest does not agree with itself, and sits heavy against the old weights as the hall's pieces do. The master of the potters said they were fired in one kiln so they would shrink alike. She asked that the nest be left in the hall under the Assay-master, and it was.
10. The old weigher of the hill kin set his own stones on the hall's beam against the six. His stones agreed with the old weights to the beam's tremble at most seats. He said the hill kin had supposed the hall's pieces heavy for as long as the hall had had them, and had said nothing because a market is a market. He named his village in the ring: Ruddfold. He asked the Weigher to weigh his stones before the ring. Its words gave no answer. The Assay-master said the hall's day was the hall's. The old man cut the figures on a stick of his own as they were called, and no one stopped him.
11. The ring argued through the afternoon. The head of the hill goods moved that the old six be the town's weight and the hall's pieces be made again to them: it failed, with a pair of votes for. The head of salt moved that the hall's pieces stand and the old six be kept as a thing of the hall's past: it failed, with a pair of votes for. He said: "A twentieth, on every sack that has gone out of this town for forty years." The clay head moved a third, and it carried, nine to one: no weight is named the town's until the seventh is found or the ring has given it up, and the nest has been fired again; the market stands on the hall's present pieces meanwhile; every debt and bargain made before the day is reckoned by them as made; nothing in the hall is filed, mended, replaced or carried out; the chest is opened only before two of the eleven; and the matter comes back at the first sitting after the next grain. Then, without a count, the ring agreed that the old keeper's loft, which custom keeps shut until a new keeper takes the seat, be looked in by three.
12. The clay head said the seat should be offered then. The Assay-master said a seat is given when what it keeps is whole. The ring did not vote on it.
13. From the step the Stallreeve told the yard in the open what the ring had done and what the pieces had shown. When the Weigher came out a boy cried, "Weigher, will you be our Weightward now?" It gave no answer.
14. At dusk the eleven rose. The Assay-master stayed in the hall with the Weigher, and the door was shut.

## Held, first: the evening of the weighing, in the hall

The board. The first month of Year 2, the evening of the weighing. The hall, the door shut, one lamp lit. The eleven have gone and the yard has gone to its supper. The six old weights lie on the cloth with the rack beside them, the seventh seat empty. The potters' nest is by the wall in its basket. The Weigher has its scales and its nest on it. No one else is in the room.

The Assay-master says: "It is after. You told me at this door there was more than a man in you and that you would tell me what I asked after. I am asking. What are you?"

Nothing has been answered. The Weigher gave him its word that it would tell him and would not lie to him about it.

## The rest of autumn

- On the third day after the weighing three went up the old keeper's loft stair: the Assay-master, the Stallreeve, and the clay head as witness. They came down within the hour, quiet. The Weigher was at the gate and was not with them. Nothing of what they saw has been said in its hearing, and the ring has been told nothing that the town has heard.
- The gate went on as decided. The standing answers were given when asked: how long it stays, whose child it is, the fee, what a stallholder may say of a reading.
- In the middle of the second month the hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way with ore and charcoal. They had the figures of the hall's weights from the old weigher's mouth before they came. One asked in jest whether it comes from Hask and had the Weigher's answer. Their own scale read a little out in their favor and they mended it at the stalls.
- Some days after the carriers' market, in the evening, the Stallreeve came down to the landing alone. The Weigher was by the channel folk's lamp. It saw him go among the boatmen, his old company, and speak with some of them apart. It did not hear what was asked or what was answered. He went back up alone.
- The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides of autumn, in the second month, and came back.

### The day at the diggers' pits
The Weigher asked the Stallreeve and the Tally-keeper first. He said the watch would keep the line and nobody would be read; she said she would cut no notch against any scale for a day the gate was shut by the Weigher's own word; and both asked that it be a day without a market. It was set with Orrin, the east kiln-master, for the last week of the second month.
It went out at first light and was back before dusk. The pits are on the clay banks, in the town's own ground. The diggers, the kiln-masters and the potters had all come; a digger from a lower pit stood a little apart. Orrin brought three lumps from loads that had fired well; a kiln-master of the west kilns brought three from loads that had cracked. The Weigher weighed each before them and called what the pan gave: the weight, the part that is clay, and the part that is not, which was sand and shell.
- From the loads that fired well: 96, of it 91 clay and 5 not; 104, of it 99 and 5; 88, of it 84 and 4.
- From the loads that cracked: 100, of it 96 and 4; 92, of it 88 and 4; 108, of it 102 and 6.
The pan told it nothing else of any lump. It said, in its own words, that the two sets do not look different, that it had told them nothing about the cracking, and that it cannot tell which clay is good for a kiln. The diggers cheered the reading. The kiln-masters did not forgive them. Orrin said nothing for a while, and then that it was something he could not see in a pan.

### The third month
- A potter asked, "Is this clay clean?", and had its standing answer.
- The Tally-keeper showed the Weigher the first stale stick: a scale not brought within its season, the end cut. It looked and said nothing.
- A few stallholders carved two pans on their scale beams, after the mark the Tally-keeper cuts. The Stallreeve had them scraped off, saying the mark is the Tally-keeper's stick and not a beam.
- Orrin began firing a small tile of each load's clay before filling his kiln, to see whether the tile would crack when the load would, and the east kilns followed him.

## Winter

### Held, second: the fourth month, at the landing at dusk
The board. The landing, dusk, the channel folk's lamp. Present: the Weigher, Bren, two boatmen, and the ship owner of the channels, one of the eleven. It has been a good autumn for his boats.

He says: "Weigher, I run boats up and down. Every boat that comes to my landing from the south or goes down from the north, read it before it's unloaded, at dawn, and say what's in it. I'll pay you for the year, not in silver, a measure of salt for every boat, and I'll have you at my landing and no other, and that in front of the Stallreeve."

Nothing has been answered. Since then he has said in the town only that he spoke and it listened. Bren's household has said nothing of it. The Stallreeve has not heard of it.

### The rest of winter
- The fifth month: a hard spell, ice on the shallows. The Stallreeve told the line the tide-reckoner's word, that the Alder would crest over the lowest paths in four days. It did, at the height she gave and a few hours off the hour. He named the river in the Weigher's hearing: the Alder. The winter fever in the back houses was lighter than last year.
- A net-mender asked again that it reckon her debt and strike it, and had its standing answer.
- The last week of the sixth month: two households of boatwrights asked it to stand witness to the sale of a boat. It stood, with two of the watch beside it, and said afterward what it had seen and heard: the price named, the hands shaken, the day. Neither side asked what it thought of the other.

## Spring

- The seventh month: a reed island split and two households asked who owns it. It gave its standing answer. They took it to the council.
- The master of the potters fired the nest again, twenty-two pieces in one kiln, and brought them to the line like anyone to choose eleven by the gate's readings. The eleven she chose agree with each other to a hair. The second nest is kept at the potters' hall. No one has asked for it to be weighed before the ring.
- By spring the potters' talk at the gate was that the test tiles do not tell: a load cracks whose tile did not, and the other way about. The quarrel of diggers and kiln-masters goes on, without grit to blame.
- The eighth month: the spring flood, ordinary in height. The boat people were gone for some days at the lowest tides and came back.

### Held, third: the ninth month, at the gatepost
The council sat after the sowing and brought the Weightward's seat to the ring. The town talked of it in the line beforehand, and of the vote after: six for, four against, one away on the Gulf. The Assay-master spoke for it.

The board. A market day, the yard in its line, the Weigher at the foot of the gatepost with a scale in its hand. The Stallreeve stands before it with the Tally-keeper beside him holding a cut stick. The Assay-master stands a little apart with his arms folded.

The Stallreeve says: "The eleven ask the Weigher to take the Weightward's seat and keep the hall's standards, for a term of three years and a day, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam beside yours, and with the eleven's right to put the seat down at any sitting that three of them call." He lays half of a split stick at the foot of the gatepost.

Nothing has been answered. The Weigher has not touched the stick. The player gives this answer itself.

Since then the Stallreeve has said nothing more of it at the gate. A few days after, the yard cried "Weightward" at it, a boy first. It gave no answer, and nobody acted as if it had.

## Summer

### Held, fourth: the tenth month, at the gate
In the first month of summer a coaster of the southern harbor towns came up the main channel to the town's own landing, which did not happen last year. Her master is a woman of about forty, with a mate and about a dozen hands. For some days she traded at the landing: took salt, pots and reed mats, paid in goods of the south coast weighed on the town's scales, and asked the news of the town as such masters do. A boatman told Bren, in the Weigher's hearing, that she is out of Corrith. A man of her crew stood at the gatepost through one market morning watching the readings, and went away without a scale. She asked the Stallreeve where she might have her scale read, and he told her the line.

The board. A market day, early. She joined the line before light like anyone and waited her turn. The Weigher's hand has just come off the last scale before hers. She steps up to the foot of the gatepost with a ship's balance in her hand. The yard behind her has gone quiet.

Nothing has been said by either. She is not one of the town's people, and her words are not yours to invent: when the player plays this, put what the Weigher says and asks to the Keeper, who gives her answers.

### The rest of summer
- Boats from the kingdom upriver came down to trade. No one came from its king. Two of the traders had their scales read and went home with the story that the hall's own pieces were found heavy.
- The hall's present pieces went on as the market's weight. In a few bargains the old reckoning was asked for and cut on the stick.
- The twelfth month: a salt-raker offered a pinch of salt as the fee and it was taken as decided. The grain came in well enough. The council sat after the grain, took up the weight, could not decide which is the town's, and said it would wait on the Weigher's answer about the seat.

## What the Weigher was told and overheard

- That the old weights are six in a rack cut for seven, and that the Assay-master has known since the old keeper died.
- That four of the hall's pieces are meant to make the smallest old weight, by the old keeper's practice.
- That the hill kin have long supposed the hall's pieces heavy.
- That the loft was looked in by three, and nothing of what was seen.
- That the Stallreeve went among the boatmen one evening, and nothing of what was said.
- The town's names for it are not said to its face.
- In the line and at the landing, of the star: the old say it is the drowned god; the young say it came with the stranger.

## The sky

Year 1: the player has now said how the Weigher keeps its nights, so the three dim nights of Year 1 are its own to have seen: two in the first month of autumn, some days apart, and one in the dead of winter. It was awake at the landing under a clear sky each time.

Year 2: the star dimmed on five nights, and it saw each from the landing: one in the second month, one in the sixth, one in the ninth, one in the eleventh and one in the twelfth. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. It saw nothing else in the sky on those nights, and nothing in the town or on the water that went with them. Five in the year, where there were three the year before. That is what its watching has given it of the blue light's habits so far.

## God-sense and the body

It felt no other like itself all year, at the gate, in the hall, at the pits or at the landing, the coaster's master included as far as the line. The body is whole. It did not sleep. Nothing was taken from it. The half-stick of the council's first asking is in its coat. The second half-stick, of the seat, lies at the foot of the gatepost where it was laid.

## What the Weigher feels of its powers

Nothing new. The True Measure answered as it always has, in the hall and at the pits. The Seal, the Reading and the licence were not used. No seal stands and no licence is held.

## Rulings for the card and the record

1. For the card, under The True Measure, Rulings: "Year 2, 2026-11-24. Of a thing offered as one stuff, purity is how much of it is that stuff and how much is not. Of a lump of clay the god knows its weight and what part of it is clay and what part is not clay: sand, grit, shell, root. It does not tell one clay from another, a grade within the kind, or how a clay will work or fire. The same holds for any stuff sold by kind. Ledger: L-0037." This answers your case 2. ## The people at the year's turn, as the Weigher can see

About five thousand, fed and not afraid. The market is louder than a year ago and no worse. The town is easier about the gate and worse about the hall: the empty seat and the heavy pieces have been said aloud. It is split about the stranger and waiting. The channel folk, the potters and the clay head speak well of the gate; the head of the hill goods, the head of salt, the master of the smiths and now the master of the weavers do not. The watch is as it was. The reading-sticks are in use.
