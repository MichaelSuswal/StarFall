## The year
Year 1. The year opens in early autumn and runs round the four seasons to early autumn again. Months are counted from that opening.

## The first day, as the town saw it
The god came down in the hour before first light and walked into the town that morning, a market day. The town does not know what it is. What the yard and the hall saw:
- A tall stranger in a long coat stood at the hall door while the Assay-master weighed a disputed basket of salt against ten of the hall's small pieces and called it full. With the Assay-master's leave the stranger weighed the salt in a small pair of brass scales against a nest of seven brass cups, and said the beam was honest, that eight of the ten pieces agree and the chipped one and the cracked one are light, and that the salt was short by about a seventh of one weight.
- At the Assay-master's demand it weighed all ten pieces one by one in front of the door, in the Assay-master's order, and two of them twice. The Assay-master then set two pieces against each other on the hall's own beam, said aloud that the bench had been wrong in its ten, and ruled the salt short. The salt-raker said he would make it good that day. No fault was found in his basket or his word.
- Asked who it was, it said it is a weigher, that it came down the old bank at first light, that people have called it the Weigher, and that it had another name once and has lost it. It asked the Assay-master's name and was told it would have one when it had a name of its own to put in the other pan.
- For the rest of the day it read people's scales at the foot of the gatepost, off the bench, in the order they stood, for a small fee in kind. It refused scrap silver. It gave the reading only and mended nothing. The first was a channel woman, Bren, whose balance read a fish in twenty against her; she took up the cord herself. Most scales it read were out by something, both ways. One of the watch stood by for a time and said nothing. It told those still waiting it would be back the next day.
- It listened in the market, bought a clay cup, asked in passing how the old Weightward died and did not press, and ate at the landing with Bren and four of her people.
- At dusk it went up to the hall with its brass, as the Assay-master had asked. The Assay-master shut the door.

## The meeting behind the shut door
This is yours to play from the Assay-master's side. The god's side is in its decisions file, item 2, in its own words: what it will say of how it knew, what it will show again if asked, what it will not speak of, the offer it would like the Assay-master to carry to the eleven as the Assay-master's own, and that it has no name to trade but the Weigher. It tells no lie, and it may keep silent or decline to answer.
Write the meeting in your report under `## The meeting at dusk`: what the Assay-master asks, in order, in the Assay-master's own words; what the Assay-master makes of each answer the decisions file gives; and what the Assay-master decides about the offer and does about it afterward. Where the Assay-master asks, demands or offers something the god's given words do not answer, write the question in the Assay-master's words, mark it `not answered by the god's words`, and then write what the Assay-master does on meeting silence or a plain "I can't answer that". Do not put any answer in the god's mouth that the decisions file does not give.

## The rest of the year: the god in unplayed time
From the morning after, the god is among your people with no helper and no seat, and no one acts for it. It does only what its decisions file and its conduct profile already say: the yard on its stated terms (decision 3), and nothing on the lines the profile marks "check with the player before acting" beyond the order the player gave for them. It gives no licence, sets no seal on its own motion, makes no promise, and rules on nothing but a question of weight put to it.
Work out your people's year around that: who comes to the yard and who stays away, what the bench, the council, the Stallreeve, the Tally-keeper, the trades and the watch make of a stranger who reads scales true, and what each does about it.
For each thing a mortal brings to the stranger in the year, sort it as `engine/rules/encounters.md` section 6 gives and say which in your report:
- answered in full by what the god has already decided (say by which decision or profile line);
- a request, plea or question from the town that its decided words do not answer: it meets silence or a plain refusal to answer, your people react as they would, and you list it under `## Asked of the god`;
- anything else you are unsure of: write the moment it reaches the god and stop that thread there.

## The world this year, as your people met it
- The weather. An ordinary year. The autumn catch was ordinary. Winter was wet with its hard spells, as it is. The spring flood was ordinary in height. Summer was long and warm.
- The hill carriers came down the old sunken road in the middle of autumn with ore and charcoal, as every year.
- The boat people were gone from the channels for some days at the lowest tides of autumn, in the second month, and again at the lowest tides of spring, in the eighth, and came back.
- Boats from the kingdom upriver came down in summer to trade, as most summers. No one came from its king. Ships of the southern harbor towns were in the gulf in summer; none of the larger ones came up to the town's own landing this year, and their goods came up in the town's boats.
- The star dimmed on certain nights this year, as it has in other years: the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. It came early in the year, in the first month, and again in the dead of winter. Anyone awake under a clear sky saw it. What your people make of such nights is theirs. Give no count of them in anything you write; the Keeper has the count.
- No other stranger came to the town this year whom it had not seen before.

## What is yours to decide, and what is not
Yours: everything your people do, think, say and feel, every mortal among them, how each office holder meets the god and whether each takes its word, their work, stores, births and deaths, quarrels and stories. Mortals have their own will. They do not know the god's plans; they meet what it does, as it does it.
Not yours:
- An outcome that is uncertain and contested (a fight, a search, a persuasion of someone outside your people, a boat in a swell, a craft tried beyond what your people know). Do not settle it. Write it in your report as an attempt: who tries what, when, what success and failure would each mean, and every circumstance from your record that bears on it. The Keeper gives the odds and rolls. Write the rest of your year both ways where it turns on that outcome, briefly.
- What a god's power does or shows. Say what the god is seen to do and what your people make of it. Where your year turns on what a power did, say so in your report and the Keeper will settle it.
- Anything about another people or about land beyond what your record gives. Give no distance or travel time that is not in your record; say where a party set out for and by what way, and the Keeper prices the trip.
- The name of your people or their home, and the name of any office holder your record leaves unnamed: these stay unnamed unless a moment in your year has a mortal say a name aloud, and then you write who said it, when and to whom.
