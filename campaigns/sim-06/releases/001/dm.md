## The meeting at dusk, the landing day

The hall, the door shut, one reed lamp on the bench, the ten pieces back along the wall. The Assay-master across the bench, hands on the plank. In order:

1. The Assay-master: "You weighed my ten in front of the yard and made a fool of my bench. Tell me first how you knew." The Weigher answered in its given words: its scales tell it what is in the pans, and what is weighed beside them while it is near; that is the whole of it; it will show it again if asked.
2. "Then show me again." The Assay-master set out three things, one at a time: a small fired weight, and two things the Weigher had not seen before. The Weigher weighed each in its own pans and called it in its cups. He did not dispute any of the three.
3. "What do you want here?" The Weigher gave the offer in its given words: it wants to see the Weightward's old weights set right; it will weigh them in front of the council, not in a back room; it would like that to be the offer he takes to the eleven, and his offer, not its own.
4. "Why should the eleven take a stranger's scale for the town's standards?" The Weigher kept silent. The Assay-master: "A man who is selling has an answer to that."
5. "Are you something more than a man with good brass?" The Weigher kept silent. The Assay-master waited, and went on.
6. "Who sent you? Does a king of the river towns want his share of our market again?" The Weigher kept silent.
7. "Have you a name yet to put in the other pan?" The Weigher: none but the Weigher. The Assay-master: "Then that is what I'll call you, for now." He did not give his own.
8. "Why did you ask how the old Weightward died?" The Weigher kept silent. The Assay-master said only: "He was my friend."
9. "What does the Weigher want of the hall after the weighing?" The Weigher kept silent.

Then, unasked, he said that if the eleven would have it, he would stand at the bench with his own hand on the beam while the old weights were weighed. He opened the door. Nothing was promised by either.

The True Measure in the room: what the Weigher's pans held, it knew. Nothing else was weighed. The Good-Faith Reading was not used; no bargain was struck.
God-sense in the room: no weight in the air. The Assay-master is a mortal.

## The year at the gate

- From the second morning the yard filled before light. The Weigher stood at the foot of the gatepost with its scales and nest and read each scale in the order of the line, for grain, fish or a fired weight, the reading only. It refused scrap silver again when a ship-owner's runner offered it for a place at the head; the runner stood all day and was read last, and the yard laughed about it for a week.
- Every reading the Weigher gave was what The True Measure told it. Most scales were out by something, both ways. Some read level at once, and some after their owners adjusted them and had them tried again.
- On the third day the one the yard calls the Stallreeve, who runs the market and the watch, watched until noon and set two of the watch at the gatepost "to keep the line, not to guard you". From the second month he brought every quarrel of weight between buyer and seller to the gatepost, had the scales read, and ruled on it himself at the stall. He never asked the Weigher to rule.
- In the second month the hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way with ore and charcoal. A charcoal buyer would not pay until the carriers' scale was read. The Stallreeve brought both scales. The carriers' was level to a hair; the buyer's was out by about a tenth, in his own favor. He paid the difference that day.
- In the third month the potters began bringing fixed reed baskets of each digger's clay to be weighed, holding that a heavy load is a gritty one. Most loads weighed alike; one digger's ran heavy by about one part in nine. The Weigher gave the weights and nothing else. The quarrel went to the council, which settled a price and not the question. By summer the yard's talk was that the kilns that sorted by weight had cracked as many loads as before, and some potters said aloud at the gate that the scale is no use for clay.
- Stallholders hung small fired tokens on their scales saying "read at the gate". The Stallreeve had them taken down in the fourth month: a scale read level in autumn can be filed light by midwinter.
- Through the winter an apprentice of the hall brought scales to the line, fourteen in all over a month. The Weigher read each as it read any. A few had something small wrong with them: a thumbnail of clay under a pan, a thread of reed in a cord.
- The one the yard calls the Tally-keeper, who keeps the kept halves of the town's split sticks, has been cutting a stick of the gate's readings since the first night, split in the hall's manner. In the fourth month she came to the gatepost and said: "Name a mark, and I will cut it." The Weigher kept silent. She keeps her own stick.
- In the fifth month there was a hard spell and the ordinary winter fever in the crowded houses. Word went round four days ahead, from an old woman of the flats, that the water would crest over the lowest paths; stalls moved and boats were drawn up; it came half a day later than she said, at the height she gave.
- In the sixth month, at the landing, late, where the Weigher sat by the channel folk's lamp: a hand went to its coat for the nest. The Weigher's own hand was on the nest first. It said nothing and did not strike. Bren's people took hold of the man and the watch held him. He is a bonded man of a stallholder whose scale the gate had read out by about a tenth, in the stallholder's favor. At first light the Stallreeve brought him to the gatepost and asked, before the yard: "Do you ask a price of him?" The Weigher kept silent and read the next scale. The council heard it in its ring and set a price in measure, for a theft tried on a guest of the yard. The Tally-keeper holds the stick. The price lies unclaimed. The man gave his master's name; the yard says the council did not find that the master sent him.
- In the eighth month the spring flood came, ordinary in height, and the sowing took every hand.
- In the ninth month the yard had it within the day that the eleven had voted, seven to three with one away on the water, to ask the stranger to weigh the hall's old weights before them after the harvest, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. Against, as the yard tells it: the heads of hill goods and of salt, and the master of the smiths. The head of salt spoke longest: a store of worth is made by agreeing what the weight is, not by finding out.
- In the tenth month boats came down from the kingdom upriver, which their traders call the Aldermark, with grain, wool and some bronze. Two traders had their scales read on a dare; both were out by a little; they laughed and paid in barley. No one came from their king.
- In the eleventh month a mate off one of the southern coasters lying in the gulf asked to have his captain's scale read, and it was. The southern ships did not come up to the town's landing this year.
- The master of the potters has fired a nest of eleven new clay standards in one kiln, unmarked, waiting on the weighing of the old ones.

## The held encounter: the eleventh month, at the gatepost

Stop the morning story here.

A market day in high summer, the yard full. The Stallreeve came down from the hall himself with a split stick in his hand and the Tally-keeper a pace behind him. He stood in front of the gatepost and said it for the whole yard to hear: the eleven ask the Weigher to weigh the hall's old weights before them, at the first market after the grain is in, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. He laid one half of the stick at the foot of the gatepost.

The board: the Weigher stands at the gatepost with a stallholder's scale in its hand, its own scales on the clay beside it, the nest on it. The half-stick lies at its feet. The Stallreeve has not turned to go. The Tally-keeper holds the other half. The line has gone quiet and the yard can hear. The Assay-master is not in the yard. Nothing is asked in words beyond what was said. No weight in the air from anyone present.

What the Weigher knows that bears on it: the offer was its own, carried by the Assay-master as his; the Assay-master said in the shut room that he would stand with his hand on the beam; the council's vote and who spoke against it; that it has not seen the old weights or been told how many there are or where they are kept.

## What the Weigher was told and overheard

- In the line, first month: an old salt-raker told how a man called Hale the Raker led the salt people onto the bank in the flood years, and that a larger god drowned at a place they call the Maun Stair, which is why the tide keeps coming. An old woman spoke of walkers the town calls the list-singers, who came when the oldest were young, sang names all night on the bank and traded nothing.
- The hill carriers named their village up the old road, Ruddfold, and spoke of iron from stone towns far to the east, a place they call Hask.
- The upriver traders named their kingdom, the Aldermark.
- In the eighth month the yard said the boat people had been gone from the channels at the lowest tides, in autumn and again in spring, and that they go to the Maun Stair. Nobody followed them.
- The town still has no name for itself and none was said.

## The sky

The star dimmed on three nights of the year: two in the first month of autumn, some days apart, and one in the dead of winter. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. The Weigher has this from the town: the line at the gate talked of the first two in its hearing in the first month, and the watch and the boatmen at the landing spoke of the winter one. The town says it has seen such nights in other years. Many in the line tied this year's to the stranger's coming.

Whether the Weigher itself was awake and looking up on any of those nights is not set. The player has not said how it spends its nights; the record has it sitting late at the landing and no more. ## God-sense and the body

No weight in the air from anyone who stood within a few paces of the Weigher in the whole year: not the townsfolk, the carriers, the upriver traders or the coaster's mate. The body is whole. The scales, the nest and everything carried are on it. The fees were eaten, given or spent in the ordinary way; it holds a few days' worth.

## The people at the year's turn, as the Weigher can see

Fed, housed, not afraid. The market quarrels less than it did, because a quarrel of weight can be settled at the gate in an hour. The watch is a few men larger. The town is split about the stranger: the channel folk, the salt-rakers and most potters for it; the hill-goods head, the smiths' master and the head of salt against a stranger's brass setting a measure. The grain is in. The yard is shorter than in autumn except on market days.

## Deaths

None of anyone the Weigher knew by name. The winter fever took old and very young in the ordinary way.
