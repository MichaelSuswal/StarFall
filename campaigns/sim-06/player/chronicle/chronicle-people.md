# The people's side of the chronicle: Year 1

What the people would tell the stranger, and what it could see of them. Held back: anything the Keeper has yet to settle.

## Autumn

The grain was in. The channel folk fished the autumn run, the salt-rakers closed their pans for the cold, and the clay-bank farmers sowed hardy grain. The hill carriers came down the old sunken road in the middle of autumn with ore and charcoal, as they do every year, and went home with a good word for the gate.

A stranger came to the hall door on a market day and weighed the hall's pieces in front of the yard. The yard calls it the Weigher. From the next morning it stood at the foot of the gatepost and read scales in the order they stood, for a small fee in grain, fish or a fired weight. It would not take silver. A ship-owner's runner stood in the line all day for a place and was read last.

The boat people left the channels for some days at the lowest tides and came back. The blue star went dim on some nights, as it has in other years.

## Winter

The wet came, with its hard spells. Fever went through the short-stilted houses at the back of the bank and took the old and the very young. The Stallreeve moved four stalls under the hall's roof and the market stayed open. Nobody died there.

The tide-reckoner of the salt flats called the midwinter water four days ahead. It crested half a day later than she said and at the height she gave. Stalls moved, boats were drawn up, three families went to the mounds with their goats.

The star went dim for a time on a night of the cold season.

The kiln-masters began weighing a fixed basket of every digger's clay before they would take it, on scales that had been read at the gate. The council argued the diggers and the kiln-masters and settled it by a price in measure.

The Tally-keeper keeps a stick of the gate's readings, split down its length, one half in the hall and one half in her own house.

## Spring

The rains eased. The Alder rose to an ordinary flood. The weirs were mended, two reed islands joined and one split, and the boatwrights were busy. The tide-reckoner said an ordinary flood needed no feet and did not tell it. Sowing took five weeks of every hand.

The boat people left again at the lowest tides of spring and came back.

The council of eleven voted, seven for and three against, to ask the stranger to weigh the hall's old weights before them, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam, after the harvest. The master of the potters has fired a nest of eleven new clay standards in one kiln so they shrink alike. They are unmarked and wait in the potters' hall.

## Summer

Long and warm. The reed was cut early. Boats from the Aldermark came down with grain, wool and some bronze, and two of their traders had their scales read at the gate and went home laughing. No one came from their king. Ships of the southern harbor towns lay in the Gulf and their goods came up the river in the town's own boats.

The Stallreeve carried the council's invitation to the gatepost himself, on a market day, in front of the yard.

The grain came in. The pans ran. The town is a little richer and a little more tired than it was.

## What the town says it needs

A true standard in the hall. A keeper of the standards, since the old Weightward died last spring. A name for the town, which it has not yet decided and will.

## The end of Year 1, after the gatepost

The stranger said yes before the yard, took up the half stick, and gave its terms aloud. The Stallreeve carried them to the hall. The Tally-keeper cut its mark, two pans, level, at the head of her stick of the gate's readings.

A stallholder's scale read out in her own favor and she took up her cord and made it level on the second trial. The yard gave her a place to stand. Split reading sticks came into use: one half the owner's and one half the Tally-keeper's, holding for the day cut on them, lapsing at a season, a notch for each market a scale is not brought. The Stallreeve's rule is that no stick is hung on a stall or a scale.

The master of the bonded man brought his scale to the gate in the third week of the month. It read level, and the price of the thief was cut as remitted.

Bren's people told it, when it asked once and quietly, what the town says of the boat people's low-tide trips: that none has followed and none knows.

The eleven took the terms whole, as the hall's rules for the day, eight to three, before the harvest. They told the stranger where the old weights are kept: in the chest in the hall, under the old keeper's loft, with two keys, the second the Assay-master's and the first hanging in the ring.

The grain came in.

# The people's side of the chronicle: Year 2

## Autumn

The old weigher of the hill kin came down the old sunken road with his own stone weights. The hall's day was the first market of the autumn. The Assay-master opened the chest before the eleven. The rack holds six weights in seven seats. The seventh seat is empty and clean. He said he had found it so after the old Weightward was laid out, and that he had told only the Stallreeve, the evening before.

The stranger called the old weights in its own ones: forty-eight, ninety-six, a hundred and ninety-two, three hundred and eighty-four, seven hundred and sixty-six, fifteen hundred and thirty-six. The Assay-master's beam agreed with each call. He read the fifth wrong at first, level, then light on the second trial, and said so. The Tally-keeper cut both.

Four of the hall's fired pieces set against the first old weight go down: the hall's pieces run heavy by about a twentieth. The potters' nest of eleven, weighed the same day, called thirteen, twenty-five, thirty-seven, fifty-two, seventy-five, eighty-nine, ninety-eight, a hundred and thirty, a hundred and fifty, a hundred and seventy-nine, a hundred and eighty-one. It does not agree with itself. The hill weigher's stones agreed with the old six to a hair and asked to be weighed in ones and were not.

The eleven did not decide which is the town's weight. They decided to wait. The market stands on the hall's present pieces, debts stand as they were made, nothing in the hall is mended or carried out, and the chest is opened only before two of the eleven. It comes back at the first sitting after the next grain is in.

The stranger took no fee and no gift that day. The hill weigher went home ahead of the carriers. The hill carriers came down in the middle of autumn with ore and charcoal and went home with the figures.

The boat people were gone from the channels some days at the lowest tides and came back. The blue star dimmed on a night in the second month.

In the last week of the second month, on a day with no market, the stranger went to the diggers' pits and weighed three lumps from loads that fired well and three from loads that cracked, in front of the diggers and the kiln masters. Each lump was about a twentieth part sand and shell, and the cracked lumps carried no more than the well fired. Orrin and the west kiln master looked at the figures and said the sets did not differ. It said that it had then told them nothing about the cracking, and that it could not tell which clay is good for a kiln.

Rain came in. The first reading stick lapsed unbrought. Some stallholders carved the two pans on their scales and the Stallreeve had them scraped off.

## Winter

The wet, with its hard spells. Fever in the short-stilted houses, fewer than last year. The tide-reckoner called the winter water four days ahead. The stranger heard the Stallreeve tell the line.

The stranger stood witness at a boat sale between two households of boatwrights and said what it saw and heard.

The star went dim for a time on a night in the dead of winter.

## Spring

The Alder rose to an ordinary flood. A reed island split and two households asked the stranger who owned it. It said owning is not a weight, and offered to weigh what each household said was cut or drawn. They took it to the council. Sowing took five weeks. The boat people were gone at the lowest tides of spring and came back. The star dimmed on a night in spring.

## Summer

Long and warm. The reed was cut early. Boats from the Aldermark came down with grain, wool and some bronze; no one came from their king. The grain came in. The council sat after the grain and left the weight where it stood. The star dimmed on two nights late in the summer.

## What the town says it needs

A weight, which the eleven have not set. A name for the town, which it has not decided and will.

# The people's side of the chronicle: Year 2 told again, from the evening of the weighing

Where this section and the Year 2 section above differ, this section is what happened. Held back: anything the Keeper has yet to settle.

## The first month

On the evening of the weighing the eleven rose, the yard went to its supper, and the stranger stayed in the hall with the Assay-master and the door was shut. When it opened the Assay-master sent for the Stallreeve, who stood over him while he locked the old weights in the chest with his own key. The key on the peg was not touched and the seventh seat was left unwiped. At the hall door the stranger told the Stallreeve what it is. He said the gate was kept as it was and he would say nothing in the yard. He carried the Assay-master's asking for a sitting round to the eleven that night without naming the matter. It went to the winter sitting.

On the third day the Assay-master, the Stallreeve and the clay head went up the old keeper's loft and came down quiet.

## The second to the third month

The boat people were gone at the lowest tides and came back. The stranger's word for the old weigher of the hill kin went home with the carriers on a stick the Tally-keeper cut. The Stallreeve went down to the landing alone one evening. The pits day, the first stale stick, the tiles and the scraped marks were as the old section has them.

## The fourth month: the landing

The ship owner of the channels put a bargain to it at the landing before Bren and two boatmen. It said no to his landing and no other. It gave its terms: the captain sets out the load, it calls the weight, the count and what the stuff is, what it is not shown it says it was not shown, and it reads at the landing of any owner who asks. He took them, for a pinch of salt a boat. The Stallreeve said the gate comes first and a landing's line is its owner's to keep. The Tally-keeper agreed to cut sticks for boats on her own terms. Other owners asked for a dawn: the ship owner of the open water, two smaller owners of the channels, Bren's household. Some captains would not show, and sold a tenth low.

## The fifth and sixth months

Ice, fever in the back houses, fewer than the year before. The tide-reckoner called the water four days ahead, right in height, a few hours off the hour. The star went dim on a night in the dead of winter.

Early in the sixth month a boat of the channel ship owner's own came in with salt that was one part in six sand and grit under a clean top. The stranger called it basket by basket before the captain and the landing. The ship owner said "Read the rest." The household that raked it had a thin season. The Stallreeve ruled short measure, not theft, and the household made good the sixth in clean salt in two instalments. Captains at the landing now turn out every fifth basket.

In the middle of the month the ring sat. It let the yard in, nine to two. The stranger stood at the foot of the ring and said it is a god, what it had told the Assay-master, that it weighs and the ring decides, that it did not ask to be Weightward and did not refuse it yet, that it would answer for the seat when the loft had been looked in and the seventh looked for, and that it did not take the seventh or touch the chest. The Assay-master said it was said to him in the hall on the evening of the weighing and that he asked. The head of salt asked whether it can tell when a man is lying; it said it reads a bargain and never a mind and will not use it on anyone in the town without their asking. The hall went silent, then breathed, and the yard went to its supper. The line at the gate next morning was twice as long and nobody bought fish until noon.

In the last week two households of boatwrights asked it to stand witness to a boat sale, and said they asked nothing of the other thing.

## The seventh to the ninth month

The reed island, the flood and the second nest were as the old section has them. The second nest agreed with itself to a hair. The spring flood was ordinary, sowing took five weeks, the boat people were gone at the lowest tides of spring and the star dimmed on a night in spring.

The ring sat after the sowing and offered the seat, six to four, the ship owner of the open water away on the Gulf. The Stallreeve carried it to the gatepost. The stranger read a stallholder's scale first, heavy on the buying side by about one part in thirty, and said so. Then it said it is a god, said yes, and gave its terms: three years and a day, the Assay-master's hand on the beam, the ring's right to put it down at any sitting three of them call, the seat unwiped and not waited on, the chest never opened by it alone, no fee for the seat, the gate as it was. To the yard last: "I am the Weightward, for a term. I am not their god, and nothing here is mine." The yard cried "Weightward" until the Stallreeve put up his hand.

## The tenth to the twelfth month

In the first days of the tenth month the ring sat and the stranger took the old keeper's key off its peg in front of them. A coaster of the southern harbor towns came to the landing. Her master stood in the line and asked it nothing at first. It asked her what the southern harbors read their scales against, whether Corrith has a measure the other towns will take, and what a southern captain would want to know of the town's weights. She said each house against its own, that Corrith keeps a set for quarrels and nobody swears it is what it was, and that she wanted to know which is the town's weight, the old stones or the clay. It gave her no answer. She asked who it works for and whether it said it was a god. It said yes. She said a god ashore is a landlord and it takes barley. Her coaster went down the channel after three days.

At the gatepost at dusk the stranger asked the Stallreeve, with the Assay-master by, whether the town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast. The Stallreeve said not this summer and to ask after the grain sitting. In the hall's doorway the stranger asked the Assay-master what the three saw in the loft, and he told it: the old man's coat and boots hanging on a peg, stiff with dried river silt to above the knee, nobody had moved them, nothing else was out of its place, they touched nothing and came down, and the ring had had it since the winter.

The channels ran low. The diggers asked for a second day at the pits. The pan said as before and the quarrel keeps. Boats from the Aldermark came down in the summer and went away with the story. The grain came in. The council did not sit on the weight in the twelfth month.

# The people's side of the chronicle: Year 3

## Autumn

The grain was in. At the first sitting after the grain the stranger weighed the six again before the ring with the Assay-master's hand on the beam, and called 48, 96, 192, 384, 766 and 1,536. The Assay-master read the fifth light on the first trial and said he had learned that one. The ring then voted the old six the town's weight, six to two, from the first market of the autumn that comes round next year, with the clause that bargains made before the change stand as made. The master of the potters is to fire a nest to the old six in the spring. The seventh seat stays as it is.

The star dimmed on one night of the month. The boat people were gone at the lowest tides and came back.

The hill carriers came down the old road in the middle of the month with ore and charcoal. The old weigher of the hill kin came with them, a year older, in a cart, and on the third market morning had his six stones weighed at the gate in front of the head of the hill goods, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve. The stranger called 48, 96, 193, 384, 768 and 1,536. The three large stones needed a beam and the Assay-master brought his own. The old weigher said the hill kin's stones and the hall's agreed as far as anyone knew. Asked what his people say the seventh was, he said it is a story, that the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, and he would not stand behind it.

The autumn catch was ordinary. The dawns at the landings ran on the terms given.

## Winter

The wet, with its hard spells. The Assay-master asked the stranger to check six scales he had passed; two read level and four a hair out. Two households of boatwrights asked it to stand witness to the lease of a boatyard and said again they asked nothing of the other thing.

Fever in the back houses, fewer than the year before, with four stalls and four braziers under the hall's roof. The tide-reckoner called the winter water four days ahead and was right in height and a few hours off the hour. The star went dim on two nights of the fifth month, in the dead of winter.

## Spring

The Alder rose to an ordinary flood. The master of the potters fired a nest to the old six and brought the pieces to the line like anyone. Sowing took five weeks. The boat people were gone at the lowest tides of spring and came back. The star dimmed on two nights of the seventh month and one of the eighth.

## Summer

Long and warm. The reed was cut early. The ship owner of the open water's coasters went out past the Horn. No coaster of the southern towns came. Boats from the Aldermark arrived with grain, wool and some bronze, a pair of traders had their scales read, and they asked in the yard what the keeper of the town's weights was. The grain came in.

## What the town says it needs

A name for the town, which it has not decided and will. The new nest to hold, so that the old six are the town's weight on the first market of the new autumn.

# The people's side of the chronicle: Year 3 told again, from the fifth day of the first month

Where this section and the Year 3 section above differ, this section is what happened. The Year 3 section above is set aside from the fifth day of the first month. This section stops on the fourteenth day of the seventh month, at the door of the potters' hall. Held back: anything the Keeper has yet to settle.

## Autumn

On the fifth, a market morning, the Weightward read a fish seller's balance first: out, the buying pan heavy by about one part in sixty. He took it away to mend the cord himself. Then it gave the word before the yard, the Tally-keeper cut it on a pair of sticks and split both. The first stick is the town's word and the ship owner of the open water has the carrier's half. The second stick the Stallreeve carried up to the eleven, each thing by itself, neither yes nor no. The carrier took three of its instructions and not a pair. It told the Stallreeve that it answers the master of the smiths and the woman of the back houses only when either next asks at the gate, or when the ring asks in session.

From the sixth, at its landing dawns, it asked the ship owners and the captains to bring any large stone they own, saying twice the sixth by the run of them is three thousand and seventy-two in its ones. It weighed each on the landing's beam with the owner watching and said only what it is. Seven came in the first month. It asked no one to give anything up.

Each night at the landing by the lamp it runs its thumbnail down the cups of its nest, smallest first, and then counts the day's readings. The boatmen call it running the cups.

The star dimmed on the night of the twenty-second of the first month. A boy at the gate asked whether it is its own, and it gave him no answer.

The hill carriers came down in the second month and the old weigher of the hill kin with them. On the eighth he had his six stones weighed at the gate before the head of the hill goods, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve: 48, 96, 193, 384, 768 and 1,536. The Assay-master brought his own beam for the three large stones. The old weigher said the hill kin's stones and the hall's agreed, and that the hill kin tell boys the seventh weighs what is owed, and he would not stand behind it.

On the eleventh a young carrier asked it whether it can be killed. It said: "No. I can be hurt, held or scattered, and I come together again." Asked what would break it, it did not answer. The same day it asked the Stallreeve for the fourteenth, the day of the lowest tide, to walk out to the salt pans and look at the household that sent the salted baskets. He gave it. The household was told the night before.

On the fourteenth the post stood empty with two of the watch at it and no notch cut. It walked out over the marsh to the Flats. The household turned out every basket of the season's store unasked. It weighed a basket it chose itself and called it clean salt, took a pinch from the head of the household, and was back before dusk. The boat people were gone from the channels from the twelfth and back on the sixteenth.

The rain came in the third month. The master of the boatwrights brought his yard's door-stone to be read, and the master of the smiths a forge slab of five thousand nine hundred and sixty, and asked it nothing. A young stallholder asked whether it can tell who is lying and was told what it told the ring, and then that both pans show and that anyone it reads knows whether it means its own terms.

## Winter

The wet, with its hard spells. In the fourth month, on the eighteenth, at the fish landing, an old net-maker brought her door-stone on a hand sledge. It read three thousand and eleven in the Weightward's ones, sixty-one short of its figure. It said only what the stone is, and told the Stallreeve and the Assay-master first and the ring second. The Assay-master looked at the stone and said it was never cut, and was a door-stone. The net-maker kept her step. The Assay-master asked it to check six scales he had passed, and two read level and four a hair out. Two households of boatwrights asked it to stand witness to the lease of a boatyard, and it stood.

Fever in the back houses, fewer than the year before, with four stalls and four braziers under the hall's roof. An old woman of the back houses asked it whether it can cure a fever, for her daughter's boy, and it said: "No. I have no power for it." The tide-reckoner called the winter water four days ahead, right in height and a few hours off the hour. The star dimmed on two nights of the fifth month.

On the fifteenth of the sixth month the ring sat on the second stick with the door shut and did not send for it. On the seventeenth the Stallreeve said at the post what the eleven took and did not take. Taken, thing by thing: that the hall does not know what the seventh weighed and has looked for it; the four fired pieces against the first bronze, about a twentieth heavy; the chipped piece and the cracked piece against the eight; that the ten have not been weighed against the six since and the Tally-keeper has the figures; that the hall keeps no weight that anyone may not see weighed; and its asking that the word go in the order said and nothing added. Not taken: that the fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so, four for and five against, two not voting; and that any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read, three for and seven against, one not voting. The ring gave him leave for one reason, that a scale is read at the gate like any and the hall is for the town's. It did not answer when he asked what it would have cut for the carrier now. The ship owner of the open water said he carries what is on his stick, as cut.

## Spring

The Alder rose with the thaw. The tide-reckoner told the Stallreeve it would be an ordinary flood and she did not need her feet for it. The star dimmed on the night of the seventh of the seventh month. On the fifth the master of the potters said at the post that the pieces of the third nest are shaped and drying on their boards.

On the fourteenth of the seventh month, a market day, when the gate was done, the Weightward walked to the potters' hall with the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper, and came to its door.

## What the town says it needs

A name for the town, which it has not decided and will. A stone that is the seventh, or word that there is none. The new nest to hold.
