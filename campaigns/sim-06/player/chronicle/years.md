# Year by year

Newest year at the top. Only what the Weigher knows.

## Year 002

Told as far as the fourth month, at the landing at dusk. More than one moment of the year is held; the rest of the year is entered here as each is played and the year past it is told.

Season opened: early autumn.

The god: The played day stood at the held moment of Year 1, late in the eleventh month. It said yes to the council's asking before the yard and spoke its terms aloud (scene 1, rolling save, Year 2, section 1). It read a stallholder's scale out and then level, and gave its standing answers at the gate (section 2). It asked and had leave for the split reading-sticks (scene 2, section 3). It went to the hall door at dusk (section 4). At the door, on its handed-off words, it gave the Assay-master its five answers, among them "Yes" to whether it is more than a man with good brass, with the rest promised for after the weighing; he asked three things more, and it kept silent on each. It told the Stallreeve his rule on the sticks is a good one, asked the master of the potters to bring the new nest to the hall, and asked Bren's household once about the low-tide trips. In the first month of Year 2 it weighed the hall's old weights before the eleven on its handed-off terms, the three bronze in its own pans and the three stone on the hall's beam with its scales in its hand, and the potters' nest of eleven after them. It took no fee and no gift. It kept silent to the master of the smiths, to the old weigher of the hill kin and to a boy on the step. Its scales gave it nothing in the six that does not read true. It sensed no other like itself. Its body is whole and it did not sleep. It has told no one it is a god.

What it saw and was told: The Assay-master has nothing to tell yet of the old Weightward's death. The old weights are kept in a chest in the hall, in the room under the old keeper's loft; the Assay-master holds the second key and the old keeper's key hangs in the ring. The eleven took its terms whole, eight to three. The rack is cut for seven weights and held six; the seat for the largest is empty, and clean where the others are dusty; the Assay-master said he found it so after the old keeper's laying out and had told only the Stallreeve, the evening before. The six, in its ones: 48, 96, 192, 384, 766, 1,536; the fifth is chipped at its foot and was read level and then light by the Assay-master. Four of the hall's fired pieces go down against the smallest: the hall's pieces run heavy by about a twentieth. The nest of eleven: 13, 25, 37, 52, 75, 89, 98, 130, 150, 179, 181. The stones of the old weigher of the hill kin agree with the old six; he says the hill kin have long supposed the hall's pieces heavy. The ring carried the clay head's motion, nine to one: no weight is named the town's until the seventh is found or given up and the nest fired again; the market stands on the hall's present pieces; debts stand as made; nothing in the hall is filed, mended, replaced or carried out; the chest is opened only before two of the eleven; the matter comes back at the first sitting after the next grain. The ring agreed that the old keeper's loft be looked in by three. Bren's household would say what the town says of the low-tide trips: the salt-rakers say the water folk go to the Maun Stair, the channel folk say to meet kin, and none has followed or knows.

Told on the Year 3 played day. The evening of the weighing, in the hall, played (rolling save, Year 3, sections 1 to 3): asked "What are you?" by the Assay-master, it told him it is a god, that others came down when it did and it has felt none, and what its scales give; that there is more to it, which it would not list; that it did not take the seventh weight and never touched the chest. It answered him on the key, on the seventh (it cannot find it; it can weigh a piece brought to it) and on the beam. It asked him to keep it between them until he had decided what the ring should hear; he would not hold it alone. At its asking the chest was locked before the Stallreeve, the empty seat left unwiped. It told the Stallreeve itself, with the Assay-master beside it. The Stallreeve keeps the gate as it was, says nothing in the yard, and carried the Assay-master's asking for a sitting round that night. It means to come to that sitting and say it first, and has asked to say it at the foot of the ring with the yard let in. It sent a word to the old weigher at Ruddfold by the next carrier: his stones weighed at the gate for a pinch.

The autumn after, as it stood before that evening was played: on the third day after the weighing the Assay-master, the Stallreeve and the clay head went up the old keeper's loft stair and came down within the hour, quiet; nothing of what they saw has been said in its hearing. The gate went on with its settled answers. The hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way in the second month with the figures of the hall's weights already in hand; one asked about Hask; their scale read a little out in their favor and they mended it. The Stallreeve went among the boatmen at the landing one evening; it did not hear what was said. The boat people were gone at the lowest tides of the second month and came back. It saw the blue light dim on one night of the second month, from the landing. With the Stallreeve's and the Tally-keeper's leave, on a day without a market in the last week of the second month, it went to the diggers' pits on the clay banks and weighed before diggers, kiln-masters and potters. From loads that fired well: 96 (91 clay, 5 not), 104 (99, 5), 88 (84, 4). From loads that cracked: 100 (96, 4), 92 (88, 4), 108 (102, 6). What was not clay was sand and shell. Orrin and a kiln-master of the west kilns said the two sets do not look different; it said it had then told them nothing. In the third month: its settled answer to "Is this clay clean?"; the first stale stick, shown by the Tally-keeper; two pans carved on scale beams and scraped off by the Stallreeve's word; Orrin began firing a test tile of each load, and the east kilns followed. Held, second: the fourth month, the landing at dusk, the ship owner of the channels and his offer.

The people: At the year's turn, fed and not afraid; the grain in, well enough. The market is louder than a year ago and no worse. Easier about the gate and worse about the hall. Split about the stranger, and waiting: the channel folk, the potters and the clay head speak well of the gate; the heads of hill goods and of salt, the master of the smiths and the master of the weavers do not. The reading-sticks are in use.

Encounters: Minor: the stallholder's scale; the bonded man's master with his mended scale, twelfth month of Year 1. Major: the hall door at dusk, resolved on handed-off words; the weighing, resolved on handed-off terms. Played on the Year 3 played day: the Assay-master's "What are you?", the evening of the weighing, in the hall. Held: the ship owner of the channels at the landing, the fourth month.

Messages: none sent, none received.

Rulings this year: purity of a stuff sold by kind, ledger L-0037; a piece too heavy for its pans, and what is held in unplayed time, ledger L-0038 (canon.md).

Deaths of note: none of anyone it knows by name or office.

Map: Ruddfold named again, by its own weigher, in the hall.

Open at year's end: see threads.md.

## Year 001

Season opened: early autumn.

The god: Landed before first light on an old clay bank about a mile from a town and walked in unannounced on a market day (scene 1, rolling save sections 1 to 2). At the Assay-master's bench it weighed a disputed load of salt and then the hall's ten small pieces, and the bench changed its ruling (scene 2, rolling save sections 3 to 4). It called itself a weigher, the Weigher, its other name lost. It read scales in the yard for a fee in measure, listened in the market and ate with Bren's people (rolling save section 5). At dusk it met the Assay-master with the door shut: it told him what its scales tell it and showed it again, offered to weigh the Weightward's old weights before the council as his offer, kept silent on five questions, and promised nothing. For the rest of the year it stood at the gatepost and read scales in the order of the line, refused silver, ruled on nothing, and kept silent on what its decided words did not answer. It sensed no other like itself in anyone who came near all year. Its body is whole and it carries all it came with, the fees of a few days, and a clay cup.

The people: At the year's end, fed, housed and not afraid; the grain in. The market quarrels less, since a quarrel of weight can be settled at the gate in an hour. The watch is a few men larger. The town is split about the stranger: the channel folk, the salt-rakers and most potters for it; the heads of hill goods and of salt and the master of the smiths against a stranger's brass setting a measure. Told to it at landing by no one; numbers were not given to it.

What it was told: The Stallreeve runs the market and the watch, set two of the watch at the gatepost to keep the line, and from the second month brought quarrels of weight to be read and ruled on them himself. The hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way in the second month. Potters weighed diggers' clay by the fixed basket from the third month; by summer the yard said the scale is no use for clay. Tokens saying "read at the gate" were hung on scales and taken down by the Stallreeve in the fourth month. The Tally-keeper cuts a split stick of the gate's readings and keeps it. An apprentice of the hall brought fourteen scales in a month of the winter, a few with something small wrong. An old woman of the flats called the midwinter water four days ahead; it came half a day late at the height she gave. The council voted seven to three, one away, to ask the stranger to weigh the old weights after the harvest with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. The master of the potters fired a nest of eleven new clay standards, unmarked. Traders from the Aldermark and a mate off a southern coaster had scales read. The boat people left the channels at the lowest tides in autumn and spring; the yard says they go to the Maun Stair. Old tellings in the line: Hale the Raker, the drowned larger god at the Maun Stair, the list-singers.

The sky: told by the town, at the gate and the landing, that the blue light dimmed twice in the first month of autumn and once in the dead of winter, each time for about an hour in the middle of the night. The town says it has seen such nights in other years. Whether the god saw any of them itself is not set.

Encounters: Minor: the ship-owner's runner with silver, refused and read last; the upriver traders; the coaster's mate. In the sixth month a bonded man reached for the nest at the landing; the god's hand was there first; the council set a price in measure, unclaimed. Major, held: the Stallreeve at the gatepost with the council's invitation and half a split stick, in the high summer.

Messages: none sent, none received.

Rulings this year: a true scale, ledger L-0031 (canon.md).

Deaths of note: none of anyone it knew by name. The winter fever took old and very young in the ordinary way.

Map: the marsh country it stands in, heard named the Reedmouths; the town, seen; the Bank Way, walked and named; the Old Rudd Way, named; rumors by their tellers: the Maun Stair, Ruddfold, Hask, the Aldermark.

Open at year's end: see threads.md, all items.
