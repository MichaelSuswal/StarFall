# Year by year

Newest year at the top. Only what the Weigher knows.

## Year 004

Told as far as the tenth month, the fourteenth day, at the gatepost before the gate opens. One moment of the year is held there; the rest of the year is entered here when it is played and the year past it is told. The clock has run on through Year 5 and is closed after the Year 5 night; the Weigher's own Year 5 has not begun.

Season opened: early autumn.

The god: Told at the Year 6 morning (`releases/005/dm.md`; `years/006/morning-story.md`). On the second day of the first month, a market and the ring's day, at the Assay-master's asking, it called the eleven new pieces in its pans: 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 84, 96, 120, 144, 168 and 180, each to a hair. It kept the gate on market mornings and the landings on the other dawns, and its nights at the landing by the channel folk's lamp. All that first month it read the stallholders' pieces against the eleven, and it read the scales of a pair of old bargains and said nothing of the bargains. It gave an old man of the flats its standing answer on whether it goes now the weights are set. On the twentieth day of the fourth month it stood witness to the lease of a weir for a pair of netmaker households and said what it saw and heard, using no power. On the eighth day of the fifth month it told a netmaker, in its decided words, that it cannot tell the height of the water as the tide-reckoner does. On the twenty-third it told the woman of the back houses, who came to the gate and asked nothing, that it has no power to cure a fever and that it is sorry about her child. It kept silent to a boy's "Is the star yours?" on the seventeenth day of the second month and to a girl's on the twentieth day of the eighth. It was not sent for by the ring. On the thirteenth day of the tenth month, told that the southern coaster was in, it did not go to her boat. No weight came into the air at any time. Its body is whole and it did not sleep. It read no bargain and no person, set no seal and gave no licence.

What it saw and was told: On the ring's day the Assay-master opened the chest before the yard and read the ring's word. From that market the old six are the town's weight and the eleven are what the market weighs by; the hall's ten fired pieces stand on the hall's bench and serve only to reckon bargains made before that day. Most stallholders' pieces read about a twentieth heavy, being copies of the hall's old pieces; a few read level; a pair were worse and were filed down. The Stallreeve ruled a pair of bargains made before the day by the ten, as made. In the second month the hill carriers came down and their scale read level; the old weigher of the hill kin did not come, and the head carrier said at the gate that Ruddfold has the ring's decision and that the old man had seen agreeing done and would not come down a third time. The kilns cracked loads as before. In the fifth month the Stallreeve gave out the tide-reckoner's word, and the Alder crested on the twelfth at the height she gave. On the seventeenth day of the sixth month the Stallreeve said at the post that the ring's winter sitting had set no word for the Weigher. In the ninth month the second year of the seat came round; the line talked of a year to the term, and the Stallreeve said the term is three years and a day; on the seventeenth he said the ring had spoken of the term, set no day to speak of it again but its first sitting after the grain, and asked nothing of the Weigher. The boy who asks about the star has been put to a boatwright at the far channels for the season. On the evening of the twelfth day of the tenth month a southern coaster came up the channel and tied up at the town's landing: the hull that lay three days at the town in the tenth month of Year 2, with the same master. On the thirteenth Bren told it at the fish landing that she was in, and that her master had asked the landing who sits in the Weightward's seat now and which is the town's weight, and had told the landing nothing. The Stallreeve said he had a pair of the watch on the gate as always and would put no more. The ship owner of the open water's coasters lay loaded at his landing, waiting on a wind. The blue light dimmed on the nights of the fifteenth of the second month, the twenty-second of the third, the ninth of the fourth and the nineteenth of the eighth, each seen from the landing; the year is not told to its end and no count is given. The boat people were gone from the channels about the ninth to the thirteenth of the first month and from the twelfth to the sixteenth of the eighth.

The people: As far as the fourteenth day of the tenth month. Through a winter with a hard spell and fever in the back houses, and an ordinary flood at the thaw. The line was twice its length through the first month, with every stall's pieces brought to be read against the eleven. The line counts the term of the seat.

Encounters: Minor, in the stretch: the ring's day; an old man of the flats; the old bargains at the gate; the hill carriers; the weir lease; a netmaker on the height of the water; the woman of the back houses at the gate; the children's question about the star. Major, held: the tenth month, the fourteenth day, a market day, first light, the gate not yet open. The master of the southern coaster stands at the head of the line, a brass balance in a leather case under her arm, one of her crew a pace behind her. She has said nothing yet in the Weigher's hearing.

Messages: none sent, none received.

Rulings this year: none of its own as far as told. The ruling released with this telling belongs to the kiln yard of Year 3 (see Year 003).

Deaths of note: none told.

Map: nothing changed. It went nowhere it had not been.

Open at this point of the year: see threads.md.

## Year 003

Told whole, at the Year 6 morning. (An earlier line here read: "Told as far as the seventh month, the fourteenth day, at the door of the potters' hall." Both scenes held there were played on the Year 5 day, and the rest of the year was told at the Year 6 morning.)

Season opened: early autumn.

The god: At the first sitting after the grain, in the first month, the ring took its offer to weigh the six again. The Assay-master opened the chest before the head of the hill goods and the master of the potters. Its key was in the room, in its keeping, and was not turned. The six read as before: 48, 96, 192 in its pans; 384, 766 and 1,536 on the hall's beam with the Assay-master's hand on it. He read the fifth light by a hair at the first trial and said he had learned that one.

The first month, the fifth day, at the gatepost, played on the Year 4 day (rolling save, Year 4, section 1): it read a fish seller's balance and called it out, took up the half-stick of the ring's asking, and gave the keeper's word at the post, cut as said and mended before the split. The first stick is the town's word, with its carrier (m-0005). The second stick went up to the ring, neither yes nor no. It gave the carrier his instructions; he took three and would not promise a pair. It told the Stallreeve where it stands on the master of the smiths' ask.

From the gatepost to the potters' door, told at the Year 5 morning (`releases/004/dm.md`; `years/005/morning-story.md`). It kept the gate on market mornings and the landings on the other dawns. Every night at the landing it ran its thumbnail down the cups, smallest first, and then counted. From the sixth day of the first month it asked at its landing dawns for large stones and weighed each on the landing's beam with the owner watching, saying only what it is; thirty have come to the beam. In the second month it weighed the old hill weigher's stones at the gate, told a young carrier that it cannot be killed and did not answer what would break it, and on the fourteenth day, with the Stallreeve's leave, walked out to the Flats and weighed a basket at the pans of the raking household: clean salt. In the third month it told a young stallholder what the Reading does, and the rest of it when he asked further. On the eighteenth day of the fourth month an old net-maker's door-stone read 3,011 against its own reckoning of 3,072 for the seventh; it said what the stone is and nothing more, and told the Stallreeve and the Assay-master first. It checked six scales the Assay-master had passed and stood witness to the lease of a boatyard, using no power. In the fifth month it told an old woman of the back houses that it has no power to cure a fever. It was not sent for at the ring's winter sitting. It kept silent to a boy's "Is the star yours?", to the Stallreeve's question of what it would have cut for the carrier now, and to a netmaker's question about the height of the water. On the fourteenth day of the seventh month, the gate done, it walked to the potters' hall with the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper. No weight came into the air at any time. Its body is whole and it did not sleep. On it: the scales, the nest, the half-sticks and the old keeper's key.

From the potters' door to the evening of the fourth day of the eighth month, played on the Year 5 day (rolling save, Year 5, sections 1 and 2; `player/decisions-005.md`). At the door it asked the master of the potters for leave to weigh each piece of the third nest green and again after the kiln, and for three spares of each size; she took both on her own terms. On the fourteenth day of the seventh month it weighed the twenty-two green in its pans, and on the twenty-fifth the thirty-three spares, each called and cut by the Tally-keeper. At her asking it held the green figures unsaid until the kiln was opened. On the first day of the eighth month, at the kiln yard, all fifty-five pieces came out whole; it weighed each again and called only what the pan gave, and said nothing of the loss. The master broke the fifth spare of each size. It weighed the halves together and then each alone, and said how much of each piece is clay and how much is not clay, sand and shell; the halves together gave what each piece gave whole, with nothing gathered in one half more than the other (the figures are in `releases/005/dm.md` and in the Tally-keeper's cuts). It said it does not say which clay is better or whether the kiln did well. On the second day, at the gate, it answered the Stallreeve's question of the sixth month: nothing more of its own is to be cut for the carrier, the second stick is the ring's to cut or leave, and it said what it would have the carrier told. On the fourth day the Assay-master said at the hall door, before the yard, that eleven of the new pieces sit level with the old six and with one another on his beam, and that the ring's day stands.

From the evening of the fourth day of the eighth month to the year's end, told at the Year 6 morning (`releases/005/dm.md`; `years/006/morning-story.md`). It kept the gate and the landings. On the eleventh day of the eighth month it told a digger of the lower pit, in its decided words, that it does not say which clay is better or whether the kiln did well, and gave Orrin, from the Tally-keeper's cuts, the figures of the sixes and the twelves before the fire and after. It was not sent for at the ring's sitting of the sixteenth day of the ninth month. On the seventeenth it answered the head of the hill goods, who asked as himself whether it would cut the six things for the carrier now, in its words of the second day. On the third day of the tenth month it read the loads of both coasters at the open-water landing. On the eighth day of the eleventh month it answered an upriver trader of the Aldermark, in its decided words, on whether one of the hall's old weights is light. It heard the carrier's account on the twentieth day of the twelfth month and said nothing. It kept silent to a boy's "Is the star yours?" on the eighth day of the eighth month.

What it saw and was told: The ring voted the old six the town's weight, from the first market of the next autumn, with bargains made before then reckoned by the present pieces, and the master of the potters to fire a nest to the old six in spring. The Stallreeve told the yard from the step. The seventh seat stays as it is. Told at the Year 5 morning: the old weigher's six stones read 48, 96, 193, 384, 768 and 1,536, and his grandfather's saying is that the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, which he would not stand behind. The Assay-master said of the door-stone that it was never cut; four people tried to buy it; the yard says "sixty-one short". On the seventeenth day of the sixth month the Stallreeve brought the ring's answer on the second stick to the post: six things taken as the town's word, and a pair not taken, that the fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so, and that any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read. He gave one reason, for the captain's scale: a scale is read at the gate, like any, and the hall is for the town's. The Assay-master said at the post that the fifth is light by a hair on his own beam and that he would read it so again. The ship owner of the open water said the ring's answer changes nothing he carries; his coasters go out in the first days of the tenth month. The master of the potters said at the post, on the fifth day of the seventh month, that the third nest is shaped and drying on its boards. The blue light dimmed on the night of the twenty-second day of the first month, on a pair of nights in the fifth month, and on the night of the seventh day of the seventh month, each seen from the landing. Told at the Year 6 morning: from the fifth day of the eighth month the Tally-keeper kept both sticks on her table and read them aloud to anyone who asked, figure by figure; about forty asked in the month. The master of the smiths had the fifteens read, nodded once and asked nothing. Orrin worked the loss out aloud to the line: a thirteenth off the whole, and not the same thirteenth twice; nothing of that was cut, and the yard has "a thirteenth" by heart. On the seventeenth day of the ninth month the Stallreeve brought the ring's word to the post: the hall's paying for the spares taken as the hall's cost, seven for, three against, one not voting; a motion that the ring itself cut a second stick for the carrier, of the six things it took in the winter, not carried, five for, five against, one not voting; one reason given, that a stick cut on a tied vote is a word the ring is not agreed on. On the twenty-ninth the Stallreeve told the ship owner of the open water what the Weigher would have the carrier told; he said that was on his stick, and that the pair of things he would not promise stands as he said it in the first month. His coasters went out past the Horn on the tide of the fourth day of the tenth month. No southern coaster came up to the town that summer. The grain came in in the twelfth month, and the potters' hall fired sets of pieces for the stalls. The coasters came back on the evening of the nineteenth, and on the twentieth the carrier gave his account at the post (messages.md, m-0005). The blue light dimmed on the night of the thirtieth day of the seventh month and the night of the sixth day of the eighth. Seen from the landing, it dimmed on six nights of Year 3 in all: one in the first month, a pair in the fifth, a pair in the seventh and one in the eighth. The boat people left the channels at the lowest tides twice in the year, in the second and eighth months.

The people: As far as the fourteenth day of the seventh month. Through the winter, with the fever in the back houses lighter than the year before; the thaw come and the Alder rising to an ordinary flood. The yard heard the word given and mended and was proud and ashamed of it together. The landings have its figure for the seventh by heart. The raking household of the Flats was looked at and its salt read clean. (A paragraph stood here that gave the town as it stood at the turn into Year 4, from the older telling of a Year 3 resolved to its end with the ring's asking unanswered. It is set aside: that turn has not been reached.) To the year's end, told at the Year 6 morning: fifty-five whole from one kiln was a thing each trade accounted for in its own way. The grain came in. ## Year 002

Told whole, at the Year 4 morning. The year was resolved again from the evening of the weighing, on what was played on the Year 3 day; where the paragraph headed "Told at the Year 4 morning" below differs from the paragraphs above it, that paragraph is what happened, and the earlier ones are kept as they were told.

Season opened: early autumn.

The god: The played day stood at the held moment of Year 1, late in the eleventh month. It said yes to the council's asking before the yard and spoke its terms aloud (scene 1, rolling save, Year 2, section 1). It read a stallholder's scale out and then level, and gave its standing answers at the gate (section 2). It asked and had leave for the split reading-sticks (scene 2, section 3). It went to the hall door at dusk (section 4). At the door, on its handed-off words, it gave the Assay-master its five answers, among them "Yes" to whether it is more than a man with good brass, with the rest promised for after the weighing; he asked three things more, and it kept silent on each. It told the Stallreeve his rule on the sticks is a good one, asked the master of the potters to bring the new nest to the hall, and asked Bren's household once about the low-tide trips. In the first month of Year 2 it weighed the hall's old weights before the eleven on its handed-off terms, the three bronze in its own pans and the three stone on the hall's beam with its scales in its hand, and the potters' nest of eleven after them. It took no fee and no gift. It kept silent to the master of the smiths, to the old weigher of the hill kin and to a boy on the step. Its scales gave it nothing in the six that does not read true. It sensed no other like itself. Its body is whole and it did not sleep. It has told no one it is a god.

What it saw and was told: The Assay-master has nothing to tell yet of the old Weightward's death. The old weights are kept in a chest in the hall, in the room under the old keeper's loft; the Assay-master holds the second key and the old keeper's key hangs in the ring. The eleven took its terms whole, eight to three. The rack is cut for seven weights and held six; the seat for the largest is empty, and clean where the others are dusty; the Assay-master said he found it so after the old keeper's laying out and had told only the Stallreeve, the evening before. The six, in its ones: 48, 96, 192, 384, 766, 1,536; the fifth is chipped at its foot and was read level and then light by the Assay-master. Four of the hall's fired pieces go down against the smallest: the hall's pieces run heavy by about a twentieth. The nest of eleven: 13, 25, 37, 52, 75, 89, 98, 130, 150, 179, 181. The stones of the old weigher of the hill kin agree with the old six; he says the hill kin have long supposed the hall's pieces heavy. The ring carried the clay head's motion, nine to one: no weight is named the town's until the seventh is found or given up and the nest fired again; the market stands on the hall's present pieces; debts stand as made; nothing in the hall is filed, mended, replaced or carried out; the chest is opened only before two of the eleven; the matter comes back at the first sitting after the next grain. The ring agreed that the old keeper's loft be looked in by three. Bren's household would say what the town says of the low-tide trips: the salt-rakers say the water folk go to the Maun Stair, the channel folk say to meet kin, and none has followed or knows.

Told on the Year 3 played day. The evening of the weighing, in the hall, played (rolling save, Year 3, sections 1 to 3): asked "What are you?" by the Assay-master, it told him it is a god, that others came down when it did and it has felt none, and what its scales give; that there is more to it, which it would not list; that it did not take the seventh weight and never touched the chest. It answered him on the key, on the seventh (it cannot find it; it can weigh a piece brought to it) and on the beam. It asked him to keep it between them until he had decided what the ring should hear; he would not hold it alone. At its asking the chest was locked before the Stallreeve, the empty seat left unwiped. It told the Stallreeve itself, with the Assay-master beside it. The Stallreeve keeps the gate as it was, says nothing in the yard, and carried the Assay-master's asking for a sitting round that night. It means to come to that sitting and say it first, and has asked to say it at the foot of the ring with the yard let in. It sent a word to the old weigher at Ruddfold by the next carrier: his stones weighed at the gate for a pinch.

The autumn after, as it stood before that evening was played: on the third day after the weighing the Assay-master, the Stallreeve and the clay head went up the old keeper's loft stair and came down within the hour, quiet; nothing of what they saw has been said in its hearing. The gate went on with its settled answers. The hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way in the second month with the figures of the hall's weights already in hand; one asked about Hask; their scale read a little out in their favor and they mended it. The Stallreeve went among the boatmen at the landing one evening; it did not hear what was said. The boat people were gone at the lowest tides of the second month and came back. It saw the blue light dim on one night of the second month, from the landing. With the Stallreeve's and the Tally-keeper's leave, on a day without a market in the last week of the second month, it went to the diggers' pits on the clay banks and weighed before diggers, kiln-masters and potters. From loads that fired well: 96 (91 clay, 5 not), 104 (99, 5), 88 (84, 4). From loads that cracked: 100 (96, 4), 92 (88, 4), 108 (102, 6). What was not clay was sand and shell. Orrin and a kiln-master of the west kilns said the two sets do not look different; it said it had then told them nothing. In the third month: its settled answer to "Is this clay clean?"; the first stale stick, shown by the Tally-keeper; two pans carved on scale beams and scraped off by the Stallreeve's word; Orrin began firing a test tile of each load, and the east kilns followed. The fourth month, the landing at dusk, played (rolling save, Year 3, sections 4 and 5): the ship owner of the channels offered a measure of salt for every boat read at his landing at dawn, at his landing and no other. It refused "no other" and put back its own terms, and he took them: the load set out and called, at any landing whose owner asks, his first and standing, a pinch for a boat from the captain, the gate kept. Set before the Stallreeve, who added that there is no reading on a market morning anywhere but the gatepost. The sticks for the boats are to be asked of the Tally-keeper.

Winter and spring, as they stood before the hall and the landing were played: in the fifth month a hard spell; the Stallreeve gave the line the tide-reckoner's word that the Alder would crest over the lowest paths in four days, and it did, at her height and a few hours off. The winter fever was lighter. Its settled answer to the net-mender. In the last week of the sixth month it stood witness to a boat sale between two households of boatwrights, with a pair of the watch, and said what it saw and heard. It saw the blue light dim on one night of the sixth month. In the seventh month, its settled answer on a split reed island; the master of the potters fired the nest again, twenty-two pieces, and chose eleven by the gate's readings that agree to a hair, kept at the potters' hall. The test tiles do not tell. The eighth month: the spring flood, ordinary; the boat people gone at the lowest tides and back. The council sat after the sowing and voted to offer the seat, six for and four against with one away on the Gulf; the Assay-master spoke for it. The ninth month, at the gatepost, played (rolling save, Year 3, section 6): the Stallreeve laid down the eleven's offer of the Weightward's seat, a term of three years and a day, the Assay-master's hand on the beam beside its own, the eleven's right to put the seat down at any sitting that three of them call. It read the scale in its hand (out, heavy on the buying side by about one part in thirty), took up the half-stick and said yes before the yard, saying first that it is a god. Its own terms, spoken: it keeps the standards and does not set the town's weight; it does not wait for the seventh, and the rack stays as it is; the chest is never opened by it alone; the old keeper's key is taken before the ring and goes back at the term's end; no fee; the gate stays. To the yard: it is the Weightward, for a term, not their god, and nothing here is its own. The Tally-keeper matched the halves and said the terms back; the Stallreeve said the key is taken in the ring; the Assay-master said to expect to be argued with at the beam. How the yard and the ring took it is not yet told.

It saw the blue light dim on one night of the ninth month. In the first month of summer a coaster of the southern harbor towns came up the main channel to the town's own landing; her master is a woman of about forty with a mate and about a dozen hands; a boatman said she is out of Corrith. She traded some days, a man of her crew watched the readings one market morning, and she asked the Stallreeve where to have her scale read. Held, fourth: the tenth month, at the gate, the coaster's master at the head of the line with a ship's balance.

Told at the Year 4 morning, the year as resolved again from the evening of the weighing (`releases/003/dm.md`; `years/004/morning-story.md`). The first month: the Stallreeve carried the Assay-master's asking round that night and named no matter; no sitting was called on it, and it went to the winter sitting. So the word was held by the Assay-master and the Stallreeve alone through the autumn and most of the winter. The autumn stands as told, with one thing more: from the first market after the weighing the Assay-master stood at the gate on market mornings, a little way off, arms folded, watching its hands, its face, and what it did with a reading that went against someone who could hurt it; the Stallreeve looked at it each dawn as a boatman looks at weather; neither spoke of the evening. Its word for the old weigher went up the Old Rudd Way with the hill carriers on a stick the Tally-keeper cut (m-0002). The fourth month: the landing bargain as played, struck with a man who did not know what it is. At the next market the Tally-keeper said yes to sticks for boats, with additions of her own (canon.md, the people's code), and took the ship owner's gift of a measure of salt a month as the hall's. From then on it read boats at his landing at dawn on days with no market; the ship owner of the open water asked for a dawn in the fifth month, and a pair of smaller owners and Bren's household in the sixth. Some captains would not show a load; it said it was not shown. Early in the sixth month, at the ship owner's landing, one of his own boats came in from the salt flats with raked salt in baskets, about five parts salt and one part sand and grit under a clean top; it called each basket before the captain, the landing man and the landing; the ship owner came down and said, "Read the rest." Heard afterward: the baskets came so from one raking household with a thin season; the Stallreeve ruled short measure and not theft; the household made good the sixth, the captain was docked half and the ship owner bore the rest in the open; the captains now turn out every fifth basket unasked.

The ring's winter sitting, the middle of the sixth month: the yard was let in, nine for it, with the head of salt and the master of the smiths against. Standing at the foot of the ring it said first what it is, a god; that others like it came into this country when it did and it has felt none of them; that it does not know where it came from or whose child it is; what its scales give; that there is more, answered yes or no if asked; "I weigh, and they decide"; "I do not ask to be Weightward and I do not refuse it yet." At the Assay-master's asking it said before the ring that it did not take the seventh weight and never touched the chest; he said only that it was said to him in the hall and that he asked. An old salt-raker woman said, "The Maun Stair." A child asked through its mother whether it is the one that drowned. The head of reed and fish asked what else it is. The head of salt asked whether it can tell when a man is lying: it would not say no, said what the Reading does and does not do, and that it has used it on no one in the town and will not without their asking; he said no bargain in the town is safe from it, and that he and his people will not ask. It kept silent to the master of the smiths ("Can you be killed? What would it take to break you?") and to a woman of the back houses ("Can you cure a fever?"). The landing readings stand. The hall was cleared and the ring sat on alone; it learned as the town did that the ring voted to have the seventh looked for in every way open to the town and set its sitting on the seat for after the sowing. At the next market the line was twice as long and nobody bought fish until noon; the Stallreeve put eight of the watch at the post and sent six home by midday; Bren said she had fed it first and would again; the old of the flats took bread out toward the Maun Stair at the next low tide. In the last week of the month it stood witness to the boatwrights' sale. The looking for the seventh: four of the hall's officers through the hall's rooms for three days; the Stallreeve and the Assay-master to the old keeper's house in the seventh month; the Stallreeve at every landing through the spring; nothing brought to the ring. Spring stands as told.

The ninth month, at the gatepost, as played: the ring that voted the seat had heard it in the winter, and the yard was hearing it a second time. A boy cried "Weightward" and the whole line took it up, the channel folk and the potters loudest, until the Stallreeve put up his hand; the salt-rakers stood silent; nobody called a sitting to put the seat down. How the ring divided, heard in the line: for the seat, the Assay-master, the clay head, the master of the potters, the head of reed and fish, the master of the boatwrights and the ship owner of the channels; against, the master of the weavers, the head of salt, the master of the smiths and the head of the hill goods. In the first days of the tenth month it took the old keeper's key from its peg before the ring, and the Tally-keeper cut it; the ring's rule for the chest is that any pair of the ring may stand for it, one of the pair to be of the four who voted against the seat. The coaster's master at the gate, resolved on its handed-off words: her balance read level and it took a pinch of barley; she said each southern house reads its scales against its own weights, that the council in Corrith keeps a set for quarrels and nobody swears it is what it was, and that what a southern captain wants to know is which is the town's weight, the old stones or the clay; it gave no answer for the town. Asked who it works for, it gave its settled words. Asked plainly whether it had said so at the post, it told her yes, it is a god; she said, "Where I come from we say a god ashore is a landlord. You take barley." It does not leave the gate; she gave no name; her coaster lay three more days and went down the channel. At dusk that day, asked whether the town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast, the Stallreeve said, "Not this summer. Ask us after the grain sitting." After the coaster had gone the Assay-master told it, at its one asking, what he saw in the loft: the old man's coat and boots hanging on a peg, stiff with dried river silt to above the knee, unmoved, and nothing else out of its place; the ring has had it since the winter; he does not know what it means. The eleventh month: a second day at the pits, wanted by the diggers, with lumps of both kinds and a tile from each; every lump much alike, about a twentieth part sand and shell; the quarrel keeps. In the last ten days of that month, at the lowest water, the Stallreeve, twelve of the watch and the boatmen of a pair of households dragged the Alder below the hall's landing and the reaches either side; it saw it from the landing and the gate; nothing that was a weight came up. Upriver traders had scales read and went home with the story that the hall had been found heavy and had said so. A salt-raker's pinch of salt was taken as the fee. The grain came in. It saw the blue light dim on one night of the eleventh month and one of the twelfth. The ring did not sit in the twelfth month; its sitting on the weight was set for the first market of the new autumn.

The people: At the year's turn, fed and not afraid; the grain in, well enough. The market is louder than a year ago and no worse. Easier about the gate and worse about the hall. Split about the stranger, and waiting: the channel folk, the potters and the clay head speak well of the gate; the heads of hill goods and of salt, the master of the smiths and the master of the weavers do not. The reading-sticks are in use. (As told at the Year 3 morning. For how the town stood toward it after the winter sitting and the seat, see the paragraph above and Year 003.)

## Year 001

Season opened: early autumn.

The god: Landed before first light on an old clay bank about a mile from a town and walked in unannounced on a market day (scene 1, rolling save sections 1 to 2). At the Assay-master's bench it weighed a disputed load of salt and then the hall's ten small pieces, and the bench changed its ruling (scene 2, rolling save sections 3 to 4). It called itself a weigher, the Weigher, its other name lost. It read scales in the yard for a fee in measure, listened in the market and ate with Bren's people (rolling save section 5). At dusk it met the Assay-master with the door shut: it told him what its scales tell it and showed it again, offered to weigh the Weightward's old weights before the council as his offer, kept silent on five questions, and promised nothing. For the rest of the year it stood at the gatepost and read scales in the order of the line, refused silver, ruled on nothing, and kept silent on what its decided words did not answer. It sensed no other like itself in anyone who came near all year. Its body is whole and it carries all it came with, the fees of a few days, and a clay cup.

The people: At the year's end, fed, housed and not afraid; the grain in. The market quarrels less, since a quarrel of weight can be settled at the gate in an hour. The watch is a few men larger. The town is split about the stranger: the channel folk, the salt-rakers and most potters for it; the heads of hill goods and of salt and the master of the smiths against a stranger's brass setting a measure. Told to it at landing by no one; numbers were not given to it.

What it was told: The Stallreeve runs the market and the watch, set two of the watch at the gatepost to keep the line, and from the second month brought quarrels of weight to be read and ruled on them himself. The hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way in the second month. Potters weighed diggers' clay by the fixed basket from the third month; by summer the yard said the scale is no use for clay. Tokens saying "read at the gate" were hung on scales and taken down by the Stallreeve in the fourth month. The Tally-keeper cuts a split stick of the gate's readings and keeps it. An apprentice of the hall brought fourteen scales in a month of the winter, a few with something small wrong. An old woman of the flats called the midwinter water four days ahead; it came half a day late at the height she gave. The council voted seven to three, one away, to ask the stranger to weigh the old weights after the harvest with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. The master of the potters fired a nest of eleven new clay standards, unmarked. Traders from the Aldermark and a mate off a southern coaster had scales read. The boat people left the channels at the lowest tides in autumn and spring; the yard says they go to the Maun Stair. Old tellings in the line: Hale the Raker, the drowned larger god at the Maun Stair, the list-singers.

The sky: told by the town, at the gate and the landing, that the blue light dimmed twice in the first month of autumn and once in the dead of winter, each time for about an hour in the middle of the night. The town says it has seen such nights in other years. Whether the god saw any of them itself is not set.

Encounters: Minor: the ship-owner's runner with silver, refused and read last; the upriver traders; the coaster's mate. In the sixth month a bonded man reached for the nest at the landing; the god's hand was there first; the council set a price in measure, unclaimed. Major, held: the Stallreeve at the gatepost with the council's invitation and half a split stick, in the high summer.

Messages: none sent, none received.

Rulings this year: a true scale, ledger L-0031 (canon.md).

Deaths of note: none of anyone it knew by name. The winter fever took old and very young in the ordinary way.

Map: the marsh country it stands in, heard named the Reedmouths; the town, seen; the Bank Way, walked and named; the Old Rudd Way, named; rumors by their tellers: the Maun Stair, Ruddfold, Hask, the Aldermark.
