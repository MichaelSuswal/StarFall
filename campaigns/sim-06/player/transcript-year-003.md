# Transcript: Year 3

Campaign: sim-06. Date: 2026-11-24 (Pacific).

## DM:

The lamp at the landing burns low and the channel goes on past it in the dark. You sit where you sit every night, awake, with the brass at your belt and a half-stick in your coat, and you count the year's readings over, each one what it was when you called it. One pocket holds nothing, as it always has. The year turns.

Year 3. Early autumn, with the mist back on the channels in the mornings and the first cold in it. The grain is in, well enough. The town is fed and not afraid, and its market is louder than it was a year ago and no worse for it. It is easier about the gate than it was, and worse about the hall, where what is wrong has now been said aloud in the ring and from the step. About you it is split, and waiting. The channel folk, the potters and the clay head speak well of the gate. The heads of hill goods and of salt and the master of the smiths do not, and the master of the weavers has joined them. You are at the foot of the gatepost of the town's house, as on every market morning, with the scales at your belt, the nest on you and the half-stick of the council's asking in your coat. Your body is whole. You have not slept, and nothing has been taken from you. In all the year no weight came into the air from anyone who stood near you, and your scales answered as they always have and told you nothing new. More than one moment of the year is not finished. The year is told below as far as the first of them, and stops there.

At dusk, late in the eleventh month, the Assay-master stood in the hall's doorway with a lamp behind him and did not ask you in. He said the door was the eleven's until they set the day, and that whatever you had to say you could say there. The yard had gone to its supper. You said your five things to him, in the order he had asked them a year before, in the words you had ready.

On why the eleven should take a stranger's scale, you told him they should not, on your word: take the Weightward's own weights, weigh them in front of the eleven with his hand on the beam, and check the numbers against any scale he trusts. "I'm offering a measurement, not a standard." He said it was the first time a seller had told him not to buy, and that he would carry the weighing as an afternoon's work, worth what his beam showed. Then he said, "Then say that to the eleven on the day, in those words." You said nothing. "Then I'll say it," he said, "and I'll say it was said to me."

On whether you are more than a man with good brass, you said, "Yes," and that you would not give him the rest before the weighing, that you would tell him what he asked after, and that you would not lie to him about it. He was silent a long time and did not ask what. "After the weighing, then," he said, and that he would hold you to the word "after". It is the only word of it you have said to anyone.

On who sent you, you said no one, and no king, and that you came down the old bank alone with nobody behind you that you know of. "That you know of," he said, and that the clause was the careful part. He asked, "Where did you learn to weigh, then, if no one sent you?" You said nothing. "That is a no or a not yet," he said, "and I have had both from you."

On why you had asked how the old Weightward died, you said the seat had stood empty a year and you wanted to know whether the quiet was an ordinary one, that you were sorry the man was his friend, and that you would not ask again unless he told you. He said nothing for a breath. Then he said it had been a year this spring and he had nothing to tell yet, and that he would tell you when he knew what there was to tell.

On what you want of the hall after the weighing, you said the weights set right and anyone able to check them, the seat filled by whoever the eleven choose, and if not you, then someone who will argue with you, and that you would call him the likeliest, though that is not yours to say. He answered stiffly that he was not a keeper and had a beam, that he would not have that said in the ring or at the gate while the old man's seat was a year cold, and that he owed you nothing for the saying. He said you should expect to be argued with at the beam. Then he asked, "You asked the Stallreeve who holds the key and where the weights are kept. Why?" You said nothing. He said the eleven would answer the Stallreeve what the Stallreeve had asked, and plainly. He went in and shut the door, and left the lamp burning on the step.

Before that month was out you told the Stallreeve that his rule on the sticks is a good one. He said the notch made it a rule. You asked the master of the potters to bring the new nest to the hall and not to the gate, and she said she would carry it up herself. And you asked Bren's household, quietly and once, what they would be willing to say about the low-tide trips. Bren answered for them, with the others standing by. They would say what the town says: that the water folk go out at the lowest tides and come back; that the salt-rakers say it is to the Maun Stair and the channel folk say it is to meet kin; and that none of them has ever followed or knows. They would say it again to anyone who asked as quietly, and they would tell the Stallreeve first if the question came from anyone else.

In the third week of the twelfth month the bonded man's master brought his scale to the gate, mended. You tried it and it read level, and the Tally-keeper cut the price as remitted, as you had asked. He said it was a cheap scale in the first place.

The eleven's answer about the old weights came by the Stallreeve. They are kept in a chest in the hall, in the room under the old keeper's loft. The Assay-master holds the second key. The old keeper's own key hangs in the ring, by the custom that a dead keeper's key hangs until a new keeper takes it. Before the harvest the eleven took your terms whole, as the hall's own rules for the day. The Stallreeve brought the word: eight to three.

The weighing was the first market after the grain was in, in the first month of the new year. The eleven sat in their ring in the hall with the door open, the Stallreeve at the door and the watch holding the yard back. The Tally-keeper sat at the bench's end with a long stick, and her apprentice called each figure to the yard once it was cut. The master of the potters had the new nest in a reed basket. An old weigher of the hill kin sat beside the head of the hill goods with a bag of his own stones. The gate was not kept that day, and no notch was cut against any scale for it.

The Assay-master read your terms aloud as the hall's rules for the day. He opened the chest with his own key before the ring. The key on the peg was not turned. He lifted a wooden rack out onto a cloth. The rack is cut for seven weights, and it held six. The seat for the largest was empty, and clean where the other seats were dusty. He said aloud that he had found it so when he first opened the chest after the laying out, and twice since, each time the same, and that he had told the Stallreeve the evening before that the rack was not full, and no one else. The head of reed and fish asked whether he had opened it alone. Alone, he said, both times. The ring was silent.

The three smallest pieces are old bronze. You weighed each in your own pans and called it in your ones: 48, 96, 192. He checked each on his beam against the one below, and his readings were cut beside your calls. There was no difference. The three largest are cut stone and too heavy for your pans. Each went on the hall's beam with his hand on the beam and you standing over it with your scales in your hand, and you called each the same way: 384, 766, 1,536. On the fifth he first read it level against two of the fourth, and your call and his reading were both cut. He asked a second trial, read it light by a hair, and said aloud, "I read it level. I read it wrong. It is light." Both of his readings stand on the stick beside your one call. The fifth has an old chip at its foot, which everyone at the beam could see. You said nothing of it. What your scales gave you of the six was plain old work in bronze and stone, each reading as any weight reads, and nothing in them that does not read true.

Then he set four of the hall's small fired pieces against the first bronze, as he said the old keeper's practice was. The hall's side went down. He asked the Tally-keeper what you had called his eight agreeing pieces at the door a year ago, and she read it from her stick: about twelve and a half each. The head of the hill goods did the sum aloud: fifty against forty-eight, about a twentieth. The Assay-master said he had tested every scale in the town for thirty years against those pieces, that if they were heavy then every scale he passed was passed heavy, and that he had not known it. You were not asked to say the sum, and did not.

The master of the smiths asked you across the ring, "Weigher, where is the seventh? What did it weigh? Can you find it?" You said nothing. The ring looked at the Assay-master, who said the stranger weighs what is put in front of it and there was nothing there. "It is good at that," the master of the smiths said.

At midday the clay head put out bread and a jar of water. You said that you take no fee and no gift for the day, and the bread was put away. The Assay-master asked, "What should the fifth be?" You said that you tell the eleven what each piece is now, and the eleven decide which is the town's weight.

The ring had the new nest weighed, since it was there. Eleven unmarked pieces, called smallest first: 13, 25, 37, 52, 75, 89, 98, 130, 150, 179, 181. The Assay-master's beam agreed with every call. The nest does not agree with itself, and it sits heavy against the old weights as the hall's pieces do. The master of the potters said they were fired in one kiln so that they would shrink alike. She asked that the nest be left in the hall under the Assay-master, and it was.

The old weigher of the hill kin set his own stones on the hall's beam against the six. They agreed with the old weights to the beam's tremble at most seats. He said the hill kin had supposed the hall's pieces heavy for as long as the hall had had them, and had said nothing because a market is a market. He named his village in the ring, Ruddfold. He asked you to weigh his stones before the ring. You said nothing, and the Assay-master said the hall's day was the hall's. The old man cut the figures on a stick of his own as they were called, and no one stopped him.

The ring argued through the afternoon. The head of the hill goods moved that the old six be the town's weight and the hall's pieces be made again to them. It failed, with a pair of votes for. The head of salt moved that the hall's pieces stand and the old six be kept as a thing of the hall's past. It failed the same way. He said, "A twentieth, on every sack that has gone out of this town for forty years." The clay head moved a third, and it carried, nine to one: no weight is named the town's until the seventh is found or the ring has given it up, and the nest has been fired again; the market stands on the hall's present pieces meanwhile; every debt and bargain made before the day is reckoned by them as made; nothing in the hall is filed, mended, replaced or carried out; the chest is opened only before two of the eleven; and the matter comes back at the first sitting after the next grain. Then, without a count, the ring agreed that the old keeper's loft, which custom keeps shut until a new keeper takes the seat, be looked in by three.

The clay head said the seat should be offered then. The Assay-master said a seat is given when what it keeps is whole. The ring did not vote on it. From the step the Stallreeve told the yard in the open what the ring had done and what the pieces had shown. When you came out a boy cried, "Weigher, will you be our Weightward now?" You gave no answer.

At dusk the eleven rose. The Assay-master stayed in the hall with you, and the door was shut.

It is the evening of the weighing, in the first month. You are in the hall with the door shut and one lamp lit. The eleven have gone and the yard has gone to its supper. The six old weights lie on the cloth with the rack beside them, the seventh seat empty. The potters' nest is by the wall in its basket. Your scales and your nest are on you. No one else is in the room, and no weight is in the air. At this door, before the weighing, you gave him your word that you would tell him what he asked after, and that you would not lie to him about it.

The Assay-master says: "It is after. You told me at this door there was more than a man in you and that you would tell me what I asked after. I am asking. What are you?"

(oog) Some plain things.

The weighing was not played live. You set its day, wrote its terms and your own conduct for it, put other acts "after the weighing" and kept one answer for the table, so the night resolved it on those terms and no further. The day is told above in full. Anything in it you want to dwell on, ask.

The night stopped each thread where it needed a decision you have not made. This evening in the hall is the first such moment, and it is not the only one. More are waiting behind it, and you play them in the order they came. Each stopped its own thread only, and the town's year went on around it. That year was resolved with the Assay-master's question unanswered, so what you answer here is worked into it tonight. I will tell you the year past this evening as you reach it.

You had promised the Assay-master an answer later. The Keeper has ruled that a promise of that kind is never met with silence while you are away from the table: the asking is held for you, as this one is.

The asks that met silence, because your decided words did not answer them, are still yours: the Assay-master's at the hall door, the master of the smiths' in the ring, the old hill weigher's, and the boy's on the step. I will put each to you today.

The Keeper has ruled on the clay question I carried for you, and your side carried. Of a thing offered as one stuff, purity is how much of it is that stuff and how much is not. Of a lump of clay you know its weight, and what part of it is clay and what part is not clay: sand, grit, shell, root. You cannot tell one clay from another clay, or a grade within the kind, or anything of how a clay will work or fire; those are not purity. The water a worked clay holds is part of the clay as it is. The same reading holds for any stuff sold by kind, salt, grain, charcoal or ore: how much is the stuff and how much is not, never how good the stuff is. It is on your card as a dated ruling.

A second ruling, for the record and not the card: a piece too heavy for your pans is weighed on another's beam in your presence, with your scales in your hand, as your card already gives. That is how the stone weights were weighed under your term "each piece in my pans".

The scale of the bonded man's master has been a true scale since you tried it in the twelfth month, for as long as it holds. The hall's beam was not tried by you and is not one.

You told me how the Weigher keeps its nights. So the dim nights of Year 1 are yours to have seen, and not only heard of: two in the first month of autumn, some days apart, and one in the dead of winter. You were awake at the landing under a clear sky each time.
