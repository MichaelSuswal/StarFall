# Decisions, Year 3

Campaign sim-06. Handed off by the DM at the close of the Year 3 played day, 2026-11-24 (Pacific). Decisions only, in the order given. The player's words are quoted where they set the terms. All of them were played at the held moments of Year 2, from the first month to the tenth; the clock reads Year 3. Items 1 to 4 were played and stand as facts of Year 2. Item 5 is handed off unplayed. Items 6 to 12 are for the rest of Year 2 and for Year 3.

1. The Assay-master's "What are you?", the evening of the weighing, in the hall. Answered: "I am a god, Master." It told him others came down when it did and it has felt none of them; that it came down the old bank and does not know where from or whose child it is; what its scales give, and that it cannot give a false measure and can keep silent. "There is more to me than the scales, and I am not going to list it tonight." "If you ask me a question about a particular power, I will say yes or no, or I will say I will not answer. I will not say no to a yes." "I did not take the seventh weight. I never touched the chest. I did not know the rack was cut for seven until you lifted it out today, and I will say that in front of the eleven if you want it said." Of the seat, that evening: "It is not whole, as you said, and I will not take it half."
   - To his three questions. The key: "a weighing nobody can open is a promise with nothing behind it"; it had no other reason and did not know the rack was short. The seventh: it cannot find it; "If you bring me a piece and ask whether it is the seventh, I can tell you what it weighs"; "That is a weaker thing than finding it, and I will not call it more." What tells a god from a man on a beam: "Nothing." "Watch what I do when the true reading costs me." "I am not asking to be believed."
   - It asked him to keep it between them until he had decided what the ring should hear. He refused (r-0011). At its asking the chest was locked before the Stallreeve and the empty seat left unwiped.

2. The Stallreeve told, the same evening, by the Weigher itself with the Assay-master beside it: "I'm a god, Stallreeve." "I ask nothing for it. If the ring wants me gone from the gate, I'll hear that and weigh my answer. I won't run off, and I won't change my reading for anyone's sake." It told him what it told the Assay-master and no more.

3. The ship owner of the channels, the fourth month, at the landing. "What I won't do: be yours alone." Its terms, which he took (r-0013) and which were set before the Stallreeve: at dawn the captain sets out the load, sack by sack or basket by basket, on the landing's beam or its own, and it calls weight, count, and how much is the stuff it is sold as; "If he won't show, I say I wasn't shown"; it reads at the landing of any ship owner who asks, on the same terms, on a day it is not at the gate; his landing has the first dawn and a standing place; the fee is a pinch for a boat, from the captain, weighed, as at the gate, and anything more "he can give it to the Tally-keeper for her sticks, not to me"; "If the two clash, the gate wins." "You'll want a nod from me that your boats are better than the rest. I won't give it."

4. The Weightward's seat, the ninth month, at the gatepost: yes, before the yard, the half-stick taken up. Said first: "I am a god. The Assay-master asked, and I told him, and the Stallreeve has known since the evening of the weighing." Its terms, spoken:
   - "I take the seat for three years and a day, as the eleven set it, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam beside mine, and with the ring's right to put it down at any sitting three of them call. I don't ask for that to be softened. A keeper the ring cannot put down is not a keeper, it is a lord."
   - "I keep the standards. I do not set the town's weight. I call what each piece is now, and the eleven say which is the town's."
   - "I will not wait for it to be found, because a hall without a keeper while it is looked for is a hall that keeps nothing. The rack stays as it is, with the seventh seat unwiped. If it turns up, it turns up in front of the ring."
   - "The chest is opened only before two of the eleven, as the ring said, and never by me alone. I take the old keeper's key off its peg in front of them, and when my term ends it goes back on the peg. I take no fee for the seat. The hall's pinch at the bench is the hall's, and I keep none of it."
   - "The gate stays as it is, market mornings at the foot of the post, and the Stallreeve's rules on sticks and tokens stand. The Assay-master tests, as he has always done, and I check what he has passed against my scales when he asks, not when I like."
   - To the yard, and as its answer to the yard's cry: "I am the Weightward, for a term. I am not their god, and nothing here is mine."

5. The master of the coaster, the tenth month, at the gate. HANDED OFF, NOT PLAYED: her words are the Keeper's. The Weigher's conduct and words, whole:
   - "I take her scale from her hand, the same as I'd take anyone's in the line, and I say nothing first. I read it against my brass, pan by pan, and I tell her what it does, which pan and how far, in my cups, and if it reads level I say so. I take the pinch of barley for it. I don't make the line wait on her, and I don't hurry her either."
   - Then three things, "and only these, quietly, so the yard has to lean to hear": "What do the southern harbors read their scales against, Master? Is there a measure in Corrith that the other harbor towns will take, or does each house keep its own? And what would a southern captain want to know about this town's weights before she signed to buy in them?" It tells her why it asks: it is keeper of the hall's standards for a term, and would like to know whether what the hall keeps can be read by anyone who comes up the channel.
   - If she asks about itself: "I am the Weightward of this hall for three years and a day. The scales tell me what is in the pans and what is weighed beside them while I stand near. I did not come from Corrith or Hask or any harbor, and I do not know where I came from. I will say yes or no to a plain question about a power of mine, or I will say that I will not answer, and I will not say no to a yes." It does not tell her what it is beyond that unless she asks plainly; "if she asks it plainly, I tell her, because the town already knows."
   - What it will not answer: anything about the chest, the missing seventh, or what the eleven argue. "Those are the town's, and she is a guest at the gate." It names no price on any good she carries and does not say what her goods are worth; it says what the pan reads of them if she sets them there. It does not say what the Aldermark, Hask or the hill kin think of her trade: "I don't know and I would not guess aloud to a stranger with a ship."
   - If she offers pay for a reading at her own harbor: it does not leave the gate; "her harbor may bring its scales here or send its own to be set against the hall's." If she offers a gift: no, "as I do to everyone."
   - Where she asks what these words do not cover, it keeps silent and the ask comes back to the player.

6. The sitting the Assay-master asked for on the evening of the weighing. The Weigher comes and says it first. "I'll ask to say it standing at the foot of the ring, with the yard allowed in, so nobody hears it from a neighbor. I'll tell the eleven what I told him, and I'll tell them the same terms I gave for the weighing: I weigh, and they decide." (Its words there on the seat, "I do not ask to be Weightward and I do not refuse it yet", were given for that sitting and are overtaken in the ninth month by item 4.)

7. Its other powers, standing words for anyone who asks. It does not volunteer the seals or the Reading. Asked about a particular power it says yes or no, or that it will not answer. "I do not need to be asked twice about the Seal." Asked whether it can tell who is lying: "I will not say no, because that would be a false word, and I will say what the Reading does and what it will not do, and I will tell him that I will not use it on anyone in this town without their asking."

8. The old weigher of the hill kin. A word sent to Ruddfold by the next carrier on the Old Rudd Way: "The Weigher will weigh your stones in front of any of the ring who want to see it, at the gate, any market morning. No fee but a pinch. Bring the stones in your own bag, and I'll call them in my own cups." With it: that the old weights and his stones agreed to the beam's tremble, and that it would like to hear what his people think the seventh was. "I ask nothing more."

9. The gate and the boats, for the rest of the year: "I keep the gate on market mornings, read the boats at the ship owner's landing on the other dawns." It asks the Tally-keeper, with the ship owner present, what sticks for boats would do to her count. "If she says no, I leave it." Its terms for those sticks, if she takes them on: the reading cut, which pan and how much, one half to the captain and one to the buyer if he wants one, holding for that day only.

10. The town's weight. At the first sitting after the next grain, when the matter comes back, it offers to weigh the six again before the ring. (The ring set that the matter comes back, not a second weighing; the weighing is its offer.)

11. The pits. "I say once to the diggers that I can come to the pits one more day if they want it, and that I still cannot say which clay is good. I leave it to them." A new decision, not a promise it had given.

12. Two asks of the town's officers, each made once.
   - The Assay-master, quietly: what the three of them saw in the loft. "If he will not say, I leave it."
   - The Stallreeve, in front of the Assay-master: "whether the town wants a keeper's word sent down the coast with this captain on what the hall's weights are and are not, in the plain terms I gave the eleven, and I do not send it without their say."

Standing at the close: no seal set, no licence given, the Reading not used. Promised: the seat on its terms; the sitting; the boats; the old weigher's stones.
