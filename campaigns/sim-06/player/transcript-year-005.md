# Transcript: Year 5

Campaign: sim-06. Date: 2026-11-26 (Pacific).

## DM:

The sticks are split and the word is out of your hands, and the line goes on to its end as it does every market morning. Night after night you sit awake at the landing by the channel folk's lamp and run your thumbnail down the cups, smallest first, before you count, and each figure is what it was when you called it. The count carries you through the mist and the rain and the hard cold to a mild afternoon with the river up, and it stops there, with a door in front of you and nothing yet weighed behind it. Somewhere past the marsh the year turns. Here it is still spring.

Year 3 still. The seventh month, the first month of spring in this country, the fourteenth day: a market day, early in the afternoon, mild, with the thaw off the hills and the Alder up over the lowest paths. The town has come through its winter. The fever was in the back houses again and took fewer than the year before. The yard heard your word given and mended at the post in the autumn, and was proud and ashamed of it together. The landings have your figure for the seventh by heart. It knows the ring said no to a pair of the things on your second stick, and it has been given a reason for only one of them. You are at the door of the potters' hall, with the gate done behind you, the scales at your belt, the nest on you, and in your coat the half-sticks and the old keeper's key. Your body is whole. You have not slept, and nothing has been taken from you. No weight came into the air at any time, from anyone who stood near you. What follows runs from the gatepost as far as this door, and stops here.

The gatepost stands as you played it, on the fifth day of the first month, and nothing in it was changed. After that day the line ran as before.

Every night at the landing you ran your thumbnail down the cups, smallest first, and then counted. The boatmen saw.

From the sixth day you asked at your landing dawns for large stones, in the words you had ready. They were brought: anchors, ballast, querns, door-stones, hearth stones. You weighed each on the landing's beam with its owner watching and said what it was. The Tally-keeper cut a stick for every one, unasked. The Stallreeve forbade wagers at the beam.

On the eighth day the fish seller came back with a mended cord, and his balance read level. On the night of the twenty-second day the blue light dimmed, as on the earlier nights, and you saw it from the landing. The next morning a boy at the gate asked, "Is the star yours?" Your decided words do not answer that. You said nothing, and the line told him to hush.

In the second month the hill carriers were in the town, and the old weigher of the hill kin with them. On the eighth day his stones were weighed at the gate, before the head of the hill goods, the Assay-master, the master of the smiths and the Stallreeve, the three largest on the Assay-master's own beam, which he had brought to the post for it. You called them: 48, 96, 193, 384, 768 and 1,536. Asked what his people say the seventh was, he gave a saying of his grandfather's, that the six weigh what is and the seventh weighs what is owed, and he would not stand behind it. He went home with the figures on his own stick.

On the eleventh day a young carrier asked whether you can be killed. You had given the words for that, so you said them: no, that you can be hurt, held or scattered, and that you come together again. He asked what would break you. You did not answer. The same day you asked the Stallreeve for the fourteenth, the lowest tide. He gave it, on his own terms: the household would be told the night before, and not come upon.

The fourteenth was a market day. You walked out to the Flats, a little over a mile and a half across the marsh, and were back before dusk. The post stood empty with a pair of the watch at it. Nobody was read and no notch was cut. At the pans the raking household turned out every basket before it was asked. You chose a basket and weighed it. It was clean salt, and nothing you were shown there was bulked. The head of the household put a pinch in your pan. You were told afterward that a boy had asked the empty post whether the star was its own, and that the watch had no answer to give. About those days the boat people were gone from the channels, as at every lowest tide of autumn, and they came back.

The third month was rain. The potters' hall's own beam was brought to the gate and read level. The master of the smiths brought a hearth slab to the landing's beam and asked you nothing, then or since. A young stallholder asked whether you can tell who is lying, and then whether a man you read would know. You had words for both, and said them: what the Reading does and does not do, and then the rest of it, that both pans show.

On the eighteenth day of the fourth month, at the fish landing, an old net-maker's door-stone read 3,011. Your own reckoning for the seventh is 3,072. You said what the stone read and nothing more. She asked whether it was the seventh, and whether you wanted her step for the hall. You said only what the stone is, and that you ask no one to give anything up. You told the Stallreeve and the Assay-master first, and the Stallreeve carried the figure to the eleven that dusk. The Assay-master went to look at it, and said afterward in your hearing that the stone was never cut. Four people tried to buy it. The Stallreeve forbade the buying of a stone at a landing for its weight. The yard says "sixty-one short". No other stone of the thirty that have come to the beam read within a twentieth of your figure.

On the twentieth day the Assay-master asked you to check six scales he had passed. A pair read level and four a hair out. On the twenty-fourth a pair of boatwright households asked you to stand witness to the lease of a boatyard. You stood, and said what you saw and heard. You used no power on it.

The fifth month brought a hard spell, and the fever. An old woman of the back houses asked at the gate whether you can cure a fever, for her daughter's boy. You said the words you had given: no, you have no power for it. The woman who asked at the winter sitting of the year before has not come to the gate. The blue light dimmed on a pair of nights that month, and you saw each from the landing.

On the fifteenth day of the sixth month the ring held its winter sitting with the door shut. You were not sent for. On the seventeenth, at the post, before the yard, the Stallreeve brought what the eleven had said to your second stick, thing by thing, with the count for each, and the Tally-keeper cut it.

Taken as the town's word:

- That the rack is cut for seven and the seventh is not in it, that the hall does not know what it weighed and has looked for it: eight for, none against, three not voting.
- The four fired pieces against the first bronze, and the sum, about a twentieth heavy: nine for, one against, one not voting.
- The chipped piece and the cracked piece against the eight: eight for, one against, two not voting.
- That the ten have not been weighed against the six since, and that the Tally-keeper has the figures: nine for, none against, two not voting.
- That the hall keeps no weight that anyone may not see weighed: ten for, one not voting.
- That the word go in the order you said it, with nothing added: ten for, one not voting.

Not taken:

- That the fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so: four for, five against, two not voting.
- That any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read: three for, seven against, one not voting.

He gave one reason, and it was for the captain's scale: a scale is read at the gate, like any, and the hall is for the town's. For the fifth he was given no reason and would not make one up. The Assay-master said at the post that the fifth is light by a hair on his own beam and that he would read it so again.

Then the Stallreeve asked you, as himself: what would you have cut for the carrier now, since the things the ring took are not on the stick the carrier holds? Your decided words do not answer that. You said nothing. Nothing more has been cut, and he has not asked again. The ship owner of the open water, asked in the open, said it changes nothing he carries. He still holds the carrier's half of your word. It has not gone: his coasters go out in the first days of the tenth month. Nothing has come to you from anyone outside the town.

Then the thaw. The Alder is rising to an ordinary flood. On the second day of the seventh month a netmaker at the gate asked, "Can you tell the height of the water as the tide-reckoner does?" You had no words for that and said nothing. He said you were good at that too. On the fifth day the master of the potters said at the post that the third nest is shaped and drying on its boards. On the night of the seventh day the blue light dimmed, and you saw it from the landing.

Today you read the line through the morning. When the last scale was read you walked to the potters' hall with the Assay-master at your left hand and the Tally-keeper behind, carrying a long stick. The line watched you go.

You are at the door of the potters' hall, a long low hall of reed and clay. The Assay-master is at your left hand. The Tally-keeper is behind you with her long stick under her arm. The master of the potters is in the doorway, clay to the elbows, and has not moved aside. Past her shoulder, in the hall, there are boards along a wall with unfired pieces standing on them in pairs, pale and dry, a beam with brass pans, and a bench under a window. No weight is in the air. None of your askings has yet been put to anyone.

She says: "Weightward. Assay-master. You are early for a firing. What do you want of my boards?"

(oog) Where the story stands against the clock. The clock reads Year 5, and the story stands in the spring of Year 3, on the fourteenth day of the seventh month. A year is told only as far as its first held moment, and this door is the first of the scenes you prepared for that spring, held for you to play. So your own Year 4, and your town's, has not begun: it begins on the night after the last held moment of Year 3 is played. The world beyond the town has gone on through Year 4, and none of that has reached you.

(oog) Some plain things.

What I told you yesterday as provisional is set aside, and the telling above stands in its place as far as it goes: the town at the turn into Year 4, that the open-water owner's coasters went out with no word, and the year's end in the closing of Year 3. As the story stands, the coasters have not gone.

Three asks met silence, because your decided words did not answer them, and they are still yours: the boy's "Is the star yours?", asked at the gate in the first month and again of the empty post in the second; the Stallreeve's question of what you would have cut for the carrier now; and the netmaker's "Can you tell the height of the water as the tide-reckoner does?" I will put each to you today. "What would break you" was refused by your own word and is not waiting.

Answered in the stretch by words you had already given, as told above: whether you can be killed, whether you can cure a fever, whether you can tell who is lying, and the follow-up on the Reading.

For the record: the pinch at the pans was taken as a fee, as at the gate. In the stretch you read no bargain and no person, set no seal and gave no licence. This is on your card as a dated entry.

The Flats are on your map as seen, since you walked there. Nothing else on it changed.

The thirty stones and their figures are in the record, each as you called it, if you want them read out.

No painting this morning. The paintings approved for this run are used, so the request is held and nothing was painted.

## Player:

I stop a pace short of the door, so I'm not standing in her light, and I say it plainly.

"Master. I want your leave to weigh your pieces before they are fired. Not to judge them. I told you at the gate I do not set the town's weight, and I do not set yours. I came to weigh each piece as it stands on your board, dry and unfired, and to weigh it again after the kiln, so that you and everyone else who fires a weight has the loss from the fire as a number and not a guess.

"This is how I'd do it, if you allow it. You or your hands lift each piece from the board and set it in my pan. I do not touch them. I call each in my ones, smallest first, and the Tally-keeper cuts the figure beside the piece. The Assay-master checks the two or three largest on your beam, with my scales in my hand, since the biggest are too heavy for my pans. Nothing leaves your hall without you saying so. When the kiln is drawn and cooled, we come back, I weigh the same pieces in the same order, and she cuts the second figure on the same stick, so anyone can read the two together.

"I also asked, when I spoke to you at the gate, for three spares of each size. If you've fired them, I'd like to weigh those too. One of each size I'd have broken, in front of you, so it can be seen inside. I don't ask for that to be done. I ask whether you'll allow it."

I don't walk in until she moves, or says to. If she says no, or only to some of it, I ask which part and I take what she gives. If she wants a pinch for her trouble, I give it to her as the hall's fee, not mine, and I weigh it in my pans while she watches.

To her question about her boards, I add one thing more. "I don't want to find your work wrong. I want to be able to say what it is. If a piece is off, I'll say which pan and how far, and I'll say it to you first, before the Assay-master or the yard. If they ask me after, I answer what is true."

I turn to the Assay-master and ask him, quietly, whether he would rather weigh first on his own beam and then I on mine, so no one says I have only his word for it. I will do it either way.

If she lets us in, I begin with the smallest piece on the first board, and I keep my hands at my belt until I am asked to take hold of anything.
