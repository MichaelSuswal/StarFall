

## DM:

She listens to the whole of it at her door, with the yellow light on the boards behind her.

"That will do," she says. "It is on the stick and not yours to cry. Say that to anyone, the ring too, and send them to me." Of the reading on the first: "It is my kiln. I'll be at it." She looks at the Tally-keeper, who nods at the stick under her arm, and at the Assay-master, who says, "I have seen it cut. I will see it read."

Of the breaking she says: "One of each size, as I said. Not green. A dry piece snapped tells me what any broken pot tells me, and I'll not lose eleven before the fire for it. Fired, on the first. I break them, I look first, and then they go in your pan. What is left is mine." She says it before you have touched anything, as you asked.

That night you are at the landing as on every night, with your thumbnail down the cups, smallest first, before the count.

The seventeenth is a market day, with the mud of yesterday's shower in the yard. When the line is read you ask the Stallreeve whether the watch needs to stand at the gate on the twenty-fifth and the first. He counts on his fingers. "Neither is a market. There is no line to keep on either, so there is nothing for the watch to do but lean. If she moves a day onto a market, tell me the day before, and you'll have the gate kept as you had it for the pans. Not a pair of markets running." You tell him what you told the master of the potters about the green figures. He says, "Then I'll not be the one who asks you for one," and goes to see about a stall.

(oog) He has asked you nothing more, and he will not ask twice. But his question of the seventeenth day of the sixth month is still yours, and this is the man it came from: what would you have cut for the carrier now, since the things the ring took as the town's word are not on the stick the open-water owner holds? The coasters go out in the first days of the tenth month. You stood in front of him today and said only what you chose to say, so nothing is read into it. If you have words for him, say when you give them.

Nobody at the gate or the landing asks you for a green figure in these days. The netmaker has not asked about the water again. Your words for him are kept for when he or another does.

The potters' hall is shut to you while she shapes. There is a shower on the twenty-first. The twenty-second is dry and bright. About the twenty-fourth the Alder comes off the lowest paths and begins to fall.

On the twenty-fifth, when your dawn at the landing is done, the three of you are at her door again and she is already at the bench. The spares stand on a third board, carried in from the kiln's floor, three of each size, pale and dry. The Tally-keeper has a second long stick. She marks each foot as before, with three scratches, four or five. The master sets each in your pan with her own hands, and you call it.

No stone in any, and no hollow. The stick is read back and is as you called it. Before you are out of the yard her firemen are carrying boards round to the kiln, a pair of men of the clay-bank people who nod to you and say nothing.

On the twenty-sixth and the twenty-seventh there is smoke over the potters' roofs, by day and through the night between, and a shower on the second afternoon. Then three days of nothing, and a kiln nobody goes near. The markets of the twenty-sixth and the twenty-ninth are read as any.

On the night of the thirtieth, from the landing, you see the blue light dim, as on the earlier nights: the blue deepens, it stands dim through the middle of the night, and then it is as before.

The first day of the eighth month. First light, dry and bright, no market. The owner of the landing that had asked for this dawn told you last evening that the master of the potters had been to him, and that he would spare you.

You are in the kiln yard behind the potters' hall. There is one round kiln of clay and brick, with its door bricked up and clayed over. No heat comes off it. The master of the potters stands at the door with a mallet, her sleeves down. Her firemen stand behind her with an empty board apiece, and more boards are laid on trestles in the yard. The Assay-master is beside you. The Tally-keeper has both long sticks, the green figures on them and a clean run beside each. Your scales are at your belt. No weight is in the air.

The master of the potters says: "It is cold. Stand back from the door, there is dust."
