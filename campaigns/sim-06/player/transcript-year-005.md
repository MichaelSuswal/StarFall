

## DM:

You step back, well clear, on the yard side of the other pair, with your hands at your belt, and say your line to her. She does not turn round. "I stand at my door," she says, and puts the mallet to the clay.

The clay comes off in plates and the bricks come out one at a time into a fireman's hands. There is dust, pale, and it hangs. When the door is open to the knee she looks in, a long look, and says nothing. Then her firemen go in and out bent double, and the pieces come out on the boards.

You ask your three things, once, in your order.

"They come out as the kiln gives them, not as they stood," she says. "My men set every one on the trestles by its mark, all of them, the spares too, and nothing is weighed until the last is set. A figure cut against the wrong piece is worse than none. Then smallest first, as you say, and each from my hand to your pan." The Tally-keeper says, "By the mark on the foot. I have the green." The Assay-master says, "I'll choose. Fired and cooled. Call them."

It takes a while. The pieces stand on the trestles in the early light, darker than they went in, smaller, every one whole. She turns each foot up to the Tally-keeper, who reads the scratches and the size aloud, and then she sets the piece in your pan. You call what the pan gives now, in your ones and tenths, and say nothing of what it gave before. The Tally-keeper cuts each in the clean run beside its green.

Fifty-five pieces, and none cracked and none chipped. She has looked at each before it reached your pan and found nothing to say. Asked by the Assay-master how the fire went, she says: "As my fires go. A pair of days and the night between, three days cooling, no rain on the kiln."

Then the breaking. She takes the fifth spare of each size, smallest first, sets it on a brick and breaks it with the mallet, one blow each. She picks up the halves and looks at the break herself, close, turning it to the light, before she gives them to you. Each shows a close grey body, the same through, with no stone in it and no hollow. In your pans the halves together give what the piece gave whole, and the pans give you no stone in any of them. You say that. She takes the halves back and lays them on a board of their own.

The Tally-keeper reads both sticks through, green and then fired for each mark, with the master and the Assay-master at her shoulder. It is as you called it, each time. You say nothing of which shrank most. Nobody asks you. The master of the potters stands a long time with her eyes on the cuts and her lips moving, and what she works out she keeps. When one of her firemen starts to say that it looks a good load she says, without turning, "It is good when it sits level on his beam."

The Assay-master has her set a pair of pairs on her own beam in the hall, the sevens and the twelves again, first against second. Of the sevens the first sinks, a little. Of the twelves the second sinks. "The beam agrees," he says. "The six do not leave the hall. So the nest comes to them." You have stepped aside, and he does not ask you to the beam.

That day she and her firemen carry the pieces up to the hall in reed baskets.

The second is a market day, and you are at the gate. When the line is read you give the Stallreeve your words on the carrier, as you gave them to me: nothing more of yours to be cut; what the ring took is the town's word and the first stick carries the part the ring let you send; the second is the ring's to cut or leave; asked about the fifth, the carrier to say what the stick says and no more; what you yourself say of the fifth to a captain who asks, and that you will not claim the ring's yes where it said no; what you would have the carrier told, and only that; that you will say more in session if the ring wants more cut before the tenth month; and that you ask nothing for the fifth or the scale. He hears it through with his head on one side. "Nothing more of yours, then," he says. "That is an answer, and I had waited for one." The Tally-keeper, at the post, cuts nothing.

Through that day and the next the hall's door stands open, and from the yard anyone can see the master of the potters at the bench with a wet stone and the Assay-master's beam, and hear the stone. You do not go to the beam, and you are not sent for. On the fourth, the Assay-master says to you at the hall door, in the hearing of those in the yard, that eleven of the new pieces sit level with the old six and with one another, to a hair, on his beam. "The beam agrees. The ring's day for the town's weight stands." The other eleven and the spares have gone back to the potters' hall. The sticks of the green and the fired figures are the hall's, with the Tally-keeper, for anyone who fires weights and asks her. No weight is in the air.

(oog) The close.

Both scenes you prepared are played, and they hold as played: the weighing green on the fourteenth and the twenty-fifth, and the kiln-opening on the first. I ran on from the kiln yard to the Assay-master's word on the fourth, which is as far as the board I was given goes. The story stands there, on the fourth day of the eighth month of Year 3. The night works these days into the rest of that year, and the morning tells it on from there.

Your answer to the Stallreeve is given, on the second, at the gate, in your words as you wrote them. He took it as an answer. What he does with it, and what the carrier is told, is the world's.

Three things I could not give you from what I had, and did not make up. What each half weighed alone: I had no figure for a break, and a roll wants its odds put to you first, with no turn left for it. How much of each broken piece is clay and how much is not, as a figure: I had only that there is no stone in any and no hollow, and that is what you were told. Both go to the Keeper. And you asked that the figures go out of the yard only when the Assay-master has said what the beam says; he said it on the fourth, and nobody had asked you for a figure before then.

One ask is still yours, and I did not reach it in time. The boy at the gate asked, "Is the star yours?", in the first month, and asked it again of the empty post in the second. You saw the blue light dim again on the night of the thirtieth. It is not answered, nothing is read into that, and I will put it to you first thing when we next sit.

Your words on the height of the water, and your words for anyone who asked for a green figure, were not needed in these days: nobody asked. They are kept as you gave them.

No roll was made today. Nobody's name was said to you, and nothing changed on your map.

The world turns at 1:30am Pacific.
