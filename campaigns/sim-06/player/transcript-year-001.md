Year 1. Early autumn, the hour before first light. The turning has stopped. You are standing where you landed, on the top of an old raised bank, with firm clay under your feet and the wet on either side of it. The air has the first edge of cold in it. Mist lies on the water and thins over the bank. You smell reed, wet clay and woodsmoke. Water is moving in a channel close by, low and slow after the summer, and rain is not far off. The coat is on you with what its pockets hold, and the scales hang at your belt where they hang, with their dent. It is dark still. A round blue light stands in the north-east, about a fifth of the way up the sky, wider than the moon and steady, and it gives light enough to walk by. You have no name for it. By it you can see the bank under your feet and the pale line of a path running along the bank's top. You feel no other like yourself. That knowing comes only at close quarters, and nothing here gives it.

## Player:

I stand a minute on the bank while the light comes, and I check what I carry. The scales are at my belt and the dent is where it was. I take out the chalk and the cord and put them back. The empty pocket is still empty.

Then I walk. I take the path south-east toward the town and keep to the top of the bank. I do not go into the reeds. I want to see the town before it sees me, so I stop where the road meets the path, about a mile out, and watch what moves in the morning: who comes out first, what they carry, whether anyone goes to the high bank with the drum.

I do not announce myself. I walk in as a stranger with a scale under the coat and a pocketful of weights, and I go to the market, since that is where the four groups meet. I want to find the hall on the highest bank where track meets channel, and I want to see it from the gate before I speak to anyone.

At the gate, if anyone asks, I say I am a weigher and I have come to see the market. I do not say I am a god. I do not lie about a measure. If someone asks what I weigh, I tell them I weigh what is put on the pans, and that I am happy to check anyone's scale for the price of a handful of grain.

Question for the DM: as I walk in, do I feel anything from the town, or does anyone there feel me? And what are people carrying to the market at this hour?

## DM:

You walk. The clay of the bank top is firm and the path is easy, and by the time the mist has thinned to the height of the reeds you are close under the town. You stop on the bank short of the first roofs, with the road in plain view, and you watch.

The boats come out first. Flat-bottomed, poled by wiry, sun-dark people, they slide out of the side channels before the light is full and go off among the reed islands, most of them empty but for nets and baskets. A little later they start coming the other way, and those are loaded: bundled reed stacked higher than the poler's head, rolled mats, basket-work, strings of dried fish, a morning's catch under wet sacking. One boat rides very low under a load of grey river clay, with two broad, stocky men in it who do not talk to each other. On the banks, people come out of the reed and clay houses with barrows and shoulder poles. There are pots packed in reed, a great many of them, and from the far side of the town, the side you cannot see past, quiet weathered people come in with covered baskets carried two to a pole. The road is the emptiest thing in view. It is an old road, sunk below the ground on either side and grown over with grass in places, and in all the time you watch only a few people use it, none of them carrying much.

Nobody goes up to any high place. You can pick out one bank that stands above the others, with a larger roof on it, and all the loads are moving toward the foot of it. From here you cannot make out a drum. You hear none.

From the town itself you feel nothing but what your nose and ears give you: kiln smoke, fish, brine, wet reed, and the sound of a great many people starting to argue amiably about prices. Nobody on the banks stops, turns or looks up as though something had touched them. A few glance at a tall figure in a long coat standing on the bank, the way people look at any stranger, and go on with what they are carrying.

When the traffic is thick you go in with it. The path takes you where it has been going all along, to the high bank where it comes down to the main channel. Boats are drawn up along a landing there, and the open trodden ground between the landing and the bank is the market: stalls of pole and reed mat, heaps, baskets, pots in rows, and all four kinds of people you were promised in one place, the wiry dark ones off the boats, the weathered quiet ones with the covered baskets, the broad stocky ones with the clay and the pots, and a few tall pale ones standing by a thin showing of charcoal. People make way for you as they would for anyone and look a moment longer than they would at a neighbor. As you pass within a few paces of them no weight comes into the air. Nothing you pass is one like you.

You keep to the path along the edge of it and do not go in among the stalls, so no scale is yet within a few paces of yours. There are balances at work down there, you can see the pans dip, but they are too far off for the scales at your belt to tell you anything.

The gate stands at the top of the bank, two posts and a lintel with nothing on either side of it, no wall and no fence, and the gate itself is open. You stop in it. Beyond is a yard of beaten clay, and the house: long, reed-roofed, clay-walled, the largest roof in the town. To one side of the yard, on a frame of heavy timber, hangs a drum as wide as a cart wheel, with its beater tied up out of the weather. The door of the house stands open for the market. Through it you can see into a long dim room: a plank bench, a row of fired clay weights along the wall behind it, a balance on the bench, and someone sitting behind the balance. Two people stand in front of the bench with a basket between them, and both are talking at once. Three or four more wait in the yard with scales or baskets of their own, and one of them has turned to look at you.

No one has spoken to you yet.

## Player:

I take a step through the gate and stop inside it, out of the way of the people waiting. I nod to the one who turned to look and say, "Morning. Is the hall open to anyone, or only to people the Warden knows?" I keep my hands where they can be seen, and I don't touch the scales at my belt.

Then I ask what the quarrel at the bench is about and who sits behind the balance. I ask both quietly, of whoever is closest, and I do not name my business yet. If they ask, I am a weigher, passing through, curious about the hall.

I move up along the wall of the yard to where I can see the pans on the bench from a few paces. I don't push in. I want to watch the weighing from there. I keep the coat closed, my scales on me, and my mouth shut about what I think of the result unless someone asks me. If the person at the bench reads the weight wrong, I say nothing in the first minute. I want to see how the hall handles a mistake. If they ask me directly what it weighs, I answer truthfully.

On the three lines of standing conduct, they stand as written, with one note on the order. The keeper's place I fill in the open, by checking the town's standards against my scales, but only once the Warden knows what I am doing and has agreed to watch. The Clerk's office is the one I would license first, and only after I have seen that person work. I make no promise today. Anything touching the lost house, the name or the debt is left alone until I say. That one is firm.
