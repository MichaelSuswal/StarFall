# Transcript: Year 2

Campaign: sim-06. Date: 2026-11-24 (Pacific).

## DM:

The light goes off the channel and the last pans in the market come to rest. You stand at the foot of the gatepost with the brass at your belt and a few days' fees in your pockets, and behind you the town shuts itself up for the night, door by door, each one a small weight set down. One pocket is as empty as it was on the bank. The blue light stands where it stood. The year turns.

Year 2. Early autumn again, the mornings in mist and the first edge of cold back in the air. The town is fed, housed and not afraid. Its grain is in, and its market quarrels less than it did, because a quarrel of weight can now be settled at the gate in an hour. It has not made up its mind about you. The channel folk, the salt-rakers and most of the potters are for the stranger at the gatepost, and some of its chief people are against a stranger's brass setting a measure. You are where you have been every morning of the year, at the foot of the gatepost of the town's house, with the scales and the nest on you and a few days' worth of fees in your pockets. Your body is whole. In all the year no weight came into the air from anyone who stood near you, and your scales told you what they always tell you and nothing new. One moment of the high summer is not finished. The year is told below as far as that moment, and stops there.

The door shut, and the Assay-master stood across the bench from you with his hands on the plank. "You weighed my ten in front of the yard and made a fool of my bench," he said. "Tell me first how you knew." You told him: your scales tell you what is in the pans, and what is weighed beside them while you are near, and that is the whole of it, and you would show it again if asked. "Then show me again," he said, and set out three things, one at a time: a small fired weight, and two things you had not seen before. You weighed each in your own pans and called it in your cups. He disputed none of the three.

He asked what you wanted here. You gave him the offer as you had meant to: that you want the Weightward's old weights set right, that you would weigh them in front of the council and not in a back room, and that you would like it to be his offer to the eleven and not yours. He asked why the eleven should take a stranger's scale for the town's standards, and you said nothing, and he said, "A man who is selling has an answer to that." He asked whether you were something more than a man with good brass. You said nothing. He waited, and went on. He asked who sent you, and whether a king of the river towns wanted his share of the market again. You said nothing. He asked whether you had a name yet to put in the other pan, and you told him none but the Weigher. "Then that is what I'll call you, for now," he said, and did not give his own. He asked why you had asked how the old Weightward died. You said nothing. He said only, "He was my friend." He asked what the Weigher wants of the hall after the weighing, and you said nothing to that either.

Then, unasked, he said that if the eleven would have it, he would stand at the bench with his own hand on the beam while the old weights were weighed. He opened the door. Neither of you had promised anything.

From the second morning the yard filled before light. You stood at the foot of the gatepost and read each scale in the order of the line, for grain or fish or a fired weight, the reading and nothing more. Most were out by something, both ways. Some read level at once, and some after their owners had adjusted them and had them tried again. When a ship-owner's runner offered scrap silver for a place at the head you refused it, and he stood all day and was read last, and the yard laughed about it for a week. In that first month a stallholder asked how long you would stay, and a child in the line asked whether you were the drowned one's child. You answered neither.

On the third day a man the yard calls the Stallreeve, who runs the market and the watch, stood watching you until noon. Then he set two of the watch at the gatepost, "to keep the line, not to guard you." From the second month he brought every quarrel of weight between a buyer and a seller to the gatepost, had the scales read, and ruled on it himself at the stall. He never once asked you to rule.

The line talked while it waited. An old salt-raker told how a man called Hale the Raker led the salt people onto the bank in the flood years, and how a larger god drowned at a place they call the Maun Stair, which is why the tide keeps coming. An old woman spoke of walkers the town calls the list-singers, who came when the oldest were young, sang names all night on the bank and traded nothing.

In the second month the hill carriers came down the Old Rudd Way with ore and charcoal. They named their village up the road, Ruddfold, and spoke of iron from stone towns far to the east, a place they call Hask; one of them asked you, laughing, whether you came from there. A charcoal buyer would not pay them until their scale was read. The Stallreeve brought both scales. The carriers' was level to a hair. The buyer's was out by about a tenth, in his own favor, and he paid the difference that day. A salt-raker asked whether salt would do for your fee, got no answer, and paid in fish the next day.

In the third month the potters began bringing fixed reed baskets of each digger's clay to be weighed, holding that a heavy load is a gritty one. Most loads weighed alike. One digger's ran heavy by about one part in nine. You gave the weights and nothing else. A kiln-master named Orrin said you should bring your brass to the diggers' pits and say which clay is good, and a potter held up a ball of clay and asked whether it was clean. The quarrel went to the council, which settled a price and not the question. By summer the yard's talk was that the kilns that sorted by weight had cracked as many loads as before, and some potters said aloud at the gate that the scale is no use for clay.

A stallholder asked, "May I say my scale was read at your gate?" Before long there were small fired tokens hanging on scales all over the market, saying so. The Stallreeve had them taken down in the fourth month: a scale read level in autumn can be filed light by midwinter.

In the fourth month a woman the yard calls the Tally-keeper, who keeps the kept halves of the town's split sticks, came to the gatepost. She has been cutting a stick of the gate's readings since the first night, split in the hall's manner. She said, "Name a mark, and I will cut it." You said nothing. She keeps her own stick. In the same month a bonded net-mender, whose owner's grain scale you had read out by about a twentieth in the owner's favor, said to you, "Reckon my debt for me, and strike it." You said nothing, and the council later took a twentieth off her debt.

Through the winter an apprentice of the hall brought scales to the line, fourteen in all over a month. You read each as you read any. A few had something small wrong with them: a thumbnail of clay under a pan, a thread of reed in a cord.

The fifth month brought a hard spell, and the ordinary winter fever in the crowded houses. Word went round four days ahead, from an old woman of the flats, that the water would crest over the lowest paths. Stalls moved and boats were drawn up. It came half a day later than she said, at the height she gave. Two households came to you over a reed island that had split, saying, "Who owns it? You say what is true." You did not, and they took it to the council.

Late one night in the sixth month, at the landing, where you sat by the channel folk's lamp, a hand went to your coat for the nest. Your own hand was on the nest first. You said nothing and did not strike. Bren's people took hold of the man and the watch held him. He is a bonded man of a stallholder whose scale the gate had read out by about a tenth, in the stallholder's favor. At first light the Stallreeve brought him to the gatepost and asked, before the yard, "Do you ask a price of him?" You said nothing and read the next scale. The council heard it in its ring and set a price in measure, for a theft tried on a guest of the yard. The Tally-keeper holds the stick, and the price lies unclaimed. The man gave his master's name. The yard says the council did not find that the master sent him. That same month a stallholder asked you to stand witness to a bargain, and when you did not answer, two of the watch stood witness.

More than once in the seventh month the yard asked you outright, "Will you be our Weightward?"

In the eighth month the spring flood came, ordinary in height, and the sowing took every hand. The yard said the boat people had been gone from the channels at the lowest tides, in autumn and again in spring, and that they go to the Maun Stair. Nobody followed them.

In the ninth month the yard had it within the day that the eleven had voted, seven to three with one away on the water, to ask the stranger to weigh the hall's old weights before them after the harvest, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. Against, as the yard tells it, were the heads of hill goods and of salt and the master of the smiths. The head of salt spoke longest: a store of worth is made by agreeing what the weight is, not by finding out. The master of the potters has fired a nest of eleven new clay standards in one kiln, unmarked, and came and asked whether you would weigh the new nest against the old and set the hall's standards by them.

In the tenth month boats came down from the kingdom upriver, which their traders call the Aldermark, with grain, wool and some bronze. Two of the traders had their scales read on a dare. Both were out by a little, and they laughed and paid in barley. No one came from their king. In the eleventh month a mate off one of the southern coasters asked to have his captain's scale read, and it was. The southern ships did not come up to the town's landing this year.

Twice in the first month of autumn, some days apart, and once in the dead of winter, the town says, the blue light in the sky changed. You have it from them: the line at the gate talked of the first two nights in your hearing that same month, and the watch and the boatmen at the landing spoke of the winter one. As they tell it, each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the light stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. The town says it has seen such nights in other years. Many in the line tied this year's to the stranger's coming.

No one you knew by name died. The winter fever took the old and the very young in the ordinary way. The town still has no name for itself, and none was said.

Then a market day in high summer, in the eleventh month, with the yard full. The Stallreeve comes down from the hall himself with a split stick in his hand, and the Tally-keeper a pace behind him. He stands in front of the gatepost and says it for the whole yard to hear: the eleven ask the Weigher to weigh the hall's old weights before them, at the first market after the grain is in, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam. He lays one half of the stick at the foot of the gatepost.

You are standing at the gatepost with a stallholder's scale in your hand. Your own scales are on the clay beside you and the nest is on you. The half-stick lies at your feet. The Stallreeve has not turned to go. The Tally-keeper holds the other half. The line has gone quiet and the whole yard can hear. The Assay-master is not in the yard. Nothing has been asked in words beyond what was said. No weight is in the air from anyone present. You have not seen the old weights, and no one has told you how many there are or where they are kept.

The meeting at dusk ran on the words you handed off, in those words and no others. Where the Assay-master asked something those words did not answer, you kept silent, as your card lets you. The rest of the year ran the same way: you had no helper, you did what your decisions and your conduct already said, and what the town brought you beyond that met silence. Every one of those asks is still waiting for you, the Assay-master's five and the year's, and I will put each to you today.

The Keeper has ruled on the question I carried from the landing day. A scale is a true scale when you have tried it by your own hand, with your scales on you or in your hand, and it read level, at once or on a new trial after it was adjusted. Your nest counts as part of your scales for this. It stays a true scale only for as long as it reads true. So Bren's balance has been one since its second trial, and so is each scale you found level at the gate this year, each for as long as it holds. It is on your card as a dated ruling.

Your map has grown by names: the Bank Way, the Old Rudd Way, and as rumors with their tellers, the Maun Stair, Ruddfold, Hask and the Aldermark.

A question for you. How does the Weigher keep its nights? It needs no sleep, and the record has it sitting late at the landing and no more. Until you say, I can tell you only what the town told you of those nights in the sky, not what you saw.

## Player:

I put the stallholder's scale down on the clay, read, and tell her it was level. Then I pick up the half-stick from the foot of the gatepost and say, loud enough for the yard, "Yes. I'll weigh the hall's old weights before the eleven, at the first market after the grain is in, with the Assay-master's hand on the beam."

I add the terms, so nobody can say later I set them in private. "I weigh each piece in my pans and call it in my cups, out loud, one at a time. The Tally-keeper cuts what I call, and the Assay-master checks each reading on his own beam beside mine. I don't set the town's standard. I tell you what each piece is now, and the eleven decide which is the town's weight. If the eleven want the new clay nest weighed against the old set the same day, the potters' master can bring it, and I'll weigh that the same way."

To the Tally-keeper, who has asked for a mark: "Cut two pans, level. Nothing else." I keep the half-stick in the coat, in a pocket that isn't the empty one.

To the Stallreeve: "Where are the old weights kept, and who holds the key? I want to know before the day, so I'm not seen asking after." I wait for his answer.

I tell the yard one more thing, since they've asked it enough. "I haven't said yes to being Weightward. I'll say it after the weighing, not before. A man who answers before he's seen the weights is selling something." That draws the answer to the line's question and nothing more. I keep my face plain, then go back to the next scale in the line.

I leave the thief's unclaimed price with the Tally-keeper's stick, and I ask her to cut it as remitted if the stallholder's scale reads level at the gate this month, as a way of putting the matter to bed without a quarrel. I won't claim a price from a bonded man.

The nights: I sit at the landing, by the channel folk's lamp, where the boats come in. I don't sleep, and I don't pretend to. I listen to the water and the talk. I count the day's readings over in my head, and I watch the blue light, which I'd like to learn the habits of. If the DM wants it written down, that's how I keep them.

For the rest of the day, I take the line in its order. I'll answer the Assay-master's open asks and the rest of the year's, one at a time, when the DM puts them.
