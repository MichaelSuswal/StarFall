# Day 3 transcript: the people

## DM:

The fall has a bottom now. Below you the dark has come apart into country, and you can see what kind.

It is late autumn. The leaves are mostly down, there is frost lying in the shade, and new snow on the highest ground. A cold, quick river runs along the floor of a valley closed in by its own ridges, with mist on the water and a wind that comes down off the heights at dusk. The valley floor rises into hills, and behind the hills stand high folded mountains, range behind range. Bare oak and beech on the lower slopes, dark fir above. Far down the valley, where the river leaves the hills, the ground goes low and wet. There are other valleys beyond the ridges, each shut away from the next.

The land shows this much of whoever is here. Smoke from more than one place along the valley floor, going straight up in the still of the evening. Walled fields in stubble, and stone walls running up the hillsides farther than anyone now seems to graze. Turf cropped short on the lower slopes. A worn track beside the river, and a place where the river is crossed on set stones. Under the turf on the valley side, in places, a line of fitted stone, wider than the track and straighter, running up the valley and out of sight. And as the light fails, a lit window, or what may be one.

That is all the land will say. It does not say who they are.

So the Between asks you, Vesper, before you are down among them. You said you want one hearth, kindled from your lantern and kept by someone who did not ask for it. A hearth is kept, and the light is seen. Who else comes to it, in time? Do you hope for a people at all, and if you do, how many, and what are they to you? Tell me who you hope is under that smoke: what kind of folk, how they live, what they do with their days.

(oog) Day 3 runs like Day 2: talk freely and I follow. The game's default is a people of about 5,000 with a town or city and advisors who carry out the god's wishes. You may take that, shrink it, or refuse any part of it, and nothing punishes the choice. Two standing rules for today. You name your people and their home; the world names everything else. And anything you say about the land or its past, I write down as your wish or as your people's belief, not as fact. The world decides, and you will see.

## Player:

I hope for a people, and a small one. The default of five thousand with advisors who carry out my wishes is too much, so I'll shrink it and refuse the advisors.

**How many.** About eight hundred, spread along the valley floor in a few steadings and one stone town at the crossing. The walls climbing the hills past where anyone grazes, and the old fitted road under the turf, tell me there used to be more of them. I want a remnant, a people living in the shell of something larger, who aren't sure whether they're its heirs or only squatters. That's my wish, not a fact, and I'm fine with the world deciding.

**Who they are.** Hill and river folk. They keep sheep and some cattle on the lower slopes, grow barley and oats in the walled fields, and cut peat and wood for winter. They make wool, cheese, cured meat and rough cloth, and trade what they can down the valley and over the passes. They're practical, a little stubborn, hard to impress. They don't pray much. They keep customs, and the strongest one is about doors and fires: you don't turn anyone from your door in the cold, and you don't let a hearth go out in winter if you can help it. I'd like that custom to exist before I arrive, with no connection to me, so that I fit into it instead of founding it.

**Name and home.** The people call themselves the Haldren. Their town is Ostrey, a cluster of stone houses above the stepping stones, with a low wall and one gate. I'd like the lit window to be a house near the ford, kept by someone who tends a lamp for travelers because it's what her family does. That's a hope. I'd like her to be a woman near the end of her working life who doesn't ask what I am.

**What they are to me.** Neighbors I'm responsible for, and not subjects. I don't want worship, a temple or a priesthood. I want to be the Doorkeeper at the edge of the fire, whom some people speak of and some people half believe in. They should be able to get on without me, and they should be worth keeping.

**What I want from the world.** Two or three old families or houses who disagree, a quiet divide between the town and the outlying steadings, and a few people who know the road under the turf is older than the walls. No chosen heir, and no enemy at the gate on the first night.

I'll ask for the edge now, so you can price it. I want any Haldren sheltered at a hearth of mine in winter to be unable to die of cold or hunger. A mortal who is let in and fed at one of my flames in the hard months simply gets through the winter. That's far stronger than it should be, I know. It would make my people nearly proof against the thing that kills small peoples. Tell me what it costs, and what shape it takes, and I'll tell you whether I can pay it.

## Player:

**Which shape.** The first night, with one change. I want to take it, but I'd rather the hearth shut only on a night when it actually saved someone. As written, a hearth that lets in a traveler who would have lived anyway still goes dark to me until the thaw, which punishes me for ordinary hospitality. My counter is that the shape fires only when the person let in would otherwise die before dawn, and only then does the hearth close to me as window and door until the thaw. A hearth that took in someone merely cold stays open to me. I'd also want to be told on the night it fires, since a hearth that goes dark unannounced is dangerous. If that is too generous, then keep the shutdown on every use and I'll accept it. The cost still lands where I want it, on a night when a life was at stake.

I decline the long room. The price of putting out one of my flames at random for every life saved is too heavy. It would cost me windows I couldn't choose to lose, and the people it saved would be thin and weak in spring anyway.

**Her name.** Anwen Corve, called Old Corve by the neighbors. Her family has kept the ford lamp for longer than the family can account for. She's near the end of her working life, a widow, and the lamp is the one thing she's never let go out. What she does when the coat stops at her door is hers to decide, and I'll find out when you do.

**Whose word opens the gate of Ostrey.** The one who keeps it. A house has someone at its door, and a town should have a single keeper at its gate, so that the vow has a name to answer to. I'd like the Haldren to appoint a gate-warden, chosen by the old houses for a year at a time, whose job is to decide who is let in and who isn't. That person's word opens the gate to me. A householder inside can then invite me over their own door once I'm within, but cannot open the gate. Any of them could still shut me out for good by refusing to ask, and I accept that.

**Who the Haldren listen to.** No one person. They listen to a moot of householders, held at the autumn gathering and again whenever a matter can't wait. The old houses carry the most weight, and each has an elder whose word counts for more. For the year there's a speaker, chosen by lot from among the three houses so no house holds it long. The speaker has no power beyond running the meeting and carrying the moot's decision to the gate-warden.

**How they settle disputes.** By hospitality, which is why I like it. Two houses in dispute must each host the other at a table before the moot will hear the matter. A feud isn't settled until each has eaten under the other's roof, and refusing to host is itself an admission of fault. It doesn't always work, but it's the custom, and it ties back to the strongest one, that no one is turned from a door.

## DM:

(oog) Your counter on the first night is fair and I take it. It was pricing ordinary hospitality, which is the one thing your god should never be charged for. Here it is as it will go on your card, as a Day 3 entry.

The first night. At any hearth of yours, burning on its own fuel: a mortal who is let in out of the cold by whoever keeps that hearth, and who would otherwise die of cold or hunger before dawn, lives until dawn. It does not feed, heal or warm beyond what the fire does; at dawn they are in the hands of whoever let them in. Once per person each winter. Drawback: a hearth that has done this is shut to you, as window and as door, until the thaw. Limits: it fires only when a life was truly at stake that night; a hearth that takes in someone merely cold stays open to you. You are told: on the night it fires you feel that hearth close, and you know which hearth, not who was saved or why. A shut hearth cannot do it again that winter, so each hearth carries one life a winter and no more. A shut hearth stays in the chain and opens again at the thaw if it is still burning; while shut it is not a flame you can return through from absence. Any mortal, not only Haldren; the custom does not ask who is at the door and neither does the fire. It does nothing for a god. Winter is the hard months where the hearth stands, first hard frost to thaw. The Keeper reviews it tonight, since it works across a people.

The long room is declined and is not on the card.

The gate. The gate-warden's word opens Ostrey to you, and only the warden's. A householder inside may ask you over their own door once you are within, and cannot open the gate. One edge I am settling so it cannot be argued later in your favor or against you: a town's gate is kept by the town, so if there is no warden for a time, the moot's word through its speaker stands in, and the gate is never "kept by no one."
