# The Sallowfolk: the people's record

Campaign: sim-04. Written by the DM at the close of creation Day 3, 2026-11-21, from the read-back the player agreed. Lines in quotation marks are the player's words. The names Sallowfolk and Sallowdene are the player's. Where anything stands is the world's.

## Who they are

Human. "They call themselves the Sallowfolk, after the willows along the river, and the valley is Sallowdene to them. They are hedge-layers, orchardists, grazers of the upland turf and keepers of bees. Nothing grand: a patient farming people whose year is set by blossom and cutting."

## How many, and how they live

About 1,500, at the player's wish, in place of the usual 5,000. "Spread over the valley in a dozen or so hamlets and farmsteads, with no seat and no town." The hamlets are unnamed and left for play.

Households of several generations. "Spring is the dangerous season: lambing, the hungry gap, the late frost on the blossom. Summer is the long bright work. Autumn is the apple press and the honey harvest. Winter is mead, mending and stories."

## Their code

- They tell their bees the family's news.
- They never take a swarm by force, only one that has chosen to settle.
- They never take the last of a hive's honey, even in a bad year.
- They never sell a hive, only give one.
- They never kill a hive for its honey.
- They never say a death aloud before the bees are told.
- They do not speak ill of someone in the hearing of a hive.

Held in shame: letting a hive die of neglect; short measure in honey or wax; burying someone without telling the bees; cutting a hedge in nesting season; borrowing a swarm and not returning the first cast.

None of this is priced; it is custom. The DM stated its practical cost and the player accepted it: a people who never kill a hive for its honey and never take the last of it get less from each hive than keepers who do, and a people who never sell a hive cannot turn one into salt in a bad year.

## Skills and culture

Bees kept in woven straw skeps and hollow-log hives. Honey, wax and mead; rushlights and wax tapers. Hedge-laying, coppice, orchards, grazing on the upland turf. "Nothing past what the world gives them: coppice and hedge, woven skeps, mead, tapers, straw and clay."

## Priced edges and weaknesses

Three defaults were refused (the number, the town and seat, the helpers). Nothing was charged for refusing. Each counted one step in the god's favor in the one edge price it bears on, as for every god.

1. **Unusually good keepers of bees, with a trade in honey, wax and tapers to match.** That they are the best in the region is recorded as their belief; how their neighbors keep bees is the world's.
   Weakness, agreed: they live by the hive. Little grain to spare, salt and iron bought with honey and wax, so a poor hive year is a hungry winter for the whole valley, and they have little else to sell.
   Full price offered: that weakness and a second (their goods say where they come from; wealth in small things with no walls round it is known to anyone who buys a taper). The smaller number and the lack of a town both bear on this edge: two steps, and the second weakness was dropped.
   Said to the player and accepted: the Working's cost falls on hives, this people's the same as any, so this weakness makes that cost land on their table. "I'll count that against myself every time I use it near their hives."

2. **They notice early.** By ordinary signs, as good keepers do, days ahead of other farmers: weather, blossom, blight. Effects, not causes, and they are often wrong about causes.
   Limit, agreed, so that nothing is taken from the god's Tasting: never before the god's own tasting of the same ground would.
   Weakness, agreed: their eye is for hive, weather and blossom, not for people. They read strangers and bargains poorly.
   Full price offered: that weakness and a second (slow to act on what they see). Having no helpers bears on this edge: one step, and the second weakness was dropped.

Both rulings: settled at creation. Ledger: pending, Night 3.

## Their magic

Bee-wives. "A bee-wife in perhaps one hamlet in three, who can calm a hive, tell a death in a swarm's behavior, and sense a coming frost. It costs her sleep, a bad night for each use, and the older ones are tired people. I'd like it to be rumor in the other hamlets."

Settled at the table: the death she reads is one that has already happened somewhere, "a hive lost, a keeper dead in another hamlet, a neighbor's stock failed, not one that's coming. She gets news late and from a distance. No foresight."

Priced by the DM on the given terms (simple, and it costs the worker): a bad night for each use, wearing over years. Subject to the Keeper's reconciliation on Night 3. How many bee-wives there are is the world's to fit; "one hamlet in three" is the player's wish, and the player asked to be told if the count changes. A bee-wife's hive is a hive kept by more than ordinary craft and is unruled under the Working until the god meets one, as the power card already says.

## What they believe

"Their gods, if any, are small and local: a spirit for each hive, a luck for each orchard."

An old saying, held on the morning the god arrives, older than the god and not of its making: that there is a luck over the valley, and that swarms settle on the right houses. "I don't want to have caused it. If the world gives it a cause, I'll find out in play. What they believe it means, the world decides."

They do not know the god exists and are waiting for nothing.

## How they are ordered, and how they settle disputes

They run themselves. "Each hamlet has a reeve chosen from among the older households, and the reeves meet at the river crossing when something touches the whole valley, such as a flood, a dispute over water or a stranger at the border. They decide for themselves."

Inside a hamlet: "The reeve settles it, with the households of the quarrel present and the elders of the hamlet sitting by. The reeve isn't a judge in the strong sense. He or she hears both sides, proposes a settlement, and the households agree or don't. If they won't, the dispute can go to the reeves' meeting at the crossing, but only for something that touches the whole valley, so most never do."

A quarrel over a borrowed swarm "goes to the bees. The two households walk to the hive together, say the matter aloud to it, and take whatever the hive does next as the answer, which is custom and nothing more." The god does not intervene in it.

When a hive goes into winter short and nobody knows why, nobody answers to anybody. The keeper blames the weather, the bees or herself.

## Helpers

Refused, 2026-11-21, creation Day 3. "Nobody, in the sense you mean. I don't want a council that carries my intent." "I'm not asking you to give them a ruler, a priest or a chosen one." "I would not accept an advisor who is also my mouth."

No offices serve the god and no orders travel. The reeves and their meeting are the people's own; they do not know the god and owe it nothing.

Designed, not yet serving: one mortal, Orrin Teal (`people/orrin-teal.md`). He does not know the god. The god lands with no helpers in fact. Until he is won in play, unplayed time runs as with none: on the god's standing conduct alone, no one acts for it while it is away, and the people drift by their own lights. The DM said so plainly and the player answered: "That is what I asked for."

The game remembers out loud that this god does not play the usual way.

## The god's place among them

None that they know of. It tends and does not rule. It has no voice among them: "a pattern in the bees that a keeper may read as a warning, a welcome or a sign. That's the only language I have. They'd learn they read me wrong when the thing doesn't happen or something else does." "I'd rather be misread honestly than interpreted by someone who says they speak for me."
