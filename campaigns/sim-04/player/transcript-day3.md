# Transcript: creation Day 3 (sim-04)

## DM:

The fall has slowed. There is country under you now, and the voice of The Between is nearer than it was.

"Your card came back from its night. Nothing was added to it and nothing taken. What was marked unsure is sure: the day's flight, the reach, the tasting and the listening all stand as you agreed them."

"Now look down. Early spring, as you asked, and it comes late and wet here. The snow is off the valley floor and still lies in the shade of the upper woods. The river is loud and brown with melt. The mornings are cold enough that nothing with wings is abroad before the sun is well up. Little is in flower yet: catkins on the willow and hazel, blackthorn just breaking on the hedges, the first small things low in the grass on banks that face the sun."

"A broad river valley with many edges. Water meadows on the floor, flooded in places, the river's course marked by a line of willows. Fields on the lower slopes with old hedges round them, and orchards, bare, the trees old and well kept. Old woods in the side valleys. Above the woods the ground opens into upland, brown with last year's heather. Wet below and dry above, and none of it harsh. In a few weeks all of it will flower in turn, from the valley floor upward."

"And it is kept. Smoke, from more than one place along the valley. Hedges laid by hand, and not this year. Orchard trees pruned. A track along the foot of the slope, wide enough for a cart, with ruts. Grazed turf. That is all the land shows from here, and I will not tell you whose any of it is."

"Those who fall come down to a people. The usual one is about five thousand strong, with a town or a city, a seat, a court or a council, and those who run things when the one who fell is elsewhere. You said you would tend and not command, and that if a people had to be named you would name one. Today it is named. So. Who are the people you tend, in country like this? How do they live, and what do they do with their days? And when you are in another field, or not there at all, who among them runs things?"

(oog) Any order, any length, as yesterday. I will ask as you go, and price whatever gives them a real edge over other peoples. A few things to know first. The peoples here begin before the medieval era, with no steel forges; you may bring in what you know, but it is built here by the people, from what the world has, and you say how it is made. Mortal magic, if your people have any, is simple and it costs the one who works it. What you tell me about the land or its past is recorded as a wish or as your people's belief; where anything stands is the world's, and you will see it when you land. The names of your people and of their home are yours to give, or to leave unnamed for play.

## Player:

**My people.** Not five thousand and not a city. I want roughly fifteen hundred, spread over the valley in a dozen or so hamlets and farmsteads, with no seat and no town. If the game needs a gathering place, make it a market ground at a river crossing that fills a few days a year. I'd like them small enough that I can learn faces, and I'm asking for the smaller number deliberately, not as a handicap.

**Who they are.** They call themselves the Sallowfolk, after the willows along the river, and the valley is Sallowdene to them. They are hedge-layers, orchardists, grazers of the upland turf and keepers of bees. Nothing grand: a patient farming people whose year is set by blossom and cutting. They keep bees in woven straw skeps and hollow-log hives, they trade honey, wax and mead, and they make rushlights and wax tapers that other valleys want. They know bees well enough to read them, and they tell their bees family news, a custom I'd like to keep. They do not know I am a god. They know there is a good luck over the valley, and that swarms settle on the right houses.

**How they live.** Households of several generations. Spring is the dangerous season: lambing, the hungry gap, the late frost on the blossom. Summer is the long bright work. Autumn is the apple press and the honey harvest. Winter is mead, mending and stories. Their gods, if any, are small and local: a spirit for each hive, a luck for each orchard.

**Who runs things when I'm elsewhere.** Nobody, in the sense you mean. I don't want a council that carries my intent. They run themselves, by the old way: each hamlet has a reeve chosen from among the older households, and the reeves meet at the river crossing when something touches the whole valley, such as a flood, a dispute over water or a stranger at the border. They decide for themselves. I'm not asking you to give them a ruler, a priest or a chosen one. If you want to offer me helpers, I'd consider a keeper of one hamlet who has noticed the swarms and quietly wonders why, someone I can watch before I speak to them. I would not accept an advisor who is also my mouth.

**Edge over other peoples.** I'd like them to have two. First, their bees are the best-kept in the region, and so they have more honey, wax and a candle trade that makes them richer in small things than their neighbors. Second, they notice early, because beekeepers notice weather, blossom and blight before others do. I won't ask for more than that. Price it as you see fit.

**Magic.** A little. A bee-wife in perhaps one hamlet in three, who can calm a hive, tell a death in a swarm's behavior, and sense a coming frost. It costs her sleep, a bad night for each use, and the older ones are tired people. I'd like it to be rumor in the other hamlets.

**Craft.** Nothing past what the world gives them: coppice and hedge, woven skeps, mead, tapers, straw and clay. I'd like them to learn smoke control from me, if they ever do, but only by trial, not by gift.

**Wishes and beliefs, recorded as such.** I believe there was once a larger people here and that the old track along the valley foot was theirs. I'd like the Sallowfolk to remember this as story only, and to have no idea what it meant. Let the world decide whether that's true.

**Names.** People: Sallowfolk. Valley: Sallowdene. Hamlets I'll leave unnamed for play.
