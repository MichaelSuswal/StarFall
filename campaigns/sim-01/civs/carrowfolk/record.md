# The Carrowfolk: record

Campaign: sim-01. The player's people. Written by the DM at the close of creation Day 3 (2026-11-19), from the people as read back to the player and accepted ("Yes. That's my people"). The table talk is in `player/transcript-day3.md`. Lines in quotation marks are the player's words.

Nothing here describes ground beyond the valley head as the Keeper's brief gave it. Where the player's description reached into the land or its history, it is marked as what the people say or do, and the truth of it is the Keeper's.

## Who they are

Human. A herding people of about 1,200, "scattered through the valley in a dozen or so hamlets and summer shielings, no town." No lord, no granary, no wall. "I'd rather meet people who are still deciding what they are."

They call themselves the Carrowfolk, and the valley Carrow. "Nobody can say what it means. It's just the word, and the hamlets pronounce it a little differently."

## How they live

"They run sheep and a small hardy cattle, move up to the high pastures in summer and back down in autumn, and grow oats and barley on the valley floor where the birch gives way. Some of them, mostly the young and the restless, cross the pass in the short summer to trade wool and cheese for salt, iron and whatever else comes up from the other side. Salt is what ties the valley to the world beyond it."

What lies over the pass, and who is traded with there, was not settled on Day 3. It is the Keeper's.

## Their code

- They count. "Sheep, days, stones, favors. Debt is the glue of the valley, and everyone knows exactly who owes whom, down to a quarter of a cheese."
- They never leave a body unburied on the hill.
- They never turn a stranger out onto the tops after dark in winter.

"Those two are older than anything else they believe, and breaking either is the worst thing a person here can do."

## The reckoning

How a quarrel over a debt is settled, with no lord. In the player's words:

"When two houses quarrel over a debt, they hold a reckoning. It happens at the autumn gathering when the flocks come down, or any time within a day's walk of both houses if it can't wait. Each side names one person from a third hamlet they both trust, and the two of them count the debt aloud, item by item, in front of whoever turns up to listen. Nobody judges. The counting is the judgment. If the two counters disagree, they add a third, and the majority holds. Whoever loses pays in sheep, work or cheese, and anyone who refuses to be counted becomes someone nobody in the valley will owe or lend to again. It's slow and public, and it works because everyone's own debts are open to the same counting."

Ordinary custom. Not priced.

## Skills and culture

Herding, drystone work, wool and cheese, hill-walking, and exact reckoning of debts. An autumn gathering when the flocks come down. Nothing here is priced.

## What they believe

"Not much, and not loudly. They've always raised cairns at the passes and on the hill tracks, a stone added by every traveler, and they say it's for luck and for the dead who are still walking. They don't have a name for what the cairns are for."

They had no god before this one.

## What they remember

"About ten generations, they say. They came up the valley from the lower country, or over the pass from the far side, and different hamlets tell it differently, and the disagreement doesn't trouble them. What they agree on is that the valley was already kept when they got here. The walls and the folds were standing. Somebody had been grazing it. They don't say who, and they don't say what became of them. Their word for those people is something like "the ones before" and they stop talking when it comes up."

This is recorded as what the Carrowfolk say. What is true of it is the Keeper's to reconcile with the deep history. The player asked for exactly that: "I'd like that to be true of them, and I'd like you to decide what it covers."

## Cairns

- The habit is theirs and older than the god: a stone added by every traveler.
- An existing cairn becomes an honest cairn of the god's when someone adds a stone and means it for the god, by any name, or when the god lays its own hand on it. "Meaning it is what counts. Nobody gets it by accident."
- Raising and adding is left to whoever chooses. There is no duty. "I don't want a custom where everyone feels obliged and nobody feels it."
- Keeping a cairn (checking it, rebuilding it after a storm, never moving it or letting it lie) is left to the tally-walkers and to anyone who wants the work. "I'd rather have fewer cairns that are kept than many that aren't."

## Hierarchy

Refused. See `player/hierarchy.md`. This people has no office that carries out the god's wishes. People act from the code and from their own judgment.

## Priced edges

### The tally-walkers

A calling of "a dozen or so at first" who walk the passes and keep the line. They choose themselves, and the god says yes or no. There is no cap on how many walkers there are.

The edge: a walker cannot hand-lay a cairn. A walker can carry a stone taken from the god's own body, and where a tally-walker builds that stone into an honest cairn, the cairn is hand-laid.

The price:

- No more than three of the god's stones may be out at one time. Out means given and not yet made good.
- A stone is made good by the god taking a new stone into its body. A new stone takes a full year to become the god's own. Until the light comes into its seams it does not count.
- While any stone is out, Staying put does not hold: enough people with ropes can pull the god over. If the god is broken while a stone is out, it gathers at half speed.
- A carried stone that is lost, stolen or destroyed before it is built in is gone, and still counts as out for its year.
- A tally-walker is held to the god's honesty. Nothing prevents a walker lying. But if a walker says what they know to be false, or sets a false marker, while they carry the title, every stone-bearing cairn that walker built goes out of the god at once, unfelt, until the god walks to each and sets its hand on it.

What was asked and declined: the player first asked that walkers be trained to hand-lay cairns themselves. The DM declined it as undoing the limit that made the voice fair, and offered the carried stone in its place. The player accepted: "A dozen walkers is a calling, and three stones is a trust."

On Day 3 no tally-walker exists yet. The calling is something the god may found in play.

### The stone-wife

One person in the valley has the knack: "An old woman in the upper hamlet, past seventy, who the others call a stone-wife because she's said to know when a cairn has been moved, even if she's never seen it. She's mostly right, and she can't explain it."

As ruled by the DM: she knows only of cairns she has herself added a stone to. She knows that one of them is wrong, and roughly which way it lies. She is mostly right, not always.

The price: she has lost her own way. It came on slowly over the years. She can no longer keep a path beyond the walls of her own hamlet, and she has not left it alone in a long time. She can say a cairn is wrong, and someone else must go and find it.

Subject to the Keeper: this ruling stands unless the world's own rule for mortal magic says the knack works differently.

She does not know about the god. What she does is her own. The player hoped she would be the first to come and sit by the cairn at the valley head; nothing was promised.

She has not been given a name.

## Their magic

None in the valley but the stone-wife's.

## Weaknesses chosen

About 1,200 people where the game gives about 5,000. No town. No hierarchy. The DM counted these toward the two priced edges.
