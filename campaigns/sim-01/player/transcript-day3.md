# Day 3 transcript: the people

Campaign: sim-01. The table talk of creation Day 3, as relayed. Each post is kept as it was sent.

## DM:

The falling is nearly done. You can see where it ends.

High country, upland moor and broken hills, with long valleys running down between the tops. Stone everywhere: outcrops on the ridges, scree under them, boulders in the heather. You are coming down at the head of one of the long valleys, where it narrows and climbs toward a pass. Above is the bare top, wind and rock, and a notch in the skyline where the pass goes over. The first snow already lies up there. Below you the valley widens as it falls away. Birch and rowan and stunted pine keep to the floor and the sheltered folds. The bracken has gone brown and the heather is past.

There is smoke lower down, thin, from more than one place. There are low drystone walls and folds on the valley sides, some kept and some fallen. A worn way runs up the valley toward the pass. The turf is cropped, and something with small hooves has left its droppings on it. You can see no town from here.

The voice you left behind is fainter now, and it speaks as if across a width of water.

"People live under that smoke. I do not know them yet, and neither do you. They are made today, between us. So tell me who you hope they are. How do they live, and what do they do with their days? What is always true of them, and what would they never do?"

"You spoke all yesterday of travelers. Whoever is down there has built walls. Are the people you would lead the ones who keep a valley, or the ones who cross it?"

"And when they first learn you are here, how would you have it happen? Do they wake to find a cairn at the valley head that was not there the night before, and make of it what they will? Or do you walk down?"

(oog) Today is the people. Describe them freely: who they are, how many, how they live, their customs and skills, what they believe about gods before you. Ordinary culture costs nothing. Anything that would give them a real edge over other peoples gets priced against a weakness, the way your powers were. The game's defaults are about 5,000 people, a town or city, and advisors who carry out a god's wishes. You can keep or refuse any of those, and nothing is held against you for it.

## Player:

"People who keep a valley and cross it, both. A herding people. Maybe 1,200 of them, scattered through the valley in a dozen or so hamlets and summer shielings, no town. A town would mean a lord and a granary and a wall, and I'd rather meet people who are still deciding what they are. They run sheep and a small hardy cattle, move up to the high pastures in summer and back down in autumn, and grow oats and barley on the valley floor where the birch gives way. Some of them, mostly the young and the restless, cross the pass in the short summer to trade wool and cheese for salt, iron and whatever else comes up from the other side. Salt is what ties the valley to the world beyond it.

What is always true of them: they count. Sheep, days, stones, favors. Debt is the glue of the valley, and everyone knows exactly who owes whom, down to a quarter of a cheese. What they would never do: leave a body unburied on the hill, or turn a stranger out onto the tops after dark in winter. Those two are older than anything else they believe, and breaking either is the worst thing a person here can do.

What they believe about gods before me: not much, and not loudly. They've always raised cairns at the passes and on the hill tracks, a stone added by every traveler, and they say it's for luck and for the dead who are still walking. They don't have a name for what the cairns are for. A few of the oldest cairns on the pass are far older than the valley's memory, built of slabs nobody in the valley could move, and the people leave them alone without really knowing why. I'd like that to stay a mystery. I don't want to explain it yet. So they'll see me as one more of their own cairns, a very large one, and I think they'll take it in stride better than any other sort of thing.

On the defaults: I'll refuse the town. I'll refuse the advisors who carry out wishes, as I don't want servants who do what I say. If someone wants to help, I'd like them to choose it and keep choosing it. I'll accept a few mortals who attach themselves to me, because there'll be a few, and they'll be people, not instruments.

How they learn I'm here: a cairn at the valley head that wasn't there the night before. I land, I settle, and I stay still. The first shepherd up in the morning finds a figure the size of a big boulder with lichen on it and decides what to make of it. I don't want to walk down in the first week. I'd rather be found, and watched, and quietly tested. They'll push on me and walk around me and leave an offering or a rock, and the first person who adds a stone and then somehow doesn't lose their way home will do more than any speech.

Now an edge, and I'm going to ask for too much. I'd like the people to have a trade, a calling. I'll call them tally-walkers: a small order of men and women, a dozen or so at first, who walk the passes and carry the line. Each one is trained by me to lay a cairn the way I do, so that it counts as hand-laid. Their cairns carry my sense and my voice. That means I'd have a dozen people who can extend my line across the whole country at the pace of their own feet, and I'd get the sense and the voice at any stone they lay without having to walk there myself.

I know that's stronger than a god should get. A hand-laid line that I don't walk breaks the one thing you said kept this fair. So I'd expect a real price, and I'll offer one to start. Each tally-walker has to be honest in the same way I am. They can't say a false word or set a false marker while they carry the title, and if one of them breaks that, every cairn they laid goes out of me at once, not one at a time, and stays out until I walk it back by hand. I'd also take a cap on how many there can be, and I'd accept that I can't pick them. They pick themselves, by being the sort of person who'd do it, and I only get to say yes or no. If you want it harsher, name it. I'd rather see the price before I'm attached to the idea."

## DM:

"Twelve hundred, in hamlets and shielings, who count everything and bury their dead and turn no one out onto the tops. Then that is who is under the smoke. And you come down in the night and are found."

"Two things you said are the land's and not the people's, so I will not answer for them. What lies over the pass, and what comes up from the other side, you will learn by who crosses it. And the old cairns of great slabs: I heard the wish. Whether they stand there is for the world to say, and you will know when you look up at the pass in daylight. The habit is theirs, though. They raise cairns and add a stone and call it luck. That I can give you."

"Your words come as stones settling, and only the quiet hear them. A people is rarely quiet. You have refused servants. Is there someone among them you would still have listen for you and carry what you say, if they chose to, or would you sooner each of them had to come and be still?"

"And how long have they been in this valley, by their own telling? What do they say came before them?"

(oog) First the plain record, then the edge.

No town, no advisors, about 1,200: taken as you gave them. From here on the game remembers that this god has no one who carries out its wishes, only people who choose to help and keep choosing. The counting, the debts, the two things they never do, the transhumance, the pass trade: all ordinary culture, no price. The small number and the lack of a town are real costs you chose, and I am counting them toward what follows.

The tally-walkers. As a calling, a dozen or so who walk the passes and keep the line, yes, freely. As you priced it, no, and you named the reason yourself: a hand-laid line you do not walk undoes what made the voice fair. Yesterday's card also says your people cannot make a cairn hand-laid, and I would rather build on that than undo it a day later. Here is what I offer in its place.

A walker cannot hand-lay a cairn. A walker can carry a stone of yours. You take a slab from your own body and give it, and where a tally-walker builds it into an honest cairn, that cairn is hand-laid, because the stone in it is you. This uses what the card already says: a slab carried off is a piece you are missing.

The price is in your body.

- No more than three of your stones may be out at one time. Out means given and not yet made good.
- You make one good by taking a new stone into yourself, and a new stone takes a full year to become yours. Until the light comes into its seams it does not count.
- While any stone is out, you are less. Staying put does not hold: enough people with ropes can pull you over. And if you are broken, you gather at half the speed.
- A carried stone that is lost, stolen or thrown in a river before it is built in is simply gone, and still counts as out for its year.

And your own price, which I take as you offered it: a tally-walker is held to your honesty. Nothing stops them lying. They are mortal. But if one says what they know to be false, or sets a false marker, while they carry the title, every stone-bearing cairn that walker built goes out of you at once, unfelt, until you walk to each and set your hand on it. They choose themselves and you say yes or no. No cap on how many walkers there are. The cap is your stones.

So the line can run ahead of you, about three cairns a year at most, each one costing you a year of being easier to topple. I think that keeps what you wanted, people who carry the line, and leaves the country something you mostly still have to walk. Argue it if you think it is wrong in either direction.

Two questions on the cairns. The valley already raises them, and none of those were raised for you. I propose an old cairn becomes an honest cairn of yours when someone adds a stone and means it for you, by whatever they call you, or when you lay your own hand on it. Is that right? And should raising and keeping them be a thing every one of your people does, or left to whoever chooses? A custom spreads Not lost fastest. It also means more hands that can move a cairn wrong.

Last: you asked the world for magic that is rare, a knack. Is there anyone in this valley who has it?

## Player:

"Accepted. The price is fair, and I'll take it as written: three stones out at most, a year for a new one to come into the light, toppled by ropes while any are out, half speed gathering. I'll be careful about who gets one. A dozen walkers is a calling, and three stones is a trust. I'd give a stone only to someone who has walked a season at my side and shown they can keep a true word. The no-lying clause on the walker stays, and I accept that one false marker costs me every cairn they built.

Your cairn question: yes, that's right. An old cairn becomes an honest cairn of mine when someone adds a stone and means it for me, by any name, or when I lay my hand on it. Meaning it is what counts. Nobody gets it by accident.

Raising them: left to whoever chooses. Adding a stone is already the habit, and I'd leave it that. Keeping a cairn, which means checking it, rebuilding it after a storm, and never moving it or letting it lie, I'd leave to the tally-walkers and to anyone who wants the job. I don't want a custom where everyone feels obliged and nobody feels it. More hands means more chance of a cairn moved wrong, and a wrong cairn with my weight on it kills people. I'd rather have fewer cairns that are kept than many that aren't.

Someone to listen: yes, one or two who choose it. Not a servant, and not an advisor. Someone who sits quiet by me on the long evenings, and who may hear nothing for weeks, and may come to carry what I say to the others when they've heard it. They can leave whenever they like. I'd let them find me. I won't ask.

How long in the valley, by their own telling: about ten generations, they say. They came up the valley from the lower country, or over the pass from the far side, and different hamlets tell it differently, and the disagreement doesn't trouble them. What they agree on is that the valley was already kept when they got here. The walls and the folds were standing. Somebody had been grazing it. They don't say who, and they don't say what became of them. Their word for those people is something like "the ones before" and they stop talking when it comes up. I'd like that to be true of them, and I'd like you to decide what it covers.

Magic: yes, one. An old woman in the upper hamlet, past seventy, who the others call a stone-wife because she's said to know when a cairn has been moved, even if she's never seen it. She's mostly right, and she can't explain it. I'd leave the price to you, and I'd like it small and human, something that costs her in her ordinary days. She doesn't know about me yet. I'd like her to be the first to come and sit by the cairn at the valley head, and say nothing, and listen."
