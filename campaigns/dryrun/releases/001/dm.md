The year is held at a moment of contact in week 16 (early summer), on the east bank of the Ald a little below Fallstead, under the falls. Part A is everything the god knows up to that moment. Part B is the board at that moment. Part C is what happened in the world after it that the god will learn once the scene is played; it is not to be told in this morning story. Weeks count from the first day of spring: spring 1 to 13, summer 14 to 26.

## A. The year up to the moment of contact

### The god's own senses (power card: river-sense; god-sense, a pull at any distance, direction only)
- Waking, the shingle bar: god-sense gives two pulls, both east of him, on two bearings a little apart: one east and a little north, one due east. Direction only; nothing of distance or strength.
- Week 1, the fourth night after waking, from the bar: a sky-light over the Grey Sea to the west: a single steady pale light rose slowly from below the horizon, climbed a hand's breadth above it in about the time it takes to walk two hundred paces, dimmed, and was gone.
- Weeks 4 and 5: the first pull, the one that lay east and a little north, changes bearing a little each night until at Wellbrook (week 5) it lies due east of him. As he walks north up the valley it stays due east of him. The other pull is due east the whole time and does not change.
- Week 10, from Aldford: a sky-light over the wood to the north, climbing a quarter of the way up the sky, the same slow rise and fading. Two so far.
- Week 13 on, Fallstead: river-sense gives him the Ald to the top of the falls and no farther; above the falls he has touched nothing. The first pull now lies east and a little north, toward the wood's edge and the hills.
- Week 15, dusk, from the pool below the falls: he sees a figure standing on the rock shoulder at the top of the falls on the east side, against the last light, upright and still, too far and too dark for a face or a sex, for about the time it takes to draw a net. When he looks again it is gone. The first pull's bearing at that moment is north-east, toward the shoulder.
- Week 16: river-sense: from the falls he feels the Ald's flow at its mouth, forty miles down, begin to change over several days, as if the river were being narrowed and held between two lines. (The weir is going in; he knows what he ordered.)
- Condition at week 16: unhurt, never dry, water beading on him; he has not been more than a day's walk from running water all year and is at full strength.

### What he saw and did
- Week 1: he began walking the Ald upriver from the bar, touching the water at every village and staying about a week at each, as decided. The first Riverfolk to reach him on the bar said nothing and left a reed bundle on the shingle. He took it as they meant it and the villages heard so.
- The Ald ran high and brown for three weeks in early spring; the Aldford meadows were under water; the fords were shut. The river fell to its summer level in week 6. Barley went in during weeks 4 to 8.
- Week 5, Wellbrook: the headman Hobb and a knot of fishers came to him at the staithe and asked him for a law of giving at the weir he had ordered: what the river must be given back for what the weir takes. He observed and said nothing (standing conduct). PENDING QUESTION (player/pending.md, 1). The world did not wait: see week 16.
- Week 9, Aldford: a net-maker named Tam came to him at the water at nightfall and begged him to save his wife, Ede, who was in a hard labor. The god stayed standing in the river and said nothing (standing conduct; he has no power that bears on it). Tam went back to the house. Before dawn Ede died. The child, a daughter, lived. Tam has not come to the staithe since. Aldford speaks of it plainly: some say a god who wets his hand at every staithe but will not cross one doorway is no king; the wise-woman says he is the river's and the river does not come to the bed; most say only that he watched. Aldford has been cool to him since.
- Week 13: he reached Fallstead and the first falls, the end of his decided walk, and has stayed at the falls since, in the water most days, learning it. Above the falls the Ald comes out of old wood. From the valley he has seen, on clear days, the hills rising to the east and a grey snow range far to the north-east. The Riverfolk call the wood "the wood above the falls" and know only that hill country lies beyond.
- Weeks 11 to 14: at Fallstead the headman named Hask of Fallstead, a boatman of about thirty-eight, wide and weathered, two fingers gone from the left hand, and Brin of Aldford, a boatman of about twenty-two, lean and quick-eyed, for the errand past the falls. A dugout was built in weeks 11 to 13. In week 14, about day 94, at first light and in the god's sight, they carried it round the falls, poled a day above, hid it, and went east on foot into the wood, ten days out and ten back, as decided. Hask's wife and mother watched them go. They are not yet due back at week 16.
- Week 16: word came up by boat that the weir at Oxmouth had been begun on the line Maren, the headman of Oxmouth (a widow; the Riverfolk use the one word), chose: about 300 yards above the shingle bar, 60 yards bank to bank at low water, two rows of oak posts with stone and brush between, wicker hurdles on the upstream face, a wicker trap-pen midstream, and a giving gate four yards wide on the west side to be lifted on the dark of every moon and the first night of each run, with a tenth of each haul returned alive. Maren set the gate and the tenth herself as the code's answer to the weir. About sixty are at the work at a time.
- The people's mood as he has seen it by week 16: warming to him and wary; Oxmouth glad of him; Wellbrook kindly and watchful; Aldford cool since Ede; Fallstead anxious for its boatman. They call him the Weir-King. The elders' counts of the lights so far: two, in all twelve villages.
- Map: by week 16 the god's own eyes have added, at reported grade, the hills east of the valley, the wood above the falls, and the grey snow range to the north-east (player/map-knowledge.json, gazetteer and map redrawn zoomed on known ground). First morning: the DM reminds the player once that the map is on request.

## B. The board at the moment of contact: week 16, dusk, the east bank below the falls
The Weir-King stands knee-deep in the Ald at the foot of the first falls, a little below Fallstead, where he has stood most of the day. The falls are at his back and to his right, loud, throwing spray; the pool is black and the light is going, a low sun behind the wood on the west bank catching the top of the spray. The fishers have gone in. Fallstead is three hundred paces downstream on the east bank: smoke, a dog, two children still on the staithe looking his way. The east bank path comes down from the wood's edge through the last trees to the stones at the pool's side.

Down that path, openly, a woman is walking toward him. She is tall and plain-faced, in a hill traveler's grey wool and leather, a pack and a staff, no weapon he can see. Her hands hang at her sides and they are wet, though she has not touched the river. The first pull is where she is; he knows it as she comes, the way he knows water. She stops at the water's edge ten paces from him, on the stones, and looks at him. She has not spoken. The children on the staithe have seen her. Nothing else has happened.

Nothing in the god's own thread after this moment is resolved. The god may speak, retreat, go into the water, call to the village, or anything else; the DM plays it live. The woman's words are hers: her agent (sealed, SPOILERS_do_not_open/campaigns/dryrun/gods/thornmere/; the DM does not read it) is brought in by the day session to speak for her from her own record.

## C. Resolved for the world after the moment of contact; tell only after the scene
- Weeks 16 to 19: the weir finished in seventeen work days.
- Week 17: at the first low water of the week the mauls struck old timber under the mud near the east bank, a few yards above the weir line: two rows of black, hard, water-worn posts, cut square at the top by an axe. The line was moved eight yards downstream so none were touched. Maren says they are the Weir-King's own first weir and that he has come to build it again. Hobb says they are the roof-trees of a drowned house and a grave and would not send his men to drive near them. The elders tell the old tale of the ones who went under, who took too much and were taken back by the sea. Nobody dug.
- Week 17: Hask and Brin came home to Fallstead unhurt, within their time. Hask told the village the same day; Brin told Aldford in week 18. Whether the god heard it then depends on where the scene leaves him (DEPENDENCY: if he is still at the falls in week 17, Hask tells him that day; if not, it reaches him when he next meets his people; the Keeper resolves this next night from the scene's outcome). What they told: Above the falls the river shrinks to a stream in a day. They walked two and a half days east through old oak and pine with no track and came out into open hill country of sheep and heather, rounded hills, higher to the north where the grey snow range stands. On the fifth day they passed below a crest with old grassed-over banks and ditches on it, like a fort. On the sixth they stood on the shore of a dark mere in a fold of the hills. On the seventh, at a high steading of stone and turf above the mere, herders with dogs surrounded them and took them to an elder, an old woman with one clouded eye whom they called Grandmother Ceth. The hill people call themselves the Carn. The boatmen said who they were and that a god had woken on the bar at the river's mouth; the Carn then asked many questions about the river people, that god, and whether the river people count the lights. By the houses stood tall poles notched from top to bottom; the Carn did not say what they were for. The Carn kept them two nights, fed them, and sent them back the way they came with these words: the river people are to come no farther than the wood's edge, and the Carn will come down to see the river themselves. - Week 20, midsummer: the third sky-light of the year, over the hills to the east, climbing high. Oxmouth links it to the posts struck three weeks before: the posts the first sign, the light the second. The Aldford wise-woman says the lights mark where the river will rise and wants watchers set.
- Weeks 22 to 25: harvest, good; a winter's barley and a tenth over.
- Week 27: the council of headmen at Oxmouth: who keeps the trap and the gate; the catch shared by headcount with a tenth held for the poor; the posts (Hobb: mark and leave; Maren: open); the sky-light counts (four by every village); the Carn's words, and a watch set at the last ash grove above Fallstead; and who may ask the god things. Questions the headmen hold for him and have not put to him in person: whether the weir may take the whole run or the gate and the tenth stand; whether the posts are his own old work; whether the posts should be dug, marked or left; what the lights are and whether watchers are wanted; what the Carn are to him and whether the river people may go to the wood's edge. These are the people's talk, not pending items, until someone asks him.
- Week 32: the autumn run came with two gales off the sea; boats lay ashore two days. The weir held through both gales and the run. The trap-pen took about four tons of fish, dried and salted, and a tenth went back alive through the gate.
- Week 46, midwinter: the fourth sky-light, over the sea to the west. The true count for the year is four (tools/phenomena.py).
- Winter ordinary; the marsh creeks froze at the edges three weeks. Births about 183, deaths 169 as the people count them; about five thousand people.
- What the people would report at year's end, in their numbers: about five thousand, a few more than last year; a good harvest, a winter's grain and a little over; a weir built in seventeen days by sixty hands, and it held; four lights; black posts under the Oxmouth mud, the headmen divided; the boatmen home with word of the Carn; four hundred spears; about eight tons of dried fish and salt in the villages.
