# Keeper release to the DM, Year 1: everything the Weir-King could know

The year is held at a moment of contact in week 23 (late summer), at the pool below the falls. Part A is everything the god knows up to that moment. Part B is the board at that moment. Part C is what happened in the world after it that the god will learn once the scene is played; it is not to be told in this morning story. Weeks count from the first day of spring.

## A. The year up to the moment of contact

### The god's own senses
- Waking, the shingle bar: god-sense gives two pulls, direction only, both east of him: one east and a little north, the stronger; one due east and fainter, far.
- Week 1, the fourth night after waking: a sky-light over the Grey Sea to the west: a single steady pale light that rose slowly from below the horizon, climbed for about the time it takes to walk two hundred paces, dimmed and was gone high up. He saw it from the bar.
- Weeks 4 and 5: the nearer pull moves. Night by night it comes toward him out of the east, and in week 5, when he is at Wellbrook, it stops: in the hills east of the village, a day's walk off, and stays.
- Week 6, Wellbrook: river-sense, within the day: a single walker crossed the Wellbrook brook a mile above the village at night, coming down from the hills and going north along the hill foot, someone whose step he has never felt in any water. The pull is where the walker went.
- Late spring (about week 10): a sky-light over the wood to the north, seen from Aldford.
- Weeks 6 to 23: the nearer pull shadows him. As he walks upriver it moves with him along the hills to the east, never nearer than the hills, never gone.
- Week 17, the night the posts were struck at Oxmouth (he learns of the posts later; see below): a sky-light over the hills to the east, seen from the Aldford bank. Three so far.
- Weeks 16 to 19, from Aldford and on the road: river-sense: the Ald's flow at the mouth changes over several days, as if the river were being narrowed and held; then on the dark of the moon a gap opens in it and the water runs free through the gap. He knows the weir is going up and that the gate is opened as the people say.
- Week 19, dusk, from the Aldford bank: he sees a figure standing on the hill crest to the east against the last light, too far for a face or a sex, upright, still, for about the time it takes to draw a net. When he looks again it is gone. The pull is there.
- Week 22, Fallstead: the falls. River-sense gives him the Ald to the top of the falls and no farther; above the falls he has touched nothing. God-sense: the nearer pull is in the hills east and a little south of him, closer than it has been, a few hours' walk. The far pull is unchanged, due east.
- Condition at week 23: unhurt, never dry, water beading on him; he has not been more than a day's walk from running water all year and is at full strength.

### What he saw and did
- Week 1: he began walking the Ald upriver from the bar, touching the water at every village and staying several days at each, as decided. The first Riverfolk to reach him on the bar said nothing and left a reed bundle on the shingle. He took it as they meant it and the villages heard so.
- The Ald ran high and brown for three weeks in early spring; the Aldford meadows were under water; the fords were shut. The river fell to its summer level in week 6. Summer was dry and the ford at Aldford opened at midsummer.
- Week 5, Wellbrook: the headman Hobb and a knot of fishers came to him at the staithe and asked him for a law of giving at the weir he had ordered: what the river must be given back for what the weir takes. He observed and said nothing (standing conduct). PENDING QUESTION 1. The world did not wait for an answer: see below under the weir.
- Week 9, Aldford: a net-maker named Tam came to him at the water at nightfall and begged him to save his wife, Ede, who was in a hard labor. The god went to the door of the house and stood there through the night. The Aldford wise-woman was inside. Before dawn Ede died. The child, a daughter, lived. Tam has not spoken to him since. (The god used no power; he has none that bears on it. This is told as it happened.)
- Weeks 11 to 14: at Fallstead the headman named Hask of Fallstead, a boatman of about thirty-eight, and Brin of Aldford, a boatman of about twenty-two, for the errand past the falls. A dugout was built. In week 14 (the first days of summer) they carried it round the falls, poled a day above, hid it, and went east on foot into the wood, ten days out and ten back, as decided.
- Weeks 16 to 19: the weir at Oxmouth was built while the god was upriver: two rows of sixty oak posts, stone and brush packed between, wicker hurdles on the upstream face, a wicker trap-pen midstream, and a giving gate four yards wide on the west side, lifted on the dark of every moon and the first night of each run, with a tenth of each haul returned alive. Maren, headman of Oxmouth (the Riverfolk use the one word; she is a widow), set the gate and the tenth herself as the code's answer to the weir. About sixty worked at a time for seventeen days. Word of all this reached the god by boat at Aldford.
- Week 17: at the first low water of the week the mauls struck old timber under the mud near the east bank, a few yards above the weir line: two rows of black, hard, water-worn posts, cut square at the top by an axe. The line was moved eight yards downstream so none were touched. Maren says they are the Weir-King's own first weir and that he has come to build it again. Hobb says they are the roof-trees of a drowned house and a grave and would not send his men to drive near them. The elders tell the old tale of the ones who went under, who took too much and were taken back by the sea. Nobody dug. At Oxmouth many hold that the light over the hills that night was called up by the posts. The Aldford wise-woman says the lights mark where the river will rise and wants watchers set.
- Week 18, Aldford: Brin came home and told the god what the boatmen found. Above the falls the river shrinks to a stream in a day. They walked two and a half days east through old oak and pine with no track and came out into open hill country of sheep and heather, rounded hills, higher to the north where a grey snow range stands. On the fifth day they passed below a crest with old banks and ditches on it, like a fort, grassed over. On the sixth they stood on the shore of a dark mere in a fold of the hills. On the seventh, at a high steading above the mere, herders with dogs surrounded them and took them to an elder, an old woman with one clouded eye whom they called Grandmother Ceth. The hill people call themselves the Carn. They kept the boatmen two nights, fed them, and asked many questions: who the river people are, about the god who woke on the bar, and whether the river people count the lights. By the houses stood tall poles notched from top to bottom; the Carn did not say what they were for. Then the Carn sent them back the way they came with these words: the river people are to come no farther than the wood's edge, and the Carn will come down to see the river themselves. The boatmen came home unhurt in week 17, within their time. Hask told the same at Fallstead in week 22 and added that the Carn steadings are stone and turf and that they have more dogs than people.
- Map: the wood above the falls, the hill country, the mere and the old fort are now reported in the god's knowledge, by the boatmen (player/map-knowledge.json, player/gazetteer.md, map redrawn zoomed on known ground). First morning: the DM reminds the player once that the map is on request.
- Week 22, Fallstead: Hask's mother asked the god at the staithe to swear that if her son is sent past the falls again he will come back. He said nothing (standing conduct forbids promises). PENDING QUESTION 2.
- Weeks 22 to 23: harvest begun in the valley, good grain; the people's mood as the god has seen it: warming to him and still wary; Oxmouth and Wellbrook divided over the posts; Aldford reserved, Tam's house grieving; Fallstead glad of its boatman.
- Other pending questions carried by the people and not yet put to him in person: whether the weir may take the whole run or the gate and the tenth stand (Oxmouth); whether the posts are his own old work (Maren); whether the posts should be dug, marked or left (Maren and Hobb differ); what the lights are and whether watchers are wanted (Aldford). These were spoken among the headmen; only 1 and 2 above were put to the god in person. The DM decides how they reach the player under DESIGN 12.9.

## B. The board at the moment of contact: week 23, evening, the pool below the falls
The Weir-King stands knee-deep in the Ald at the foot of the first falls, below Fallstead, where he has stood most of the day learning the water. The falls are at his back, loud, throwing spray; the pool is black and the light is going, a low sun behind the wood on the west bank catching the top of the spray. Fallstead is three hundred paces downstream on the east bank; smoke from its fires; two children on the staithe looking his way. The east bank path comes down from the hill track through the last trees to the pool's edge.

Down that path, openly, in sight of the village, a woman is walking toward him. She is tall, plain-faced, in a hill traveler's grey wool and leather, a pack and a staff, no weapon he can see. Her hands hang at her sides and they are wet, though she has not touched the river. She is the nearer pull; he knows it as she comes the way he knows water. She stops at the water's edge ten paces from him, on the stones, and looks at him. She has not spoken. The children on the staithe have seen her. Nothing else has happened.

Nothing in the god's own thread after this moment is resolved. The god may speak, retreat, go into the water, call to the village, or anything else; the DM plays it live and the Thornmere agent (SPOILERS_do_not_open/campaigns/dryrun/gods/thornmere/, the DM does not read it; the Keeper or the day session brings her agent in) speaks for her. Her words are hers.

## C. Resolved for the world after the moment of contact; tell only after the scene
- Harvest finished in weeks 24 and 25, good; a winter's barley and a tenth over.
- Week 27: the council of headmen sat at Oxmouth: who keeps the trap and the gate; the catch shared by headcount with a tenth held for the poor; the posts (Hobb: mark and leave; Maren: open); the sky-light counts (ten villages four, two villages three, hidden by cloud); and who may ask the god things.
- Week 32: the autumn run came with two gales off the sea; boats lay ashore two days. The weir held through both gales and the run. The trap-pen took about four tons of fish, dried and salted, and a tenth went back alive through the gate.
- Midwinter: the fourth sky-light of the year, over the sea to the west. The true count for the year is four (tools/phenomena.py).
- Winter ordinary; the marsh creeks froze at the edges three weeks. No one else of note died. Births about 183, deaths 169 as the people count them; about five thousand people.
- What the people would report at year's end, in their numbers: about five thousand, a few more than last year; a good harvest, a winter's grain and a little over; a weir built in seventeen days by sixty hands, and it held; four lights; black posts under the Oxmouth mud, the headmen divided; four hundred spears; Hask and Brin home; salt and dried fish in the villages, not much to spare.
- The god's own actions after week 23 (the rest of his walk, where he winters, his watching of the sky) depend on the scene and are unresolved; the civ agent and the Keeper resolve them next night from the player's decisions.
