# Keeper release to the Riverfolk civ agent, Year 1, pass one: what the people experience

Season opens in early spring. The Weir-King woke on the shingle bar at Oxmouth a few days before the year began; the people have seen him and are wary and curious.

## Weather and the river
- Early spring: the Ald runs high and brown with snowmelt from the wood for about three weeks. The current is strong; a boat going upstream makes half its usual day. The water meadows at Aldford are under water. Fords are shut.
- Late spring: fair. The river falls to its summer level by about the sixth week of the year.
- Summer: dry and warm. The river is low and clear. The ford at Aldford opens about midsummer.
- Autumn: ordinary rains. Two gales come off the sea in the week of the autumn fish run; one is hard enough to keep every boat ashore for two days and drives the tide high into the marsh.
- Winter: ordinary. The marsh creeks freeze at their edges for about three weeks around midwinter. The river itself does not freeze. Snow lies on the hills to the east.

## The year's work, as the land allows it
- Spring sowing of barley in its five-week window after the water goes down from the meadows; harvest in late summer, about three to four weeks of every hand.
- The spring and autumn fish runs come as usual. The autumn run is good.
- Reeds and timber as usual; oak is in the valley, stone is scarce in the marsh and must be fetched from the headland at the marsh's north end or from upriver.

## Things the people see
- Sky-lights: the people who look up see three this year before the autumn and a fourth in winter: one in early spring, four nights after the god woke, rising over the sea to the west; one in late spring over the wood to the north; one at midsummer over the hills to the east; one at midwinter over the sea to the west. Each is a single steady pale light that climbs slowly from below the horizon, for about as long as it takes to walk two hundred paces, dims, and is gone high up. Where the god asked elders to count, their counts are these.
- At Oxmouth, if the people drive posts for a weir or fish-trap in the river mouth at low water this summer, the mauls strike old timber under the mud a few yards from the present bank: a line of black, hard, water-worn posts, cut square at the top by an axe, standing in two rows, older than anything the Riverfolk have built. The elders know a tale of "the ones who went under".
- Nothing else out of the ordinary is seen by the people this year: no strangers in the valley, no raiders, no sickness beyond the usual.

## The god's decisions (from player/decisions-001.md), as they reach the people
1. The Weir-King walks the Ald from the marsh to the first falls, touching the water at every village, to learn the river. He is at Wellbrook about the fifth week of spring, Aldford about the ninth week, Fallstead below the falls by late summer. He observes, avoids strangers, and does not give orders beyond the decisions below.
2. The Riverfolk are to raise a weir and fish-trap at Oxmouth before the autumn run. Pace table: a weir is 6 worker-days per yard of river width; the Ald at Oxmouth is tidal and about 60 yards across at the chosen line. Decide who does the work, when, and how the people take the order against their code (no one takes from the river without giving back). Report the design and the labor; the Keeper rolls whether it holds through the autumn run and the gales.
3. Two boatmen are to go up past the falls to see what the hill country holds, no farther than ten days out, then come back. Above the falls the river shrinks fast into old wood; the boatmen will be on foot within a day. Decide who goes and when (the spring flood makes an early start unwise). Report their departure and the Keeper will resolve what they find and whether they return.
4. The god watches the sky-lights each night and asks the elders at each village to count them too. Report the elders' counts and what the villages say about the lights.
