# Riverfolk, Year 1

Numbers used: births 182, deaths 168 (infants 41, the old 72, drownings and accidents 11, childbed 6, the rest ordinary fevers). Population 5,000 to 5,014.

## Spring
The Ald ran high and brown for three weeks. Upstream boats made half a day, the Aldford meadows lay under water, the fords were shut. The people who first reached the Weir-King on the Oxmouth bar said nothing and left a reed bundle on the shingle. He took it as they meant it, and the villages heard so.

The river fell to summer level in week 6. Barley went in during weeks 4 to 8, Oxmouth and Wellbrook first, Aldford last as the meadows dried. About 3,800 acres by hoe, five worker-days an acre: 19,000 worker-days, some 2,800 working adults, seven days each at spring pace. Fish carry the rest of the table. The spring run came as usual. Salt was boiled at the Oxmouth pans and the marsh villages and went upriver by boat; barley and ash poles came down. That is the whole trade this year, village to village.

The Weir-King came to Wellbrook in week 5 and to Aldford in week 9, and put his hand in the water at each staithe. People came to him there with asks. What he answered is his own, and three of those asks are held (see the report).

The elders' counts of the sky-lights: the first in early spring over the western sea, the second in late spring over the wood. At Wellbrook and one hill-edge village cloud hid the second.

## The boatmen
Hask of Fallstead and Brin of Aldford were named by the two headmen after the flood. A dugout was built at Fallstead in weeks 11 to 13 (30 worker-days, five hands). They set out at first light in week 14, the opening days of summer, with barley meal and dried fish for twenty days, spears and slings. They carried the dugout round the falls, poled a day above, hid it in a pool, and went east on foot into the old wood, ten days out and ten back.

## Summer: the weir
Line: across the Ald about 300 yards above the shingle bar, 60 yards bank to bank at low water, set 8 yards downstream of the old posts so none are touched. Two rows of oak posts, 60 to a row, a yard apart; stone and brush packed between; wicker hurdles on the upstream face; a trap-pen of wicker at the midstream; and a giving gate four yards wide on the west side, hurdles lifted out on the dark of every moon and on the first night of each run. A tenth of each haul goes back alive.

Labor: 60 yards at 6 is 360 worker-days. Posts can be driven only on the low-water hours, about half a working day, so 720. Add 120 for felling the posts upriver (floated down a day), 90 for twenty tons of stone fetched in ten boatloads from the north headland, 60 for hurdles. Total about 990. Sixty at a time, rotating from a pool of ninety (thirty from Oxmouth, the rest from Wellbrook and the three marsh villages), seventeen work days, weeks 16 to 19.

The headmen took the order against the code. Maren, headman of Oxmouth, ruled that a weir which takes must give: hence the gate and the tenth. Old fishers still say a trap across the whole river takes more than a gift can balance.

On the first low water of week 17 the mauls struck black posts under the mud near the east bank, two rows, square-cut at the top. Maren said these were the Weir-King's own first weir, and that he had come to build it again. Hobb, headman of Wellbrook, said they were the roof-trees of a drowned house and a grave, and would not send his men to drive near them; the line was moved down eight yards to quiet him. Elders tell the old tale of the ones who went under: they took too much and the sea took them back. Nobody dug.

That night the third light rose over the eastern hills. Many at Oxmouth hold the posts called it up. The Aldford wise-woman says the lights mark where the river will rise, and wants watchers set. Oxmouth holds they are the lamps of the drowned, climbing home.

Harvest, weeks 22 to 25: 3,800 acres at 3 worker-days is 11,400, four days a hand, stretched to four weeks by weather and the sharing of sickles. Good grain. Threshing through winter.

## Autumn and winter
The council of headmen sat at Oxmouth in week 27. It spoke of: who keeps the trap and the gate; how the catch is shared between villages (by headcount, a tenth held back for the poor); the old posts, which Hobb wanted marked and left, Maren wanted opened; the sky-light counts, ten villages with four, two with three, none agreeing what they are; and what to ask the Weir-King and who may ask. Of the boatmen it spoke as the news stood that day; their fate is the Keeper's.

The autumn run came in week 32 with two gales; boats lay ashore two days. What the weir did is not written here.

Winter was ordinary. Marsh creeks froze at the edges three weeks. The fourth light rose over the western sea at midwinter. Barley holds a full winter and a tenth over.

## Mood
Oxmouth: awed, divided over the posts. Wellbrook: warm to the god, uneasy at the posts. Aldford: reserved, gladder after the ford opened. Fallstead: anxious for its boatman. Factions: the First-Weir people (Maren) and the Graveward (Hobb). Nothing worse.
