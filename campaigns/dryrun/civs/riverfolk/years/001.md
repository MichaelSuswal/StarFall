# Riverfolk, Year 1

Numbers used: births 183, deaths 169 (infants 41, the old 72, drownings and accidents 11, childbed 7, the rest ordinary fevers). Population 5,000 to 5,014.

## Spring
The Ald ran high and brown for three weeks. Upstream boats made half a day, the Aldford meadows lay under water, the fords were shut. The people who first reached the Weir-King on the Oxmouth bar said nothing and left a reed bundle on the shingle. He took it as they meant it, and the villages heard so.

The river fell to summer level in week 6. Barley went in during weeks 4 to 8, Oxmouth and Wellbrook first, Aldford last as the meadows dried. About 3,800 acres by hoe, five worker-days an acre: 19,000 worker-days, some 2,800 working adults, seven days each at spring pace. Fish carry the rest of the table. The spring run came as usual. Salt was boiled at the Oxmouth pans and the marsh villages and went upriver by boat; barley and ash poles came down. That is the whole trade this year, village to village.

The Weir-King came to Wellbrook in week 5 and to Aldford in week 9, and put his hand in the water at each staithe. At Wellbrook the headman and fishers asked him for a law of giving at the weir. He said nothing.

At Aldford, at nightfall, Tam the net-maker begged him at the water: his wife Ede lay in a hard labor. The god stayed standing in the river and said nothing. Ede died before dawn. The daughter lived. Tam has not spoken to the god since and will not go to the staithe. Aldford is honest about it. Some say a god who wets his hand at every staithe but will not cross one doorway is no king. The wise-woman says he is the river's and the river does not come to the bed. Most say only that Ede asked nothing of anyone and that he watched. Aldford has been cool to him since.

The elders' counts of the sky-lights: the first in early spring over the western sea, the second in late spring over the wood. All twelve villages counted both.

## The boatmen
Hask of Fallstead and Brin of Aldford were named by the two headmen after the flood. A dugout was built at Fallstead in weeks 11 to 13 (30 worker-days, five hands). They set out at first light in week 14, the opening days of summer, with barley meal and dried fish for twenty days, spears and slings. They carried the dugout round the falls, poled a day above, hid it in a pool, and went east on foot into the old wood, ten days out and ten back.

Hask's mother left a reed bundle on the Fallstead staithe each dusk until he came home, and gave a fish back to the river on the night he returned. She asked the god nothing.

They came home unhurt in week 17, within their time. They found: a stream above the falls within a day; two and a half days east through old wood without a track; open hill country of sheep and heather under a grey snow range to the north; on the fifth day grassed banks and ditches on a crest, like a fort; on the sixth a dark mere; on the seventh a high steading where herders with dogs took them to Grandmother Ceth, an old woman with one clouded eye, of a hill people who call themselves the Carn. The Carn kept them two nights and fed them. They asked about the river people, about the god who woke on the bar, and whether the river people count the lights. Tall poles notched top to bottom stand by the Carn houses; the Carn would not say what for. The Carn's word: the river people are to come no farther than the wood's edge, and the Carn will come down to see the river themselves.

Brin told it at Aldford in week 18, a little taller each time. Hask told it to the god and the village at Fallstead the day they came home in week 17, short and flat, and it is Hask's version the headmen trust. What the people make of it: that the Carn are kin of the Weir-King, since they ask after the lights; that the notched poles are tallies of the drowned; that "come no farther" is a warning to be heeded, and "we will come down" is a visit of honor, or a threat, depending on the village. Fallstead is uneasy. Brin's wife is proud and frightened.

## Summer: the weir
Line: across the Ald about 300 yards above the shingle bar, 60 yards bank to bank at low water, set 8 yards downstream of the old posts so none are touched. Two rows of oak posts, 60 to a row, a yard apart; stone and brush packed between; wicker hurdles on the upstream face; a trap-pen of wicker at the midstream; and a giving gate four yards wide on the west side, hurdles lifted out on the dark of every moon and on the first night of each run. A tenth of each haul goes back alive.

Labor: 60 yards at 6 is 360 worker-days. Posts can be driven only on the low-water hours, about half a working day, so 720. Add 120 for felling the posts upriver (floated down a day), 90 for twenty tons of stone fetched in ten boatloads from the north headland, 60 for hurdles. Total about 990. Sixty at a time, rotating from a pool of ninety (thirty from Oxmouth, the rest from Wellbrook and the three marsh villages), seventeen work days, weeks 16 to 19.

The headmen took the order against the code. Maren, headman of Oxmouth, ruled that a weir which takes must give: hence the gate and the tenth. Old fishers still say a trap across the whole river takes more than a gift can balance.

On the first low water of week 17 the mauls struck black posts under the mud near the east bank, two rows, square-cut at the top. Maren said these were the Weir-King's own first weir, and that he had come to build it again. Hobb, headman of Wellbrook, said they were the roof-trees of a drowned house and a grave, and would not send his men to drive near them; the line was moved down eight yards to quiet him. Elders tell the old tale of the ones who went under: they took too much and the sea took them back. Nobody dug.

In week 20, at midsummer, the third light rose over the eastern hills. Three weeks had passed since the posts. Oxmouth counts it as the drowned answering a weir finished and the gift kept: the posts were the first sign, the light the second, lamps climbing home. The Aldford wise-woman says the lights mark where the river will rise, and wants watchers set. Fallstead, listening to Hask, says the Carn ask after them, so they know more than we do.

Harvest, weeks 22 to 25: 3,800 acres at 3 worker-days is 11,400, four days a hand, stretched to four weeks by weather and the sharing of sickles. Good grain. Threshing through winter.

## Autumn and winter
The council of headmen sat at Oxmouth in week 27. It spoke of: who keeps the trap and the gate; how the catch is shared between villages (by headcount, a tenth held back for the poor); the old posts, which Hobb wanted marked and left, Maren wanted opened; the sky-light counts, all twelve villages with three before autumn, none agreeing what they are; what to ask the Weir-King and who may ask; and the Carn: whether to send anyone to the wood's edge (the council said no, and set a watch at the last ash grove above Fallstead), and whether to welcome the Carn if they come.

In week 16, at dusk, a stranger woman, tall and plain, in hill traveler's grey, came down from the wood's edge to the god on the east bank a little below the village. Two children on the Fallstead staithe saw it. The people know nothing more.

The autumn run came in week 32 with two gales; boats lay ashore two days. The weir held. The trap-pen took about four tons, dried and salted, and a tenth went back through the gate.

Winter was ordinary. Marsh creeks froze at the edges three weeks. The fourth light rose over the western sea at midwinter. Barley holds a full winter and a tenth over.

## Mood
Oxmouth: awed, divided over the posts. Wellbrook: warm to the god, uneasy at the posts. Aldford: cool toward the god after Ede's death, Tam silent, gladder of the ford. Fallstead: relieved at its boatman, uneasy about the Carn. Factions: the First-Weir people (Maren) and the Graveward (Hobb). Nothing worse.
