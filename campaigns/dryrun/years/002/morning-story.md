This is your first year, and it is early summer in it, the sixteenth week since you woke on the shingle bar. You are standing knee-deep in the Ald at the foot of the first falls, a little below Fallstead, where you have stood most of today and most days since you came up here three weeks ago. The spray from the falls comes down on you and makes no difference; you were wet before it reached you, as you are in any weather. You are unhurt and at full strength, for you have not been a day's walk from running water since you woke. The river is in you the whole way down, every pool and ford from the lip of the falls to the marsh, and at the mouth, forty miles off, a narrowing you have felt for several days now, the Ald being gathered there between two lines. Above the falls the river gives you nothing; you have touched nothing there. The two pulls you woke with are still with you.

The Riverfolk, some five thousand of them in twelve villages along the river, are at their summer work, the barley in and the river at its low level, and they are warming to you and wary of you at once. Oxmouth is glad of you. Wellbrook is kindly and watches. Aldford has been cool since the ninth week. Fallstead, three hundred paces downstream of you, is anxious for its boatman. They call you the Weir-King.

You woke on the shingle bar where the Ald goes out into the marsh, and the first thing you knew, before you knew the river, was two pulls in you, both to the east, on two bearings a little apart: one east and a little north, the other due east. Direction only; nothing of how far or how strong. The first of the Riverfolk to reach you on the bar said nothing to you. They left a bundle of reeds on the shingle and went. You took it as they meant it, and the villages heard that you had.

On the fourth night after you woke, from the bar, you saw a light over the sea to the west: a single steady pale light that rose slowly from below the horizon, climbed a hand's breadth above it in about the time it takes to walk two hundred paces, dimmed, and was gone.

Then you began to walk, as you had decided, up the Ald from the bar, touching the water at every village and staying about a week at each. The river ran high and brown for the first three weeks of spring; the Aldford meadows were under it and the fords were shut. It fell to its summer level in the sixth week, and the barley went in between the fourth week and the eighth.

In the fourth and fifth weeks one of the two pulls began to change its bearing, a little each night, swinging from east and a little north toward due east. By the time you reached Wellbrook in the fifth week it lay due east of you, and from then on, as you went north up the valley, it stayed due east of you instead of falling away behind you to the south-east as a thing standing still would have. The other pull was due east the whole time and never changed. One of the two moves when you move.

At Wellbrook, in the fifth week, the headman, Hobb, came down to you at the staithe with a knot of fishers behind him and asked you for a law of giving at the weir you had ordered: what the river must be given back for what the weir takes. You watched them and said nothing.

At Aldford, in the ninth week, at nightfall, a net-maker named Tam came to you at the water and begged you to save his wife, Ede, who was in a hard labor in their house. You stayed standing in the river and said nothing. He went back to the house. Before dawn Ede died. The child, a daughter, lived. Tam has not come down to the staithe since. Aldford speaks of it plainly. Some say a god who wets his hand at every staithe but will not cross one doorway is no king. The wise-woman there says you are the river's and the river does not come to the bed. Most say only that you watched. Aldford has been cool to you since.

In the tenth week, from Aldford, you saw the second light of the year, over the wood to the north, climbing a quarter of the way up the sky with the same slow rise and the same fading. Two so far. The elders in all twelve villages, counting at your asking, have the same two.

You reached Fallstead and the first falls in the thirteenth week, the end of the walk you had set yourself, and you have stayed at the falls since, in the water most days, learning it. The river gives itself to you up to the lip of the falls and no farther. Above them the Ald comes out of old wood; the Riverfolk call it the wood above the falls and know only that hill country lies beyond it. From the valley, on clear days, you have seen the hills rising to the east and, far to the north-east, a grey range with snow on it. From the falls, the moving pull lay east and a little north, toward the wood's edge and the hills.

By the time you reached Fallstead the headman there had named two men for your errand: Hask of Fallstead, a boatman of about thirty-eight, wide and weathered, with two fingers gone from his left hand, and Brin of Aldford, a boatman of about twenty-two, lean and quick-eyed. A dugout had been building since the eleventh week and was done in the thirteenth. In the fourteenth week, at first light and in your sight, they carried it round the falls and went up. They were to pole a day above, hide the boat, and go east on foot into the wood, ten days out and ten back, as you had decided. Hask's wife and his mother watched them go. They are not yet due back.

In the fifteenth week, at dusk, from the pool below the falls, you saw a figure standing on the rock shoulder at the top of the falls on the east side, against the last light, upright and still, too far and too dark for a face or for whether it was a man or a woman, for about the time it takes to draw a net. When you looked again it was gone. The moving pull at that moment lay north-east, toward the shoulder.

In this sixteenth week you felt the Ald's mouth begin to change, forty miles down, over several days, as if the river there were being narrowed and held between two lines, and then word came up by boat of what it was. The weir at Oxmouth had been begun, on the line that Maren, the headman of Oxmouth, chose; she is a widow, and the Riverfolk have the one word for the office. The line runs about three hundred yards above the shingle bar, sixty yards bank to bank at low water: two rows of oak posts with stone and brush between them, wicker hurdles on the upstream face, a wicker trap-pen in midstream, and on the west side a giving gate four yards wide, to be lifted on the dark of every moon and on the first night of each run, with a tenth of every haul put back alive. Maren set the gate and the tenth herself, as the code's answer to the weir. About sixty are at the work at a time.

That brings you to this evening. The falls are at your back and to your right, loud, throwing spray; the pool is black and the light is going, a low sun behind the wood on the west bank catching the top of the spray. The fishers have gone in. Fallstead is three hundred paces downstream on the east bank: smoke, a dog, two children still on the staithe looking your way. The east bank path comes down from the wood's edge through the last trees to the stones at the pool's side.

Down that path, openly, a woman is walking toward you. She is tall and plain-faced, in a hill traveler's grey wool and leather, with a pack and a staff and no weapon you can see. Her hands hang at her sides and they are wet, though she has not touched the river. The moving pull is where she is; you know it as she comes, the way you know water. She stops at the water's edge ten paces from you, on the stones, and looks at you. She has not spoken. The children on the staithe have seen her.
