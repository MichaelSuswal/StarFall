# Chronicle of the people, Year 1

The Riverfolk are about five thousand. They say a few more than a year ago: about 180 born, about 170 dead. They sowed barley in the spring weeks and reaped a good harvest in late summer; they hold a winter's grain and a little over. Salt and dried fish went up and down the river by boat. About 400 men can fight with spear and sling. There are no walls.

At your order they raised a weir across the Ald above Oxmouth: two rows of oak posts, stone and brush between, a wicker trap-pen, and a gate left open on the dark of the moon and at the start of each run, with a tenth of each haul returned alive, so the code is kept. About sixty worked at a time, for seventeen days in midsummer. Whether it served the autumn run is for the Keeper's word.

Under the mud they found two rows of old black posts. Oxmouth says they are your first weir. Wellbrook says a grave. Both headmen ask what you want done with them.

Two boatmen, Hask of Fallstead and Brin of Aldford, went upriver in the first week of summer on your errand.

The elders counted four sky-lights: early spring, late spring, midsummer, midwinter. Most villages counted all four. Oxmouth calls them the lamps of the drowned. Aldford says they mark where the river will rise.

They call you the Weir-King, and they are warming to you.
