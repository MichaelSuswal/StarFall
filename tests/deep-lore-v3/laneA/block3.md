# Block 3. Years 21 to 30

## Year 21. The 332nd year after the Driving

- In the fourth month you had the witch's cord bundles carried up to the Greyholt wall on a whey night and laid out on the stones, and Ket came down from the moor with three Harl to look at them, because the keepers had slept on it and said it was a good night. No Harl ties such knots or can undo one. An old Harl woman, Ket's mother's sister, turned one over for a long time and said they were the wrist-string knots the valley ties on its babies, only grown up, and that the Harl do not tie them because the valley's old king stole them from the men who hung the Low One, and tied them to take the cold out of a river, and the Low One took his name for payment, so that he is called the King Without, and the bare round above Greyholt is where his cold went. She said the Harl have told that story since before the walked men came. She would not touch a second bundle.
- In the sixth month, with the nine open wells visibly full, Aske and three Greyholt men put a lever under the capstone of the eleventh and it came up on the fourth try. There is no water under it. It is a dry chamber cut like the others, with shelves cut into the rock in rings, and on the shelves heaps of grey dust the shape of coiled rope, and in every heap a fired clay tag with signs on it, two hundred of them near enough, and on the lowest shelf a bundle of thin lead sheets the size of a hand, scratched in rows of signs from edge to edge, eleven sheets, tied with a cord that broke when lifted. The Greyholt men say it is the town's tally of debts, which is why it was sealed, so no one could collect. The tags and the sheets came down to Tollan in two baskets and Orla has them.
- At the count you brought Aldo Rook and the elders of Tollan to the Stone Row together and showed them, on the eighth stone, the fourth sign, and the same sign on the Ossary tag and the witch's tag, and told them what the Harl say of the walked men, and what the board from the hill shows, and named the price of the hill: that the Driving be told with the lords walking out onto the moor not knowing their own names, and the Rook half of Scarrow back in the count and its tenth to Tollan. Tollan's elders would not alter the telling. Hob said the god had heard a Harl boast and a quarryman's gold and asked Tollan to unsay its grandmothers for it. Weir's elder said tell it both ways and see which the children remember. Aldo took the hill, and the tenth went back to Tollan, and the Rook half stood on Scarrow's stone again with the other half glaring. On the third night the Driving was told as it has always been told, and when it was done Weir's elder stood and told the moor's telling after it, flat, in two sentences. The children acted the lords and ran away shrieking, except the Rook children, who walked off slowly with their heads down, which someone had taught them as mockery and which they did very well. The valley is still arguing about which was worse.
- The Skelder came to the spring market with few pelts and said their god went north herself two years ago with forty and came back last autumn with twenty-eight, all of them as the three are, and fewer dogs, and that she sits with them and says nothing of it, and that the bands are frightened of her now and love her more. The Lotfolk trader Cass, at the autumn market, says his god's ring of stone around its city is a quarter built, every living name cut in it, and the city says the names aloud every night, which takes until the small hours. He says the god does it to keep the lot honest.
- After the count you went north-west again, with Col to pace and Ket to speak, and the keepers' leave. At Gorm Longhand's fold, empty for the winter, you had Col measure the gate: the uprights stand a little more than a man's arm-span apart, and the notch in the cross-stone is a hand wide and a hand deep and square. You lay behind it when the Lamp was up and looked through the notch, and the notch frames empty sky, a hand's breadth above the Lamp. Col sighted from the gate along the moor road and said the line of it runs north-west over the Harl rises. Eight days on at Cold Hand Col paced from the fallen stone to where the two short stones sit half sunk, and they stand the same arm-span and a little apart as the gate's uprights, and the fallen stone's notch is the same hand square. Nothing is left to sight through. From Cold Hand the line the two stones would have made, if the long one stood on them, runs toward the Lamp. From Fallow Dip the keepers, six of them with Ket, slept and said you might go on, and that the Low One was sending you to the water. Then fifteen days west and a little north across moor that turned under your feet into something else: heather giving way to grass, then to a country with more kinds of bird in a morning than the Backs have in a year, flowers no one in your party had a name for, springs that taste of iron and springs that taste of nothing and one that is warm. You stopped a day short, where the Harl say the one-color beasts turn back, and there is nothing there. The ground is the same ground. The grass is the same grass. Col found a lark's nest with six eggs in it, in the twelfth month.

## Year 22

- Hush Water. Two days across, flat and grey and without a ripple though the wind off the moor bent the grass flat at its edge, and no ice at the margin in the first month though the puddles a mile back were frozen hard. Col waded out to his chest and said the water was warmer at his feet than at his chest, and came out quickly and would not say why. Reed huts along the eastern shore, and beyond them long grass mounds the people there will not walk on and walk round. They point. They have words for the lake, the fish, the reeds, the weather, the mounds, and for you; they have no word for each other and do not seem to miss one. A half-day north along the shore, forty Lotfolk families in square huts, ten years there: the grown ones still speak names and cast their stones at every choice, and their children, who were born there or came small, point, and the parents have stopped correcting them. One Lotfolk man scratches on a lead sheet every evening and would not show it. At midnight the Lamp stands straight overhead at Hush Water, so that a man's shadow is under his feet and he has none. On your fourth night an old man of the shore people, who had let you sit by his fire because you did not point at him, said that his grandparents had names, and his father had a name he did not use, and the water did not want them. He said it as a man says the wind is from the east. Everyone who slept on that shore dreamed every night: tall figures with no faces, standing in a row, counting; and houses on poles above shallow water that is not this water. Col dreamed it and would not sleep a second night within sight of the shore and went back a mile to the frozen puddles.
- The dim night came in the second month. The Lamp faded, and the blue hair came down out of it and fell on the water, two days out, in the middle, and the water took it and did not move, and nothing came out of the water, and nothing went up but the thread itself, which was gone when the Lamp was bright. You were asleep when the Lamp faded, because you had meant to be. The faceless ones were not counting this time. They stood in their row and one of them, or all of them, offered you a thing you cannot do: to be heard at every hearth in your valley at once, as the Lamp is seen from every hearth at once, in exchange for what they called weight. You woke before you answered, because the Lamp brightened and the Lotfolk children were pointing at it. You have the sense that the question will keep.
- You came back by Fallow Dip and the moor and Hagg Ridge in the thaw and reached the Stone Row in the fourth month, lighter than you have ever been, and the valley's first spring festival since you came that you were not there for had been held without you, because Orla said it should be. Ket stayed at the cairn. Col has not gone back up the Backs.
- Orla's year on the signs. She sorted every tag, the stone, the board, the ledge signs Aske copied for her in charcoal from the hill chamber, the witch's tag and the fen plate, the way she sorts knots, by shape, and counted. Tags, stone, board, ledges and the witch's tag are one set of signs, about forty shapes with small differences, and the Vell tags and lead sheets are the same set again and more of it. The plate is another set entirely, with no shape in common. The board's scraped layers are all in the tag set, all three cuttings; the later cuttings are shallower and fewer and some signs are begun and not finished. On the lead sheets every row begins with a group of two to four signs like the groups on a neck tag, and ends with one of a handful of signs that recur; the eighth stone's line, eight signs, has three of those row-ending signs in it and no sign that looks like a neck tag's group, and the sign that is on every Ossary tag is on the stone once. Orla says she can tell you which signs are in it and which are not, and that anyone who tells you what the line says is guessing, and she will not.
- In the eighth month you went south down the Greel with Orla and the tag box, by Greelmen boats and then on foot, five weeks, and came to the Fire Hollow at the Gathering. Forty bands in the grass, more fires than the valley has hearths, and Isca a head taller than the tallest Ruun, with the iron band and the grey cords to her feet. She did not rise. She said: "The Olm has come. It has been eleven years coming and the place has been kept, as I said it would be, and now it will be filled, and nothing that is filled in the Song is ever emptied. Stand where you are and give the Ruun your names, every one, in the order you keep them, and do not leave one out, because if you leave one out I will know it is missing for as long as I am." You sang them, with Orla at your shoulder with the cords: eight villages, the nine pools, the Stone Row, the Crown Fold, the Brann Cut, the steadings, and then the living by household as the cords have them, which took the rest of the night. Eight thousand Ruun took them up line by line and sang them back, and you were heavier while they sang, a little, the way you are at a harvest festival, and by the Reed Crossing on the way home it had gone. Haddo sang the Kneeling Song after, before the whole Gathering, and the Ruun wept at it, and Isca passed into fire halfway through and back again, and said to you when it was done, in the manner of a throne: "That is the finest thing a mouth of mine has ever made, and I do not know why I would rather break it than hear it, and I will not break it, because I made him. You slept by the stone that gave it. Tell me what the stone is." She waited. She does not wait well. Whatever you said, she heard it, and she gave you forty horses and a Ruun herald to live at Tollan, and said the Olm's fires and hers were one fire now and that she would come when called and would come when not called. Orla's cords in the spring were the same length as the autumn's.

## Year 23

- Dast's price was eleven of Isca's horses, and he was pleased to have them. You went east with the autumn caravan after the market, across the Reed Crossing, and eleven days along the southern edge of the flats to the last well, which is a stone-lined hole in a crust of white with a hut beside it and the bones of camels in a ring round it. From the last well the Lamp hangs well to the west of where it hangs over Tollan, and lower, so that a man facing it has the flats at his back. The flats are white to the edge of seeing, with no shell in them and no fish bone and no line where a shore was, and the salt wind that reaches the valley in late summer is the ordinary wind here. Four camel men went one day east from the well with a stone each, their names cut on them by a Tollan mason who learned the shapes from Cass's letters, and food, and a flag to find the well again. They came back the second evening with salt to the knee and nothing else wrong with them, knowing their names, their mothers' names, and what they thought of the errand. Dast says the stones worked, and will say it at every well from here to the Reed Crossing for the rest of his life. He also says the steps are ten days on, and that you should go now that you have seen the stones work, and that his price for that is the same again. You went home. Three dim nights on the flats, and from there the blue hair came down far to the west-north-west, across the whole of the sky.
- In the ninth month, while you were on the flats, a storm came up the Greel from the south, a day and a night of wind and then rain for three days, and the Merrow Fen rose over its stilts. Two Fenner steadings near the Olm mouth went into the water with everything in them and nine people. The Fenners say the fen is remembering it was a lake. Ossary took in the living.
- The Ruun herald who lives at Tollan, Caddo, is a tall quiet man who sings the Olm's names in the Long Song's order every evening at the Stone Row to keep them in his mouth, and children have started to stand with him and do it too, and the Tollan elders do not know whether to stop it.
- Wat, who is the ferryman's man at Weir now, pulled a drowning Greelman out of the flood at the ferry landing in the storm and was given a bronze knife by the boat family, and Ferrow Ford says it always knew the river would be good for something.
- Four dim nights this year by the hides; the two you missed were seen from the Backs and the blue hair fell on the same bearing it always does. Orla has started a third cord for the year's dim nights and a fourth for deaths of stock.

## Year 24

- In the sixth month a Skelder hunter came down the Greel's west bank alone with forty pelts on a sledge, a hound pup, grey-white and long-legged and silent, and one question, which he asked at the Stone Row in these words and no others: "Does your god remember the sky?" He will carry back one answer and no second. He has not pressed for it. He is lodged at Weir, hunts for his keep, feeds the pup first, and asks nothing of anyone. The pup eats, which the Weir man says the other one never did.
- Greelmen at Weir say a new Ruun fire has been lit at the Reed Crossing, where the caravans ford, eleven bands under the young singer who sang in the valley, and they sing a song about a crowd kneeling all night, and the Gathering has not come after them, yet, and the salt caravans are going round by a worse ford and cursing.
- Caddo the herald, asked by Hob what Isca thinks of the singer's new fire, said that Isca does not think, she remembers, and that the Voice is in the Song and so is everything he does.
- The Fenners rebuilt the two drowned steadings a mile further up the fen and kept the old names for them, which Ossary says is asking for it.
- Orla's cords at the count were short, and she said so to you, and Hob said the storm took nine and the winter took its usual and that two old women had made a prophecy out of arithmetic.
- Five dim nights, and on one of them in the twelfth month the Skelder hunter at Weir sat up with the lamp-watch like everyone and said, in his own tongue, something the Weir man took to mean that his people do this too and that their god began it.

## Year 25

- In the fifth month three Lotfolk came up the valley road in grey, with a bag of stones each and a letter cut into a sheet of lead the size of a shield, which they unrolled on the eighth stone's step and read aloud in a voice the valley took for a child's. It proposed, in the manner of a court, a count of gods: that each god tell the others what it has measured, "so that no measure is lost in one hand." It stated the number of households in the Olm valley as of last autumn, and Orla checked it against the cords that night, and it is right to the one. It offered, in exchange for the Olm's answer, "the height of the Coin, which is lower by the eye of a pin in thirteen years as measured from two pins set in Ennow's cliff in the god's second year," and "what the stones carried back, which is this: eleven of two hundred returned, and their stones with them, and the names were on the stones, and the names were not in the eleven." The three counters say they will wait at Tollan for the Olm's answer until the lot says otherwise, and cast their stones every morning, and the lot has not yet said otherwise.
- In the eighth month Greelmen came up to Weir with the news as fast as the river would bring it: the Gathering came to the Reed Crossing as fire in the night and the Voice is dead and two hundred with him and eleven bands are scattered, and the ford is open again, and the ash is still warm. Caddo the herald sat at the Stone Row all that night and sang the Olm's names and then the whole Long Song, alone, and when he reached the Voice's name he sang it, because he said she would have, and his voice did not change on it, and he said hers would.
- A Lissom girl walked the valley tied at the wrist to a Lotfolk counter who had been at Tollan three months, and they arrived, and the other two counters cast their stones over it and say the lot allows it. Lissom is not sure the lot was asked.
- The spotted eel has not been taken at Dun Mere in fifteen years and the smokehouses have stopped keeping a string for it. The Dun Mere elder says the pike is still in the tarn.
- Four dim nights.

## Year 26

- The spring flood was the greatest in memory. The Olm ran over the Tollan meadow to the foot of the Stone Row and stood on the first step of the eighth stone for a day. At Ossary the whole bank gave at once, a bowshot of it, and more graves opened than the village has people, in their rows, at both depths, and the river carried off what it wanted and left the rest in the field. The headman had everything gathered that could be gathered, and there are sixty-one tags in the box at Tollan now and a third bronze needle, and the bones went up the hill in carts and were buried with the words for a week. Ossary wants to move the village.
- In the fifth month Isca came up the valley with two thousand Ruun on horses, which took a day to pass Tollan, and camped on the meadow, and the valley had never seen so many of anything but geese. She came to the Stone Row as a woman and spoke to you before Tollan and said: "The stone in the west made a song that made a fire that I had to put out with my own hand, and I have sung his name once and will sing it every autumn as long as I am, and I will not have the stone make another. I ride to break it. Ride with me or watch me go." She did not wait for an answer and rode at dawn, and the Ruun went up through Greyholt and over Hagg Ridge in a column that was still passing at dark. In the eighth month they came back down the ridge, fewer. The Ruun who would talk say she burned the cairn to glass, that the six keepers would not leave the stone and died by it, that she became fire and sat on the stone for a day and a night and it did not crack, and that the Harl who were not killed ran north. The stone is in a cart under hides, and the Ruun who take turns beside the cart sleep badly, and everyone in Tollan who slept within a bowshot of the Ruun camp dreamed, that one night, a crowd kneeling and a river. She said to you, going: "It would not break. Nothing that is mine refuses me. I will find what it is for." Ket is dead with the other five.
- The Greyholt wall. No bowl has been taken from it since the fifth month and none left on it. Greyholt leaves one anyway.
- The Rook half and the Tollan half of Scarrow cut stone from one face this year for the first time in fourteen years, because the flood took the quarry road and both halves needed the one that was left.
- Four dim nights.

## Year 27

- The one-color beasts crossed the moor this summer; Greyholt's burners on Hagg Ridge saw them from the ridge, a grey river of them far down on the moor going west and north, with no Harl anywhere in sight to tell it. The burners say the Harl have gone north for good and good riddance, and keep a man on the ridge at night anyway.
- The Skelder came to the spring market, which they have not done for six years, and before they traded they stood in a ring at the market's edge and sang their camps' names and their own, each one, which they say their god has them do every night now in the way the singers from the south did, and the traders of other peoples at the market hummed it afterward without meaning to. Asked why, the Skelder say so that the Mother can find them if they are lost.
- Caddo the herald has forty Tollan children who sing the Olm's names with him at dusk, and Hob has stopped minding, and Orla has started writing the order down in knots on a cord of its own so that it is not only in a Ruun mouth.
- The Lotfolk counters still cast their stones every morning at Tollan and have begun, the valley notices, to count things: fence posts, geese, the tags in Orla's box, which they asked to see and were shown and wrote down to the one.
- Five dim nights. From the hides the blue hair fell on the same bearing every time, and Orla's cords say the dim nights have been four or five a year for seventeen years whether you were in the valley or not.

## Year 28

- The salt caravans came small and late and said the Tallgrass is bare: locusts in clouds from the east ate the plateau to the root in a month, and the Ruun have driven their cattle down into the Greel lowland and are quarreling with the Greelmen over the water meadows, and the Gathering fire was lit this autumn in the lowland, not at the Hollow, for the first time.
- Harl came off the moor in the fourth month, more than the valley has seen in one raid, out of the north where they had gone, and took Greyholt's goats to the last head and burned two steadings above Ferrow Ford and killed four, and sang as they went a thing the Greyholt folk recognized as the kneeling song with Harl words. They left no bowl.
- Isca sent, by Caddo, word that the Harl are hers to burn when she has time, and asked, by the same mouth, whether the Olm had yet found what the stone is for.
- The Olm at the weir stood at the old mark all summer, as it has every summer Orla has knotted it; the Greelmen say the Olm mouth jetty is a hand deeper than when they were boys.
- A Dun Mere twin married the other twin's intended, and Dun Mere has stopped arguing about which is the elder.
- Four dim nights.

## Year 29

- Orla brought the cords to the Stone Row and read them to you, ten years of them: the valley's households, which were short in the storm year and the flood year and the raid year and level between, and are fewer than at the count when Hemma died; dim nights, four or five, never three, never six; stock deaths, high in the raid year and otherwise as the winters were; the Olm at the weir, level; the Olm mouth under the Greel, deeper. She says the cords say the valley loses people when the world hits it and does not get them back between blows, and that is all they say, and that Hemma would have said the same.
- Aske's son went up to the hill town alone in the sixth month, as the young Greyholt men do now for the cold water and the quiet, and brought a Lissom girl with him, and Greyholt says the town will have people in it again at this rate and does not know whether it is joking.
- The Lotfolk counters at Tollan cast their stones in the third month and the lot said that one of them should go home to Ennow with what has been counted so far, and he went, and two remain, and still wait for the Olm's answer. The one who married at Lissom stayed.
- Caddo was asked by the Lissom elders to sing the nine pools' names at the plum pressing, and did, and Lissom has decided this is now the custom at the pressing and was always the custom.
- Five dim nights.

## Year 30. The 341st year after the Driving

- At the autumn market Cass, grey now, said the counter at the lake writes that the shore people have begun walking into the water in their sleep, one or two a month since the spring, old and young, and are not seen again, and the ones left do not seem to grieve, and the forty families' children, who are grown and point, walk in too, and their parents, who still speak, have begun to cast the stones each morning about whether to leave, and the stones keep saying stay. Cass says the lake is deep and they fall in because they sleep too near it.
- A Greyholt elder complained at the count that the young of Greyholt no longer say "thee" to an elder, which Greyholt alone in the valley has always done, and that he had not noticed when it stopped, and that it is the fault of all the marrying down into Lissom. The young of Greyholt did not know what he was talking about.
- The Skelder hunter at Weir has been there six years. The pup is a grown hound, grey-white, silent, and follows him everywhere, and sleeps across the Weir man's door. He asked nothing this year either.
- Scarrow kept its silent day for Aldo Rook, who died on the hill in his turf hut wearing both torcs, and his son has taken the hut and one torc and sent the other to Tollan without a word, and Tollan has hung it in the count house beside the ell and does not know why it did.
- Isca's herald Caddo sang the Olm's names and then the Long Song entire at the longest night, which now takes two nights, and the lantern walk waited for him to finish and went out at dawn carrying no light.
- Four dim nights. On the last, in the twelfth month, the whole of Tollan sat up as it always has, and a child asked Orla why, and Orla said that she did not know, and that no one did, and that they would keep doing it.
