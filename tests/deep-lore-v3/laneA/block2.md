# Block 2. Years 11 to 20

## Year 11. The 322nd year after the Driving

- Harl raiders came off the moor in the fourth month, forty of them, and took sixty goats from Greyholt's upper folds and fired a charcoal stack. A Greyholt man took a spear in the thigh and lived. A month later there was a bowl of whey on the north wall again, as if nothing had happened, and Greyholt took it in.
- Aldo Rook came to Tollan alone when you sent for him, in the sixth month, and laid the torc on the eighth stone's step because you told him to, and did not like doing it. It is twisted gold as thick as a child's wrist, with a plain knob at each end, worn smooth where a neck would rub it. There is no mark on it anywhere. His story, in his own words: his line comes from a daughter of the hall named Ysolt, who was sent down to the quarry to hide on the night of the torches with the torc under her cloak, and married the quarryman who hid her, and the family has said nothing for three hundred years because the valley would have killed them for it. He recited thirteen generations from Ysolt to himself without stopping. Orla, whom Hemma is training to the cords, brought the cords and laid them beside his recital: the cords go back seventy-two years and Scarrow's was longer in Hemma's girlhood than it is now, and that is all the cords say. You set the fired clay tag from Ossary against the eighth stone while they were both in front of you. One of the three signs scratched on the tag is cut on the stone too, the fourth sign along. The other two are not. Aldo went home with his torc and told Scarrow the god had heard him. Tollan heard that too.
- Col has a herder in a hide at every fold on the Backs on every dim night now, and a box at Tollan for grass knots. The box is empty. Col says, when pressed, that no knot has been found and no lamb has gone since the hard winter two years ago, and he had not thought to say so because a thing that stops is not news. The herders in the hides saw the Lamp fade four times this year, in the first, second, eleventh and twelfth months, and every time the blue hair came down out of it far off to the north-west and was gone when the Lamp was bright again. They do not care for it either.
- Greyholt came down to the Stone Row in the seventh month, every soul who could walk, and stood on the meadow and said Ebb's name to him, one after another and then all together, with Nan in front and you above them on the stone. You willed it. Nothing left you and nothing arrived. Ebb listened politely and said it was a good name and he would keep it, and thanked them. By the count he answers to Ebb and calls Nan "the woman who weeps" when asked who she is, kindly. Greyholt says you gave him his name back and that the rest will come. Nan has stopped coming to the Stone Row. At the salt market you put the question to the caravan master, Dast, who has crossed the flats forty times. He says the last well is eleven days east of the Reed Crossing and that the caravans go no further, because past it the ground is one white thing to the edge of seeing, and the salt comes into a man through his feet and climbs, and when it reaches his head he is as Ebb is. His father's brother went to the dead city's steps, which stand out of the salt a further ten days on, and came back so. There are no shells out there, he says, no fish bones, no line of wrack, because the sea took everything with it when it went away angry. He will guide a god to the steps for a price he named, and will not go down them.
- In the sixth month Orla, Hob the elder of Tollan, and old Garran went south down the Greel road with two Greelmen boats and a letter of your words to carry to the Fire Hollow. Garran said he was going because someone had to go who could not be sung at.
- In the late summer thirty families came up the valley road from the south with carts and goats and children, Lotfolk, casting their stones at every fork though there was only one road. They said they were going to fish a lake in the north-west that their god had drawn for them, and asked the way over the hills to the moor road, and Greyholt showed them. They were gone over Hagg Ridge in four days. Greyholt says they will be eaten by the Harl.
- After the count you went north-west, with Col to carry and Ket to speak, in the eleventh month, with the snow already on the Caddon Hills. Two days up to Hagg Ridge by the burners' tracks. The Harl fold on the ridge was empty for the winter, six hundred sheep's worth of trodden ground inside a dry wall, and its gate is two uprights and a cross-stone with a square notch cut in its upper edge. Ket called it Gorm Longhand's fold and said the Harl call its gate the gate that points at nothing, and laughed, and you slept inside the wall out of the wind. Then down onto the moor, which in winter is eight days of heather and bog with the Harl barrows in their long rows on the rises. Ket named the clans whose dead are in each. Of one row he said that the walked men are in it: lowlanders who came up out of the valley three hundred years ago plain as sheep after the valley folk beat them so hard they forgot who they were, and whom the Harl took in, and whose blood is in half the clans, and that the valley folk have never been forgiven for it. Harl watched you from the rises the whole way and did not come down. On the eighth day Cold Hand, which is the boundary between Brude's clan and the clans north of it: a long grey stone lying flat in the heather with a square notch cut in one edge and two shorter stones half sunk beside it. The Harl say a giant dropped it. A half-day on, Fallow Dip, a hollow in the moor with a cairn in it the height of a man. The pull you have felt for ten years, that does not move and does not answer, grew plainer with every day of walking, and at the cairn it is under your hand. It does not answer there either. Ket spoke to the hollow before you went down into it. That night, with a hand laid through a gap in the cairn on a stone that is blue-grey and cold and heavier than it should be, you dreamed what the Harl dream: a crowd kneeling, faces to the ground, more people than the valley holds, and a river, wide and slow with low banks and a road along it. You know the river. You have never seen it and you know it the way you know where your wound is.

## Year 12

- Brude came to Fallow Dip on the fourth day with twenty men, because the watchers on the rises had sent for him. He is grey now and walks with a stick. Through Ket he said that the stone is a piece of the Low One, which the first men hung up as a lamp against the wolves, and which broke when the gods came through it; that a god may sleep at the cairn as any traveler may, and may lay a hand on the stone, and may not move one stone of the cairn; and that the dream is the Low One dreaming of the men who hung it. You waited for a dim night and it came in the second month. The Harl sat up for it as the valley does, and theirs is the same watch. The Lamp faded, and the blue hair came down out of it, and it did not come down on the cairn. It came down far off beyond the rim of the moor, west and a little north, fifteen days further on by Ket's reckoning, over the place the Harl call Hush Water, where the pointing folk are. The Harl do not go there. Ket says the Skelder go round it now, the long way, since their god came. The stone under your hand did nothing on the dim night that it does not do on any night. You came back by the thaw with Col. Ket stayed with his clan, which he had not seen in nine years, and sent word by the Greyholt wall that he would be at the cairn when you came again. You were lighter when you reached the Stone Row than when you left it, as you were in the spring of the fourth year.
- The envoys came home in the fourth month, thin, on Ruun horses they had been given on the road home and told not to refuse. They reached the Fire Hollow at the Gathering: about forty bands, and more people than the valley has, twice over, Garran says, who counted fires. The god Isca received them before the whole Gathering as a woman a head taller than any man there, with an iron band on her head and grey cords of hair to her feet, and heard Hob read your words. When he reached the part where the god of the Olm sends three of its people in its place, she became a fire the height of a hall, and the fire spoke. Orla has the words by heart: "Tell the fire of the Olm that Isca forgets nothing, and so she will remember that it was asked to bring itself and sent instead what it could spare. The Gathering is a hearth for fires. It is not a hearth for the ash a fire sends ahead to see whether the ground is warm. The Hollow is lit every autumn. There is a place at it for the Olm that will be given to no one else and will stand empty until the Olm fills it or the Song outlasts the asking. Carry that back whole. If you drop a word of it on the road, I will know which." Then she was a woman again, and had them fed. That night a young man the Ruun call the Voice sang from dusk to the small hours, every band, camp, spring and dead Ruun in order, and the Ruun say if he missed one the whole Hollow would know it. Nothing happened to him that Garran could see except that he was hoarse. A Ruun herdsman told Hob the Song keeps the dead where the Ember can hear them. Hob named Tollan and the villages when he introduced them, and asked afterward whether the names were now in the Song, and was told, with great courtesy, that a valley's names are given by its god or not at all. Garran's report, in full, was: "She burns people who will not sing for her. They told us so themselves as if it were a fine thing. Go if you like."
- The spring flood was the heaviest since the third year and took a long bite out of the Ossary bank. When the river was down you had the headman cut back the bank above the slump in the eighth month, a day's work for twenty men with every object kept and its depth marked on a tally. It is graves. They lie in rows, heads upstream, feet to the river, at two depths, the deeper row under the shallower with a hand of clay between. No floor, no wall, no ash. In the deeper row every third body has a fired clay tag on a rotted cord at the neck, scratched with two or three signs; in the shallow row, almost none. Beads of blue glass. A second bronze needle with two eyes, by the hand of a woman in the deep row. Pots with nothing in them. The headman says these are the lords' people from the Driving, killed on the hill and tipped into the river, and that is why Ossary reburies them with the Driving's words. Eleven tags are in a box at Tollan now, with the one from the eighth year.
- At Scarrow in the fifth month Rab Rook, Aldo's nephew, killed a quarryman called Tam with a maul in a fight over whether the Rooks' side of the village would pay the Tollan tithe, and Scarrow did not hold its silent day because half the village would not stand with the other half. In the seventh month the Rooks and their half of Scarrow went up Lord's Hill with picks and broke the custom. Under the brambles in the hall's footprint they found a flagged floor and under it steps, and under the steps a chamber. They brought up eleven gold torcs, every one the twin of Aldo's, a chest of bronze coins stamped with a wave, a great dish, and a board of oak the length of an arm cut with rows of signs, scraped back to raw wood in places and cut again shallower over the scrape, and scraped again. The older dead lie on ledges in the chamber with signs cut in the stone above each, and the Rooks left them. Aldo wears two torcs now and says the board was the roll of his house, and that the valley folk scraped his family's names off it after the Driving to make the lords nothing, and that the silver is the hill's and the hill is his. Tollan says the hill is cursed and so is anyone who wears what came out of it. The quarry is working at half strength because the two halves will not share a face.
- Hemma died in the ninth month, eighty-six, with the cords in her lap, having said that morning that this year's were middling. Orla keeps them now. The whole valley came to the burying, which is more than came to the count.
- The Lotfolk traders at the autumn market said Oss died on the road south last winter and was counted. A trader called Cass has his route. The cairn of white pebbles was at the boundary stone after the market as it always is.
- Five dim nights: the first, second, third, eleventh and twelfth months. The blue hair every time, north-west. Wat of Ferrow Ford swam the Olm at the great bend in flood for a bet and won a goat.

## Year 13

- The Skelder came to the spring market with pelts and dogs and complained of the walk, which they say is a week longer than it was because their god has moved the winter camps to the Fell side. Asked about her, they say the same as before: the Mother of Dogs feeds anyone, names everyone every night, has forbidden the one-color beasts and the south-west past the Fell, and will not say why. They would not sell a dog.
- A Ferrow Ford herder, Dunn, keeping Col's hide at the Crown Fold on a dim night in the second month, saw no light where no light should be but in the morning followed a lynx track into a fold of the Backs no herder uses and found a hut no herder built, turf over bent hazel, with the door fallen in. On a pallet inside, the bones of an old woman, small, with a fired clay tag on a cord at her neck scratched with four signs, and from the roof poles, bundles of cords tied in knots no one at Ferrow Ford can tie or untie, dozens of them, and a water jar, and a pot, and lamb bones in a heap outside the door going back years. Col says she was a Harl witch who lived on their lambs and cursed the folds with the grass knots, and the hard winter of the ninth year took her, and that is why the lambs stopped. The tag is in the box at Tollan with the others, and the cords are in a second box, because no one will have them in a house. One of the four signs on her tag is on the eighth stone.
- In the sixth month you took Pim up into the Caddon Hills with two Greyholt hunters, Aske and his son, who know the deer paths. Pim is eighteen and has a child on her back. She found the gully she went up at nine without hesitating and lost the way a half-day above it, where the gully opens onto scree, because at nine she had followed a goat and the goat knew. Twelve days you searched the grey hills, which are bigger inside than they look from Greyholt, and on the ninth day came out above a round of bare grass a half-day across with not one seed head in it and no bird over it, which the hunters would not walk on and which Aske says is salt from an old sea. From the far edge of the round a goat path climbs north, and at the top of it, four days above Greyholt as the goat walks, is the town. It is cut into the grey rock in three tiers around a yard, more house-cuts than Greyholt and Lissom have hearths together, roofs long gone, doorways square and black. In the yard, eleven round openings in the rock with a lip of worked stone to each. Nine are open, and the water in them is a man's height down, and it is cold enough to hurt the teeth in the sixth month, and sweet. One is fallen in. One is covered with a flat capstone carved with a pattern, and Aske's son said at once that it was the headman's snake, meaning the hearth-stone in Greyholt, and it is the same pattern. The three of them put their backs to the capstone and it did not shift; it would want four men and a lever. In the upper yard there is a flat grave stone with a cut border and nothing cut inside the border. There is nothing of gold anywhere, and nothing of anyone. You lifted the elders' ban on the hills at the Stone Row at the count, and the Greyholt elder who had called Pim a liar said the hills were still unlucky whatever the god said, and Pim said nothing at all, which Greyholt found worse.
- In the autumn fog you had two Fenners pole you into the Brann Cut from the abandoned steadings, and sat in the reeds at the bend where they stand thickest, and in the second hour the note came: one low note, not repeated for a long time, then again. You put a weighted line down where it seemed loudest. Four spans of water and a span of soft mud and then something hard and flat that rang when the lead struck it, the same note, small. The lead came up green. The Fenners would not touch it until you said to, and then dragged the bend with an eel hook on a pole, and the third pass brought up a bronze plate a span across, green and black, with rows of marks in bands across it and a shorter line of marks under them in a hand no one in the valley has seen. It is at Tollan. The reeds have not rung since. The Fenners say the drowned post's dead have been given back their bell and are quiet, and are fishing the cut again, and the eels there are as fat as they were.
- Aldo Rook sits on Lord's Hill in a turf hut inside the old wall and calls himself nothing yet, and his half of Scarrow sends him a tenth of its quarry money and not Tollan. At the count Scarrow's stone had two men who wanted to stand on it and neither did. The Driving was told as always, with the children running away shrieking, and Aldo's half of Scarrow was not there to hear it.
- Four dim nights. A Lissom couple walked the valley tied at the wrist and arrived in six days, which is the fastest anyone remembers, because he did not stop to look at anything.

## Year 14

- In the fifth month a band of thirty Ruun riders came up from the Reed Crossing with the young man the envoys had seen sing at the Hollow, whom they call Haddo and the Voice. They were courteous and took nothing and stayed one night at the Stone Row and sang, and he sang alone for an hour of it, and the valley came out to listen whether it meant to or not. He said Isca has sent him west to find the thing that pulls at her from the edge of the Ember's reach and will not answer, and asked the way to the moor. He said also, when a Tollan man asked after the Ruun's health, that the bands who graze to the far eastern edge of the Tallgrass come home now not knowing their own camps or their own names, and can still sing the Long Song whole, every name, and that Isca says this is her strength in them and he says so too. They went up the valley in the morning and over Hagg Ridge.
- The one-color beasts crossed the moor this summer, the Harl traders say at the Greyholt wall, hundreds of them, all one grey, no smell. Brude's clan sleeps at the cairn more than it did since a god slept there, they say, and more of them dream the kneeling.
- Col came to the Stone Row and asked whether the herders might come out of the hides, because in five years no lamb has gone and the men are tired of cold nights for nothing. You kept the watch. They saw the Lamp fade four times and the blue hair every time.
- Berit of Lissom died two winters ago at ninety-four. Her grandson, who has the grey plum trees now, says they set fruit but it is not worth the picking, small and tasting of nothing, and that he will give them two more years and then the axe, and that his grandmother's grafting ruined them.
- One of the Dun Mere twins made his first ford a year before his brother, who was ill, and the village has taken this as settling which is the elder, and the mother has not.
- Four dim nights.

## Year 15

- Harl traders at the Greyholt wall say the riders from the east came up onto the moor last autumn, thirty of them with a boy who sings, and Brude let them sleep at the cairn, and the boy dreamed the kneeling and the river and woke singing it, and sang it to the Harl, and the Harl who heard it have not stopped humming it. The riders are wintering with Brude's clan and show no sign of going home, and the boy sings it every night. Brude says the stone gave the boy its dream to carry east because the Low One wants the east to know how it was hung.
- An ordinary flood. Ossary's bank gave as usual and the headman had every bone kept until you had seen them, as you ordered, and there were two tags among them, which went into the box, and then the bones were buried on the hill with the words.
- Garran died in the fourth month sitting on the quarry wall, telling his nephew what he was doing wrong. Scarrow kept its silent day, both halves, without agreeing to.
- Greelmen tying up at Weir say the Greel is wider at the Reed Crossing than their fathers knew it by a boat's length, and that the old stone jetty at the Olm mouth is two spans under now, and that it is the northern rain.
- Five dim nights, four of them between the count and the thaw.

## Year 16

- At the autumn market the Lotfolk trader Cass said the lake families have gone quiet, which is what their god's counters have written of the thirty who went north five years ago, and that ten more families have been sent to them by lot, and he does not know what quiet means in the Tally House's hand and did not ask.
- A murrain took the cattle on the far bank of the Greel this summer, the Greelmen say, from the Reed Crossing to the dry south, whole herds dead in the grass. No cattle were taken across the Greel from the valley this year and none came in, and the valley's cattle are well. Weir says you spared them.
- A Tollan drover was beaten on the Scarrow road by Rook men for calling Aldo a quarryman's by-blow. Tollan's elders asked you to put the Rooks off the hill. Scarrow's Rook half asked you to make Aldo lord of it. You have done neither and both halves say so.
- Pim's second child was named at the flood with a knot tied in its wrist-string, at Greyholt, where Pim is spoken to again, carefully.
- Greyholt grazes its goats up to the edge of the bare round now, which the burners call the Scald after what Aske told them, and two Greyholt men went up to the town in the hill in the summer to drink from the cold wells for the pleasure of it and came back saying it was the best water in the world and the loneliest place.
- Four dim nights.

## Year 17

- Drought. No rain from the fourth month to the ninth. The Olm could be walked across at Tollan without wetting the knees, as in the fourth year. Greyholt drove its goats up the hill path to the town's cold wells in the sixth month, and watered them there all summer, and the wells did not drop a hand, and the goats came down fat in the autumn. The elders of the lower villages came to the Stone Row and asked you, formally, for rain, as they did before.
- The Skelder came to the spring market, fewer than before, and said, asked about their god, that she sent three hunters north past the end of the land three years ago to look for something, and they came back last autumn alive and able to speak and not able to say their bands' names or their own, and their dogs knew them, and the Mother feeds them first at her own fire every night before anyone, and the Skelder are afraid of them. The traders think the Mother cursed them for failing her.
- The Lissom pools were low all summer and the Kettle, the first of the nine, was a dry stone basin for a month, which no one living has seen.
- The Fenners held a wedding at the Brann Cut steadings, which are lived in again, and sent eels to Tollan for the god's table, which they have not done before.
- The salt caravans were late and small, forty camels become twenty-five, and Dast says the dry country south of the flats is drier.
- Five dim nights.

## Year 18

- In the fourth month Haddo's riders came back down over Hagg Ridge and through the valley on their way home, and stayed a night at the Stone Row, and he sang a song he did not have when he went up. It is a song of a crowd kneeling, faces down, more than a valley holds, and of a wide slow river with a road along it, and he sings it as if he had stood on the riverbank. It is your dream, in a Ruun mouth, with a tune. He says the stone at Fallow Dip gave it to him and that it is the Ember's dream of the first men who hung it, and that he is taking it to Isca as the finest thing the west has. Tollan liked the tune and children were singing it in the lanes for a month after the riders had gone.
- Weir's otters have moved from the mill race to the ferry landing, and the ferryman's Fenner wife feeds them now, and the miller says they were his.
- A Rook girl and a Tollan boy walked the valley tied at the wrist and arrived at Ossary, and are married, and neither Scarrow nor Tollan will have them, so they live at Weir.
- Harl traders at the Greyholt wall say Brude keeps four men at the cairn now in all weathers.
- Four dim nights.

## Year 19

- In the seventh month the ground shook, once, like a cart going over a bridge, and dogs barked from Greyholt to Ossary. Nothing fell but a dry wall at Lissom and the new millstone at Weir cracked clean across at rest. At Ossary a stretch of the bank slumped in the dry season, which it does not do, and bones came out in summer, and the headman kept them and three tags for you and reburied the rest with the words, and said the river was angry at being dug.
- Cass's cairn of white pebbles was at the boundary stone after the market. A Tollan child counted it for a dare, as the Weir boy did fourteen years ago, and got a number smaller than this year's households by nine, and the valley argued for a month about whether the child or Cass had miscounted.
- Wat of Ferrow Ford, who cannot be kept from the river, has been taken on by the ferryman at Weir, and Ferrow Ford says the river has finally got him.
- At the count the Driving was told. Orla says the cords are shorter than Hemma's were at her last count, and she said it to you directly, because Hemma had told her to.
- Four dim nights.

## Year 20. The 331st year after the Driving

- Harl traders at the Greyholt wall say Brude's clan has set keepers at the cairn at Fallow Dip, six men who sleep by the stone every night and speak the dream aloud at dawn, every word, the crowd and the river, and that no raid leaves the moor now until the keepers have slept and said it is a good night for it. Ket is one of the six. The traders say the Low One has chosen the Harl to keep its piece because a god came to it and could not take it.
- Lissom cut the grey plum trees in the third month, all forty, and burned them, and grafted blood plum onto the stumps. The grandson says the trees were ruined by old grafting and that the yellow and the blood are as good as they ever were. The oldest woman in Lissom says the yellow is not what it was either, and is told she sounds like Berit.
- The one-color beasts crossed the moor again this summer, the Harl say, more than in any year they remember, and went west and north toward Hush Water and turned back a day short of it, as they always do.
- The Lotfolk traders at the autumn market have a story they tell as a wonder. Four years ago their god sent two hundred people west by lot into the bare country with food for a hundred days and each one's name cut on a stone to carry. Last year eleven came back. They had forgotten everything, their city, their god, their own names, and the eleven stones in their hands had the names on them still, sharp as the day they were cut. The god has begun cutting every living Lotfolk name into a ring of stone around its city, and the whole city says the names aloud at night. Cass says the god does this so that the lot will always have the right number of names to choose from.
- At the count Orla's cords were short again. The Driving was told. The children acted the lords and ran away shrieking, and a Scarrow child, from the Rook half, would not run, and stood in the firelight with his chin up until his mother carried him off.
- The swifts came back to Scarrow's cuts in the fourth month as always, to both halves of the quarry.
- Five dim nights: the first, second, third, eleventh and twelfth months. The blue hair every time, north-west. The lamp-watch at every hearth.
