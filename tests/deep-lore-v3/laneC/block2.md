# Block 2. Years 11 to 20. Lane C

## Year 11

- In the fourth month you went up onto the moor with Ket, who had not seen his own country in eight years and talked the whole way. Two days up by Hagg Ridge, where his clan was bringing six hundred sheep to the summer fold. The fold's gate is two uprights and a cross-stone with a notch in it, older than the fold; Ket said his grandfather called it the old gate that points at nothing, and that no Longhand has ever moved it because the sheep know it. Then four days of heather and bog and the Harl barrows in their rows, each clan's dead in a line with the newest end still open. Harl men walked a hill's width off on either side of you the whole way and did not come closer and did not go away. At Cold Hand, a fallen stone as long as three men that marks the boundary between two clans, which the Harl say a giant dropped. A half-day on, Fallow Dip, a hollow with a cairn in it as high as a man.
- You had the cairn opened. The Harl who had followed you shouted from the rim and did not come down. Under the stones, a stone the size of a sheep's body, blue-grey, heavier than it should be, and cold now. You slept beside it. You dreamed a crowd kneeling, more people than live in the valley, with their faces down, and a river beyond them, broad and brown with low banks, which you knew the way you know a thing from before the meadow, and could not place. Ket slept by it too and dreamed the crowd and not the river. In the morning the Harl had come down and were re-piling the cairn around the stone without a word, and Ket stayed with them. No dim night fell while you were there. You came home by the same road, lighter in the pack and nothing else.
- At Weir you asked after the grey wolf-dog from the north. It died in the winter after the spring market it was sold at, four years ago, having never eaten. The Weir man's children buried it under the willow at the mill race and the Weir man still says he was cheated.
- In late summer thirty families came up the river road from the south-west with carts, Lotfolk, casting stones from a bag at every fork. They said they were going to fish a lake in the north-west that their god drew for them, and went up through Greyholt and over the hills by the moor road, and the valley has not heard of them since.
- Harl raiders came off the moor in the ninth month and took twenty goats and a cow from Greyholt, and no bowl has been left on Greyholt's north wall since, by either side.
- Six men went east with the salt caravan after the market, by your word, volunteers: Dace of Tollan, the brothers Hob and Sim of Weir, Alun of Ferrow Ford, Jory of Lissom, and Ebb of Greyholt, who went where he was put. Each carried a cord knotted by Hemma with his name in her knots, and each had your order to sing his own name and the others' every night. The caravaners said they would take them as far as the last well and no further, and said it was a waste of good men.
- At the count you stood on the eighth stone. The elders went quiet and stayed so. The line of marks cut along the stone's top was under your feet, a hand long, pictures you do not read. Hemma, eighty-five, said Mother Tollan stood on it last. The Ossary elder got down off his own stone and would not stand on it again that count. Aldo Rook asked you from Scarrow's stone, in front of everyone, for the hill. The hill was left where it lay. You also cut Scarrow's quarry tithe to Tollan in exchange for all of Scarrow at every festival on the meadow, and Scarrow cheered and Tollan's elders did not. Dim nights in the second month and the eleventh.

## Year 12

- A heavy flood, five days. The Ossary bank gave in a great slab and the villagers buried bone for a week. Ferrow Ford lost its footbridge again.
- In the fourth month at Scarrow, in the quarry cuts, Dagg Rook, Aldo's nephew, struck a quarryman called Tobin with a wedge over who gives the orders in the lower cut, and Tobin died the next day. Scarrow kept its silent day and did not agree on who had killed whom. Garran's nephew, who stands on Scarrow's stone, would not sit with the Rooks after it. In the sixth month the Rooks went up Lord's Hill with mattocks and twenty men and dug the hall floor, which no one in the valley has done in three hundred and twenty years. They found stone steps and a chamber. In it: eleven gold torcs like Aldo's, a chest of bronze coin of a foreign stamp, a great silver dish, the bones of the old lords laid in order with names cut in the stone over each, and a wooden tally-board with the names scraped off and cut again and scraped again, the last cuts shaky, so that only a few letters can be made out. The Rooks say Mother Tollan's people scraped it to wipe their line out of the valley's memory, and they carried the silver down to Scarrow in daylight. Tollan's elders have asked you what you intend. Scarrow's half that is not Rook has asked you the same.
- In the seventh month you went east and south to the Fire Hollow with two hundred of Tollan and Aldo Rook wearing two torcs. Five weeks: the Greel at the Reed Crossing on Greelmen's boats, a week of wet lowland where an old man of Tollan died of a fever, then up onto the plateau, grass to the shoulder and wind. A Tollan woman, Mab, drowned at the Reed Crossing on the way out when a boat tipped. On the road you were no lighter than at home, as far as you could tell.
- The Gathering. Forty bands, their cattle beyond counting, a hollow a mile across with a fire at the bottom of it the height of a hall. By day Isca is a woman a head taller than any man there, an iron band on her head, grey cords of hair knotted to her feet. At dusk she walks into the fire and the fire speaks. The Ruun say she is a woman by day and a fire by night. The Long Song took a night and most of a second: every band, camp, spring, and dead Ruun, in order, forty of a band called Orrin's among the dead, and then names in a tongue no one of yours knew, among which the Tollan folk heard names the forty strangers sang at the Stone Row five years ago, and a river named among them. The singer who holds the whole Song is a youth of eighteen called Haddo, whom she calls her Voice and keeps at her left hand. On the second night you stood before the Ruun and your two hundred sang the valley: every village, every pool of the Lissom, the Stone Row, your name, their own names. The fire sang it all back without a fault and said: "Let it be known at every fire of the Ruun that the Ember's hearth and the Lamp's hearth are one hearth from this night, and that what the Olm has given us is in the Song and nothing leaves the Song while I stand. I do not bargain. I give. And I keep nothing from a friend, and a friend keeps nothing from me, for that is the one thing I will not forgive." You were heavier while they sang the valley back, as after a harvest festival, and it did not all go afterward. She asked whether you have felt the fourth thing that pulls from the west and does not answer, and said, "We do not love what does not answer. I will send my Voice to find it in time." She asked Aldo Rook his name and sang it in. She said, when a band was named that had come back from the plateau's eastern edge not knowing its own camp but singing every verse, that this was her strength shown, that the Song outlasts the singer. She gave you a hundred cattle and twenty horses and would take nothing.
- The journey back in the tenth and eleventh months, with the cattle, through the wet lowland in rain. You were in the valley again in the first month of the thirteenth year. The count had been held without you. Hemma died in the eighth month, eighty-six, and her grand-niece Hild keeps the cords now and says she does not know what Hemma's longest cords meant and will begin her own. Oss the Lotfolk trader did not come; another, Rell, came in his place with the same bag of stones, and the white cairn was at the boundary stone as always. Tollan's harvest was short with two hundred hands away, and Weir and Lissom say Tollan fed its travelers from the common store, which Tollan denies.
- Dim nights in the first month, the eleventh, and the twelfth.

## Year 13

- You took Pim, who is twenty and lives on the Backs with her shepherd, and the Greyholt headman and two elders, who came because you said so and said the whole way that the hills are unlucky, up the goat paths above Greyholt in the sixth month. Pim did not know the way. Eleven days of paths that ended in scree before she found the stream she remembered, and then it was four days as she had said. On the way, off to the south of the path, a round of bare grass a half-day across with no seed heads on it and no bird in it. Then Vell: a town cut into the grey rock around a hollow, roofless, goat-cropped, and eleven cisterns, chambers hollowed in the hill. Nine are open and the water in them is cold enough to hurt your teeth and sweet. One has fallen in. One is sealed with a capstone carved with a pattern the Greyholt headman knew at once and said so: it is the snake on his hearth. In the upper yard a flat grave stone with a cut border and nothing inside the border. No one lives there. No knots, no fire, no track but goats'. The Greyholt elders say it was the lords' town, where they fled after the Driving and died of the curse, and they do not want it spoken of in Greyholt, and it is.
- In the autumn fog, when the Fenners said the bell was ringing, you went into the Brann Cut on the lead boat with Oda, the ferryman's wife, poling, and six Fenner boats behind that would not pass the first bend until you had. One low note, irregular, from where the reeds stand thickest at the second bend. No posts, no walls, no timbers under the pole. Hooks dragged the mud at the bend for a morning and brought up a bronze plate a span across, green, with rows of marks on it and one short line in a script no one in the valley reads. Lying half out of the mud with its edge up, it hummed when the wind came through the reeds. The Fenners say it is the drowned post's bell that fell when the post went under, and have gone back to fishing the cut. At the autumn market a salt caravaner turned it over, read two words of it, "Aud" and "tide," said it was the sea's anger written down, and offered salt for it.
- Four of the six came back with the caravan in the tenth month: Dace, Hob, Sim, Jory. The caravaners found them at the last well on the far side of the flats, sitting, singing four names in turn. Each knows his own name and the other three. None knows Tollan or Weir or Lissom, or his wife, or his trade, or what the cord at his neck is for. Hob and Sim know they are brothers because they were sung as brothers. The cords came back with every knot in them. Alun died of thirst two days out from the last well, the caravaners say, and they buried him. Ebb walked on east past where the others turned and did not come back. The caravaners say it is the salt, and that the salt is stronger the further you go, and that these four should be glad they are alive. Nan of Greyholt did not come to the Stone Row this time.
- At Weir a Greelman boat family stayed the winter for the first time, frozen in above the weir, and the ferryman's children learned to pole.
- The red deer did not come down this winter, which was mild, and Greyholt says the winter is wrong when the deer stay up.
- A dim night in the twelfth month.

## Year 14

- The one-color beasts crossed the moor in the summer, Harl traders say, come back to the Greyholt wall since the spring: a herd of hundreds, all one grey, no smell. The Harl have built the cairn at Fallow Dip to twice its height and two men of Brude's clan sit by it in turns and sleep there, and say the dream is clearer than it was.
- In the ninth month thirty Ruun riders came up the valley from the Reed Crossing with Haddo at their head, now twenty, who sang at the Stone Row one night, the valley's names first and perfectly, and asked the way to the moor. He said the Ember has sent him to find the thing in the west that pulls and does not answer. He went up through Greyholt in the tenth month and the Harl let him through, which they let no one.
- Scarrow has two festivals now: the valley's on the meadow, where Scarrow comes in a body as it promised, and the Rooks' on Lord's Hill, where Aldo's family sits in the hall's footprint on the longest night with the silver and the torcs, and about a third of Scarrow goes up. Tollan calls it the lords' night. The quarry is short-handed, and the stone for Weir's new mill wall came late.
- The Ruun cattle have been put to the valley bulls and the calves are long-horned and red and the Backs' herders are pleased and the Dun Mere people say the milk is thin.
- Wat of Ferrow Ford, now grown, has built himself a hut on the river at the ford and ferries children across for the pleasure of it and will not take payment, which the Weir ferryman thinks is an insult.
- Dim nights in the first month, the second, and the twelfth.

## Year 15

- Harl traders at the Greyholt wall say the singer from the east slept a month by the stone at Fallow Dip in the spring and sang the dream back to the men who sit there, the crowd and the river, in a song, and the Harl liked it, and two of Brude's men have it by heart and sing it over the cairn. The Harl say the Low One shed the stone when the gods came through, and the song is the stone's.
- Rell the Lotfolk trader says the Coin is a coin because one day it falls, which is a proverb among his people, and that his god lives in a bowl and is made of stones, and that his god has a city now with a wall going up around it. He said this while the stones in his bag decided the price of salt.
- Lissom's grey plum trees carried no fruit worth picking for the second year, and Berit, ninety-seven, says cut them, and her grandson says wait another year.
- A Fenner boy was lost in the fen in the autumn fog and found three days later asleep on a mere island, well, and says he followed a light that walked ahead of him, and the Fenners say that is what the marsh lights do and always have.
- Dace, who came back from the east, has been taught the valley again by his wife and knows his children's names now because she says them to him every morning, and he says them back, and he does not remember them by evening.
- Dim nights in the eleventh month and the twelfth.

## Year 16

- Across the Greel the cattle are dying, the Greelmen say, a murrain in the wet lowland and up onto the plateau, Ruun cattle in heaps, and the caravans will not drive stock across the Reed Crossing. No beast of the valley has crossed the Greel this year and no beast of the valley is sick.
- Rell the Lotfolk trader, asked after the thirty families who went to fish the lake, said the lake families have gone quiet, and cast a stone to see whether to say more, and the stone said no.
- The Skelder came to the spring market, forty of them with dogs, and said the walk is longer every year because the Mother keeps moving the winter camps toward the Fell and away from the moor, and that one of their camps is called Ashen, a name the hunter Teg took from the strangers who sang across the Fell nine years ago because he liked the sound.
- The otters at Weir have bred and there are five under the mill race and the miller has given up and feeds them himself.
- The lantern walk on the longest night went from the Stone Row to the great bend in a snowstorm with the Lamp bright above it, and a Tollan child was born on the meadow on the way back and named for the walk.
- Dim nights in the first month and the twelfth.

## Year 17

- Drought. No rain from the fifth month to the tenth. The Olm walkable at Tollan. The Kettle, the first of the Lissom pools, went dry for the first time anyone remembers, and the stream ran in a thread through the orchards. Greyholt drove its goats and most of its sheep up the goat paths to Vell in the seventh month, the headman leading, and the cisterns were full and cold, and the beasts came down in the tenth month fat. Greyholt still says the hills are unlucky and has cut a path.
- The Skelder came late to the spring market and said that three hunters the Mother sent north three years ago have come back out of the bare country alive, able to speak and hunt, unable to say their bands' names or their own, and that the Mother keeps them at her own fire and feeds them before anyone, before herself, and will not say why she sent them. The traders think she sent them to find better hunting and they found the end of the world.
- Hild's cords at the count were the shortest she has kept, which is only five years, and she says the drought did it, and it did.
- Dun Mere's eel fair was held with the plain eel only, as it has been for seven years, and the Dun Mere people now say the spotted eel was always rare and the old people exaggerate.
- The Harl left a bowl of whey on Greyholt's wall in the eighth month, the first in six years, and Greyholt did not take it in for two days and then did.
- Dim nights in the first month, the second, and the eleventh.

## Year 18

- In the fifth month Haddo came back down through the valley with twenty-two of his thirty riders, from the moor, and sang at the Stone Row for a night. He sang the valley first, perfectly, and then a new song of his own, which the Ruun with him call the Kneeling Song: a crowd kneeling, their faces down, more than can be counted, and a river beyond them, broad and brown with low banks. He says the Ember's shed piece on the moor gave it to him in sleep and that it is the Ember's own oldest song, from before the Ruun, and that he is carrying it home to her. He went on to the Reed Crossing singing it.
- The Skelder, at the spring market, said the Mother has gone north herself with forty hunters and their dogs, past the end of the Fell, and that the hounds that stayed are fewer and quieter, and that she said she would be two years.
- The Rooks' night on Lord's Hill drew half of Scarrow this longest night, and Garran's nephew stood on Scarrow's stone at the count and said Scarrow cannot keep two festivals and a quarry, and Aldo Rook stood on no stone and said nothing and wore three torcs.
- A Lissom woman walked the valley tied at the wrist with a Greelman and arrived at Ossary married, and the Greelmen say she is the first of the valley to live on the water.
- The rooks in Scarrow's trees left in the ninth month and came back in the tenth, and the quarrymen say a long winter, and it was an ordinary one.
- Dim nights in the second month and the twelfth.

## Year 19

- In the third month the ground shook for the space of a breath, the whole valley felt it, the Lissom pools slopped over, and Weir's millstone cracked across. No one was hurt. The miller says the hill moved under the mill and wants Scarrow stone for a new one, and Scarrow is slow.
- Ruun riders trading horses at the autumn market said that Haddo's Kneeling Song is sung now at a dozen fires on the plateau, and that when he sang it at the Gathering last autumn the Ember went into the fire and stayed there all night and burned nothing and spoke to no one, and that she sang his name next morning in the Song in a voice they did not know.
- Rell the Lotfolk trader said nothing about the lake and cast no stone about it. He said his god's wall is a ring of cut stone around the city and that every name of his people is going onto it and that the whole city says the names at night.
- Berit of Lissom died at ninety-nine with the grey plum trees still standing, and her grandson says he will cut them next year.
- The Fenners' geese came in the greatest numbers since the fifth year and the Fenners sold goose at the count and a Fenner elder said the fen was a lake once and will be a lake again, and that this is why the geese come.
- Dim nights in the first month, the eleventh, and the twelfth.

## Year 20. The 331st year after the Driving

- Harl raiders came off the moor in the fourth month onto Ferrow Ford's folds and again in the ninth onto Greyholt, more of them than in any raid since the Driving is told, and before each raid the folds heard them on the hill above chanting one thing over and over, which Ket's old friends at Ferrow Ford made out as the dream: a crowd kneeling, a river. Harl traders at the Greyholt wall, in the months between, say four men of Brude's clan now live by the cairn at Fallow Dip and are called the stone-men, and that every Harl who raids sleeps there first and dreams, and that the stone-men say the Low One wants it so.
- Lissom cut the grey plum trees in the second month and burned them, and there are two plums at Lissom now, the yellow and the blood, and the children do not know there were three.
- The one-color beasts crossed the moor again, the Harl say, and this year they crossed closer to the valley than any year remembered, within sight of Hagg Ridge.
- Rell the Lotfolk trader, asked about the wall, said this: that his god sent two hundred of his people west into the bare country four years ago, each carrying a stone with his own name cut on it, and that eleven came back after three years and had forgotten everything, their city, their god, their wives, but the stones still had the names, so the god is cutting every living name in stone around the city. He says it is so the god can count them if they are lost. He said this in the plain way the Lotfolk say everything, and then cast a stone to see whether to sell you salt.
- The Skelder did not come to the spring market. Greelmen say they saw Skelder hunters on the Greel's west bank in the autumn who said the Mother was back from the north with twenty-eight of her forty, and the twenty-eight are like the three.
- At the count the Driving was told as always. Hild's cords were longer than the drought year and shorter than any of Hemma's that she has seen. The Ossary bank held and Ossary feasted. Aldo Rook stood on Scarrow's stone again, Garran's nephew having given it up, and said nothing about the hill because he has it.
- Dim nights in the first month and the twelfth.
