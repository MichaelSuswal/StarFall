# Block 2. Years 11 to 20

## Year 11. The 322nd year after the Driving

- You put the granary to the Stone Row in the first month and the elders agreed to it in four days, which is fast for them. The quarrel with Scarrow was settled by paying for the stone openly on the meadow: so many blocks for so many measures of barley and eel, set down before every village, with Garran's nephew standing on Scarrow's stone to take it. Aldo Rook stood up behind him and said that any stone cut from the hill under Lord's Hill was the lord's to tithe before it was Scarrow's to sell, and half of Scarrow's men nodded and half looked at their feet. The first blocks came down the Scarrow track in the fourth month on sledges. The Tollan granary stands on a raised footing of eleven courses beside the count house; by the count it had walls and no roof.
- Harl came off the moor in the fourth month, forty of them, and took Greyholt's goats from the hill above the village and burned two charcoal clamps. No one was killed. Six Greyholt men were away in the hills with your party at the time and Greyholt has said so, more than once.
- You lifted the ban on the hills for Pim and her man, Col, and six Greyholt men, and went up with them. Pim could not find the goat path. She was nine and followed a goat, and the hill above Greyholt is all goat paths. The party searched for five weeks, sleeping out, and came down twice for food. In the eighth month they came over a saddle and found a half-day of bare grass with no seed heads on it and no bird in it, which Col calls an old burn and the Greyholt men call salt ground, and from the far edge of it saw the town. Vell is cut into the grey rock: doorways and rooms hollowed from the hill around a yard, and in the yard and the terraces below it eleven round openings with carved lids beside them, nine of them full of water that is cold enough to hurt the teeth and sweet, one fallen in, one still capped, and the cap carved with the same pattern as the headman's hearth-stone at Greyholt, which the Greyholt men knew at once and did not like. There is a flat grave in the upper yard with a cut border and nothing cut inside it. No bones in the town. No roofs, no timber, nothing that was not stone. They brought you a bucket from the first cistern and a block from a doorway. The water was cool by the time it reached Greyholt, the way any well water is. The block is grey and takes an edge; Garran's nephew says it is the same hill as Scarrow's and would save no one anything, being four days up a goat path. Pim says the hill is deep rock and holds the winter in it. The Greyholt men say the town was a town of the dead and the dead have been taken out of it, and will not sleep inside the walls.
- As for a channel from Vell to Greyholt: the Greyholt men walked the way with you and it climbs and falls four times between the town and the village. Water will not be made to do that. What can be done is drive goats up, and Greyholt has said it will, in a dry year, since it knows the way now.
- In the ninth month thirty families came up the valley road from the south with carts and children and a great many hens, Lotfolk by their speech and their bag of stones, and went through Tollan without stopping to trade. Asked where they were going, they said, "to fish a lake in the north-west that our god drew for us," and showed a drawing on hide of a round water with a shore marked. Greyholt sold them cheese and they went up over the Caddon Hills by the moor road, forty carts, in the last week before the count.
- Hemma, eighty-five, sat with you and with the Ossary tag in the count house for most of the summer. She does not like the idea. She says a cord can be felt in the dark and a mark cannot. She did it anyway. The pattern is hers: a tag for each household, fired at Scarrow, with a mark for the village and a notch for each mouth, and a hole to hang it on the village's cord so she can still feel it. Three children were chosen, one from Tollan, one from Weir, one from Lissom, and sat with her through the count. This year's count was kept both ways, cords and tags, and they agreed except at Scarrow, where the Rooks would not be counted by Tollan children and Hemma counted them herself from memory.
- Dun Mere netted the whole tarn in the seventh month for the great pike and took a pike as long as a man's leg, and ate it at a feast, and no spotted eel has been taken since, and the fishers say there must have been two.
- The Weir man who bought the grey-white dog in the seventh year, asked after it, said it never ate a mouthful and died the winter after he bought it, lying with its head toward the north, and he buried it under the mill wall and still wants his salt back.
- Dim nights: four, between the count and the thaw. The lamp-watch at every hearth.

## Year 12

- Hemma died in the second month, in her sleep, with the Tollan cord in her hand. The children have the cords and the tags. There are one thousand and ninety-four tags.
- A heavy flood, five days, the river brown to the hill foot at the bend. At Ferrow Ford the first pier's footing had been set in the low water of the autumn, with the timber frame around it, and the flood took the frame and a Greyholt man working on it, Dall, who was hauling stone there for the barley. Wat went in after him and brought him out at the willows below the ford, dead. The footing itself held. Ferrow Ford buried Dall above the ford and Wat has not stopped working.
- The flood took the lower course of the Weir granary, which was still unroofed, and a tenth of the barley in it moulded. It is being rebuilt three courses higher. The Tollan granary, roofed in the first month, was dry.
- At Ossary the bank gave as always and more bone than usual came out. The headman has not sent you anything this year. He has kept it in baskets in the eel shed, as you asked, against the work you have promised there.
- In the seventh month at the Scarrow quarry a Rook cousin, Tam Rook, told the nephew's men that the god's blocks were to be tallied by the Rooks before they left the cuts, and a quarryman named Hodd would not have it, and there was a fight with wedges and Hodd was killed. Scarrow kept its silent day. The nephew's half of the village would not cut stone for a month. In the tenth month the Rooks, forty of them with mattocks, went up Lord's Hill and dug inside the fallen wall where the hall was, which no one has done since the Driving, and on the fourth day broke into a stone room under the floor. They came down with eleven gold torcs, a chest of foreign bronze coin with marks on it, a silver dish as wide as a cartwheel, and a board of black wood with lines of signs cut in it and scraped off and cut again and scraped again, which the Rooks say is the roll of their house, cut off by Mother Tollan's people so the lords would be forgotten. They say it proves their line was wronged. There were also dead in the room, laid in stone with their names, which the Rooks left. Aldo Rook wears a torc at every market now and so do ten of his kin. Tollan's elders have said the hill is cursed and the Rooks will see it. Scarrow is two villages that share one quarry. Your blocks are coming down again, at a higher price, and from the nephew's cuts only.
- The gate at Greyholt's north wall was finished in the sixth month: two posts and a lintel of Scarrow stone, a yard outside it with a well and a wall waist-high, and Ket's house beside the gate. Ket wears a Harl cloak and a Greyholt belt and is called the speaker by both sides and worse things by both sides. Gorm Longhand, whose clan summers at Hagg Ridge two days above the village, came down to the yard in the eighth month with sixty sheep and sat with Ket and the headman for two days. The rate is set: whey, wool, and hardy sheep for iron, by a Greyholt measure that Gorm marked on the gatepost with his knife. Gorm said his clan will not raid Greyholt while the gate is open and the iron is good, and that he does not speak for Brude's clan or the deep-moor clans and would be lying if he said he did. Looking at the new gate he said that a gate should point at something, and that his own fold gate on the ridge "points at nothing and has for five grandfathers," which the Greyholt burners who cut on the ridge all laughed at as an old joke.
- Oss, the Lotfolk trader who built the pebble cairn, died on the road south of the Greel in the spring, the new Lotfolk trader says, a younger man who casts the stones faster and charges more. The cairn was at the boundary stone after the market as always.
- A dim night in the first month, two in the eleventh, one in the twelfth.

## Year 13

- The Skelder came to the spring market, forty with dogs, a week late again, and complained of the walk. They have a new winter camp on the Fell side that they call Ashen, and say the Mother of Dogs still names everyone at every fire every night and still will not say why the one-color beasts may not be hunted. They sold pelts for iron and would not sell a dog.
- The harvest festival in the ninth month was the largest the Row has seen. You asked for every village and every village came, Scarrow in two bodies that stood on either side of their stone and did not speak to each other, the Fenners from the first steadings, the Dun Mere twins who are now eight and were shown to everyone. You were heavier afterward than you have ever been. You felt it as weight.
- You went east after the festival with eleven men, four pack ponies, dark honey in pots, dried blood plum, smoked plain eel, and a sledge of dressed Scarrow stone cut small enough to carry, and no dog. Five weeks: down the valley, the Greelmen across the Greel at the Reed Crossing for a price, a week of wet lowland full of birds, then up onto the Tallgrass, which is grass to the horizon as high as a horse's shoulder, and wind. The Ruun found you on the second day on the plateau and rode beside you to the Fire Hollow, which is a round sunk place a mile across with the grass burned off it and forty bands' tents around the rim and a fire in the bottom the height of a hall.
- Isca is the fire, and she is also a woman a head taller than any man there, with an iron band on her head and grey hair in cords to her feet, knotted. She was the woman when you came down into the Hollow and the fire by the time you reached the bottom. She spoke to you before all of them, in the manner of her herald but longer. The sense of it, said with great ceremony, was that the fire of the Olm had come when called and would be remembered for it. You said who you were and where from, named your eight villages, and laid out what you had brought and what you wanted for it, horses and the freedom of the eastern road. She was courteous for a long time about it. The end of the courtesy was that Isca does not bargain, that a thing offered for a thing is a thing she cannot take, and that your eel and honey and stone would be eaten and kept as gifts freely given, and she thanked you for them in a form that took some while. Then she gave you twenty horses, and a rider for each to teach your people the use of them, and said the Reed Crossing was open to anyone who came with your word, and that this was not for the honey.
- That night the Long Song was sung, by a young man called Haddo whom she calls the Voice, nineteen, who stood by the fire from dusk to the small hours and sang every band, every camp, every spring and every dead Ruun in order while eight thousand people sang each name a half-beat behind him. Toward the end Isca herself sang, from inside the fire, and sang your eight villages in the order you had given them, and the Ruun sang them back. She said, after, that what is in the Song is in the Song. She said that if there were other names you wished kept, your dead, your households, your streams, you had only to send them and they would be sung, and that she would ask nothing for it because she does not bargain.
- Among the things she said before the Ruun, with pride, was that her bands that graze to the eastern edge of the plateau have come back this year and last not knowing their own camps or their own faces, and still able to sing the Song whole from the first name to the last, and that this shows the Song is stronger than the edge of the world. The Ruun cheered it. The men who had come back sat at the back and sang and were fed by their bands and did not know whose tents they slept in.
- She asked you, before all of them, about the weak fire to the west of her and north of you, that pulls and does not answer. You told her what you have felt of it: that it does not move and does not answer. She said she would send her Voice to look at it when the Song could spare him, since it lay nearer your country than hers. You came home in the twelfth month with the horses, and the valley talked of nothing else all winter, and the lantern walk was held late so you could be at it.
- The bridge at Ferrow Ford has two stone piers of four standing in the water. Dim nights: four.

## Year 14

- The bridge stands. Four stone piers, a timber deck a span above the Year 5 flood mark, railed, wide enough for a cart. Wat set the last pier stone himself in the low water of the first month and the whole upper valley came to walk across it in the fourth month after the flood, which was middling. The children of Ferrow Ford then wished to make their first crossing on the bridge, and the elders would not have it: the first ford is made in the water, below the bridge, as it always was, and the stone is given at the top of the bank. Three children did it in the fifth month with the whole village on the bridge watching. Shepherds driving flocks over the bridge find the sheep will not go onto the planking and have to be dragged, one at a time, by the first one.
- The twenty Ruun riders stayed through the summer teaching, and most of what they taught was that the Backs are too steep for horses and the valley floor too wet, and that a horse eats what three sheep eat. Twelve of the horses are at Tollan, four at Ferrow Ford, four at Weir. Two valley boys can ride well. The riders went home in the ninth month with plum wine and left one of their number, a woman named Reyn, who married a Weir man and stayed.
- The Harl at the Greyholt yard say the one-color beasts crossed the moor again this summer, a herd of hundreds, grey, that went north-west toward the big water and turned back at the edge of the heather as they always do. Gorm's clan drove their own sheep off the line of them. Ket says no Harl has ever seen one born or seen one dead that a man did not kill.
- Ossary. The Greelmen were paid in barley to bring forty boatloads of Scarrow stone down to the landing, and the revetment began in the seventh month at low water: a footing of great blocks set into the bank along the whole length of the village, and a channel cut across the neck of the bend above it to take the flood's head. Digging the footing trench went down through bone the whole length. The Ossary folk did it in silence and in order, as they do their burying, and laid everything in the eel shed. By the count the footing was in for two-thirds of the bank and the channel was cut and lined.
- Nan of Greyholt has gone to her sister at Lissom. Ebb burns charcoal above Greyholt with the other burners and is a good hand at it and is pleasant to everyone and calls Nan "the woman who was kind to me," which is why she went.
- The count, in tags: 1,101 households. Sheep on the Backs and in the folds 4,340. Goats 610, Greyholt's not yet recovered from the raid. Cattle 270. Horses 20. Barley in the Tollan granary at the count, a tenth of the valley's yield and three tenths of the granary. The children read it out at the Row from the tags and the elders of Lissom and Ferrow Ford disputed the sheep for a day, as always, and then found that the tags and their own cords agreed, and had nothing to argue about, and were unhappy.
- Dim nights: five, the first in the tenth month.

## Year 15

- The Ossary revetment was finished in the eighth month, the bank faced in stone from the landing to the last house, and the channel across the neck took the spring flood's head cleanly, so that the water in front of the village rose and fell and took nothing. The bank held. Ossary held its feast. The bone from the trench went up the hill in a procession of baskets and into a stone chamber the village built for it with a lintel across the door, and a stone set beside the door with a mark the headman chose, which is an eel. By the headman's own count there were some two hundred and sixty skulls and more of everything else, laid in rows. Among them, sent to the count house as you asked and not to the midden: fourteen fired clay tags like the one from the eighth year, with marks on them; two more bronze needles with an eye at each end; a bronze pin; a double handful of blue glass beads; and a child's bracelet of twisted bronze. They are in a box in the count house with the tags of the count. No one in the valley can say what the marks are. The salt caravaners, shown one, said it was not their writing and not lucky and would not hold it.
- At the Greyholt yard in the sixth month Gorm Longhand's people came down talking of riders from the east on the moor: sixty on tall horses, who came up by the Greel's west bank and across the moor's southern edge asking by signs for "the stone that fell," and were shown Fallow Dip by Brude's people for iron. One of the riders, a young man who sang, slept by the cairn there seven nights and the others would not. On the eighth day the riders went back east and the young man was singing a song the Harl had not heard before, about a crowd on its knees, which the Harl did not like because it is their dream and no one else's. Ket says Brude's clan is angry at itself for taking the iron.
- The road. You began the dry-side road from Tollan toward the Reed Crossing in the fourth month: Tollan to Weir first, raised a hand above the meadow, ditched both sides, with Scarrow chippings on top where there were chippings and river gravel where there were not. A day's walk became a half-day's. The ferryman says the bridge upstream and now a road past his landing will finish him and has bought a second boat.
- A Lissom girl walked the valley tied at the wrist to a Scarrow quarryman of the nephew's half and they arrived, and the marriage has made the two halves of Scarrow speak at one wedding, briefly.
- The black-legged fox has a den under the Tollan granary footing, in the dry course, and takes a hen a month, and the granary children feed it against orders.
- Hill lynx took eleven lambs on the Backs in the hard end of the winter and left blood everywhere, and Col said that is how a thing takes a lamb, and that whatever took them in the old days without blood has not taken one since the ninth year, which he puts down to the Harl gate. Pim says it stopped before the gate was built and no one listens to her.
- Dim nights: four.

## Year 16

- The Greelmen brought word up from the Reed Crossing in the fifth month that cattle are dying across the Greel, on the Ruun side and all down the lowland, blown up and dead in two days, and the Ruun are burning carcasses in heaps. No stock crossed the river into the valley this year and none died in it. Dun Mere says it is the tarn water that keeps the valley's cattle sound, and has begun selling it in jars.
- The Lotfolk trader at the autumn market, asked after the families who went up to the lake, said "the lake families have gone quiet," and that his god has sent ten more families to them, and cast his stones when asked what quiet meant and read the answer as "no more at this price."
- The road reached Dun Mere's turning and the dry rise under the Backs' southern end where the fen begins, so that a cart can go from Tollan to the fen edge in a day and a half in any weather. The caravan ground was laid out at the boundary stone on Tollan's south road: a flat of beaten earth, a well, hitching stones, no wall and no threshold. The salt caravans used it in the autumn and slept on it and said nothing, which from them is praise. The pebble cairn was there after the market as always, at the foot of the boundary stone, now at the edge of the caravan ground, and a Tollan child counted it: 1,106. The tags say 1,108.
- A hard storm in the eighth month knocked the barley flat at Weir and the granaries took what could be saved. The Weir granary, rebuilt higher, stayed dry.
- The Dun Mere twins, ten, settled which of them is the elder by a fight at the eel fair and the elder lost.
- Tam Rook, who began the fight at the quarry four years ago, was found dead at the foot of the Scarrow scar in the eleventh month with no mark on him but the fall, and Scarrow kept its silent day, and the Rooks say the nephew's men pushed him and the nephew's men say he was drunk. Your blocks stopped for the winter.
- Dim nights: five.

## Year 17

- Drought. No rain from the third month to the tenth. The Olm at Tollan was a chain of pools by the eighth month and the Lissom stream was a trickle between its nine, the Kettle dry for the first time Berit's grandson can remember. The granaries were opened at the count and the valley ate from them through the winter, a tenth of a harvest stretched over a bad one, and no one at Tollan or Weir went hungry, though Lissom and Ferrow Ford came down for grain and were given it and resent it. Greyholt drove its goats up to Vell in the sixth month, three hundred of them, with Pim's man leading, and kept them in the stone yard there on the cold water through the summer and brought them down fat in the tenth month, every one, and the Greyholt elders have said nothing about unlucky hills since. The burners who went up with the goats would not sleep inside the walls and slept on the bare half-day of grass instead, which they say is at least honest ground.
- The Skelder came to the spring market before the drought broke and were talkative. Three of their hunters who went west two years ago on the Mother's word, into the country past the Fell with dogs and food for ninety days, came back this winter alive, and able to speak, and unable to say the names of their own bands or their own, and their dogs knew them when they did not know themselves, and the Mother keeps the three at her own fire now and feeds them before anyone. The Skelder think it is a punishment for something the three did out there. One trader thinks it is a blessing and the three are holy, and the others told him to be quiet.
- The Ferrow Ford bridge stood over a dry riverbed for four months and the children walked under it and wrote on the piers with charcoal, and Wat whitewashed them.
- The salt wind came in the ninth month, dry on dry, and Scarrow's cuts were so full of dust the swifts left early. Garran, who is old now and still on the wall, said nothing about birds.
- The Harl yard at Greyholt did well out of the drought: moor sheep for iron, and the Greyholt folk learned to eat moor mutton. Gorm's clan kept the treaty. A deep-moor clan took forty sheep from a Ferrow Ford fold in the ninth month and Ket says they were not his people and the gate cannot stop a clan that does not come to it.
- Dim nights: four.

## Year 18

- A middling flood after the dry year, and a good barley. The granaries were refilled to the tenth. The Tollan elders have asked that the tenth become a fifth and the Weir elders have asked who is to carry it.
- The second Scarrow quarry, the Rooks' own, which they opened in the cuts above the nephew's after the deaths, sells stone cheaper and worse. Tollan buys from the nephew. Weir buys from the Rooks. Scarrow has two elders' stones' worth of grievance and one stone at the Row to stand on, and both halves stood on it at the count, back to back.
- A Greyholt woman at the Harl yard bought a hound pup off Gorm's people, moor-bred, and it eats, and Greyholt is pleased with itself.
- Reyn of Weir, the Ruun woman, had a child and named it at the first flood with a knot in its wrist-string as the valley does, and also sang its name at the river as the Ruun do, and Weir watched and said nothing and some of the Weir women have started doing both.
- The Fenners say the Brann Cut has silted so far that a boat cannot get up it in autumn now, and the bell under the fog is fainter for it, and they count that a mercy. The eels in the fen are fewer this year and the Fenners blame the drought.
- The grey plum at Lissom bore nothing anyone would pick. Berit's grandson says it is the dry year and the roots and that his grandmother was right about bland and wrong about grafting, and that the lower orchard has been sour since the piers went in at Ferrow Ford and backed the flood up into the Lissom mouth. The yellow and the blood plum were good.
- Dim nights: five, two of them on nights of the lantern walk and its eve, so that Tollan walked in the dark and came back to the Row and sat until the Lamp was bright.

## Year 19

- In the fourth month the ground shook, once, for the length of a breath, and dogs barked all down the valley and Weir's millstone, the one cut in the eighth year, cracked across. Nothing fell. The bridge piers were walked and are sound. The granary walls are sound. The miller has a new stone from the nephew's cuts and the Rooks say theirs would not have cracked.
- Ruun riders came up the new road in the sixth month, twelve of them, with greetings from Isca in her form of speech, which took the herald an hour, and a gift of forty cattle, bred back after the murrain, "for the fire that came when called." The riders sang at the Row every night they stayed. Among the lists they sang a song that is not a list, which they say is the Voice's own song, made in the west: a crowd on its knees in a place of many roofs, and a river none of them have seen, wide and slow, with boats on it. They sing it with great feeling and say the Voice sings it better. They also sang your eight villages, in order, which the valley had not heard sung by strangers since the singers came in the seventh year, and which made the old people quiet.
- The count: 1,117 households. Sheep 4,580. Goats 840. Cattle 310 with the Ruun gift. Horses 23. The granary tenth at Tollan and Weir both full. The children who keep the tags are grown, and one of them, the Lissom girl, Ede, has begun cutting a mark on each tag for the year of its making, which Hemma did not do, so that a tag may be told from a tag.
- A Harl trader at the yard, trading for a new iron pot, said the cairn at Fallow Dip has had a hut built beside it by Brude's clan this year, and two men who live in it and do not herd, and who are fed by the clan for telling the dream to men who come to sleep there before a raid. Ket does not like it and says the deep-moor clans like it very much.
- Ossary's bank held for the fifth spring and the feast has a name now, the Dry Bank, and the Fenners come up to it.
- A Dun Mere man walked into the tarn in the autumn fog, the same man as the eighth year, and was pulled out by the same dog, which is old now.
- Dim nights: four.

## Year 20. The 331st year after the Driving

- Lissom cut the grey plum trees in the second month, all of them, forty-one trees, and burned the wood and grafted blood plum onto the stumps that would take it. The pressing in the tenth month smelled the same and old Lissom people said it did not.
- The one-color beasts crossed the moor again this summer, Gorm's people said, more than they had ever seen, and turned back at the edge of the heather.
- In the ninth month a deep-moor clan came down on the Ferrow Ford folds on the Backs' north end in the night, sixty men, and took ninety sheep and killed a shepherd, Col's nephew, who stood in the fold gate. Ket says the men from the hut at Fallow Dip went with them and stood on a rock above the fold and said the dream aloud before the clan went down, the kneeling and the river, as a thing said before a fight. Gorm's clan did not go and sent word by Ket that they did not go, and have not come to the yard since, which Ket says is shame and the Greyholt headman says is guilt.
- The Lotfolk trader at the autumn market had a great deal to say for a Lotfolk. His god sent two hundred people west into the empty country four years ago, by lot, each with a stone with their own name cut on it and food for a hundred days. Eleven came back this spring. They had forgotten everything, their city, their god, their own names, and they were still carrying the stones, and the stones still had the names. His god has begun building a wall of cut stone around the city with every living Lotfolk name on it, and the whole city says the names at night. The trader thinks the west is cursed and the stones are proof the lot chose rightly who should go. He cast his stones for the price of the eel and the price was high.
- The ten years of tags hang in the count house on their cords, a thousand and more to a year, and Ede has begun a wall of pegs so that a household can be followed from year to year by its tag. The elders say it is a great deal of clay for a thing Hemma did on string.
- The harvest festival was full, every village, the Rooks on one side of Scarrow's stone and the nephew on the other. The Driving was told on the third night of the count as it always is. The children acted the lords and ran shrieking into the dark, and this year the Rooks' children would not run and stood by the fire in small torcs of twisted straw, and there was a silence, and then the Driving went on.
- You are heavier than you were in the tenth year by as much again as you gained in the first ten. You feel it as weight, and nothing else about it has changed. Dim nights: five.
