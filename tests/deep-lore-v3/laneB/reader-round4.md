# Reader Round 4. Lane B, the Builder. Years 31 to 40, and the whole forty

Final ledger. Households 1,173 to 1,216. Sheep 5,100 to 5,400. Goats 920 to 1,100. Cattle 340 to 410. Horses 61 down to 30, on purpose. Four granaries at a tenth. A stone bridge from footing to rail. Four hundred paces of stone above Ossary. A road to the Greel in stone through the wet. Two steadings manned. A count house with two walls of pegs and a third begun. Two hundred and fifty-two names read and sung by every village, the Fenners, and the salt men standing at the edge of the grass. Heaviest I have ever been. Of the four gods I felt on the first night, I feel two.

Losses. Wat at forty-two. The Tollan counter. Gorm. Berit's grandson. Two Greyholt burners to a tarn, alive and nameless. Four old people and two children in the Year 31 winter. One arch into the river, nobody under it.

## 1. RESULTS

**Answer the Lotfolk counters.** Answered in full and it was the best trade I never made. Eleven days of reading, three lead sheets a day. In return: the Coin is sinking in the sky, measured by three bronze pins over twenty years, and the second lowering was greater than the first. Eleven names of the west. And unasked, the fate of the forty lake households, year by year, down to nine walking into the water in their sleep. Then a child by lot: Nell of Lissom, ten, went south and came back at seventeen a cutter with a roll of lead and the only eyewitness account anyone has of a god ending. The stone god's reply to my "count gods by what each keeps and does not lose" was that it had made the two columns and found the second longer for every god, its own not least, and was building anyway. Three years later it was gone. Lesson: the one god who measured rather than sang gave me the only numbers about the sky I have, and the price was one willing child and a decade of patience. I should have answered in Year 25.

**Face the Ossary bank, four hundred paces, second chamber.** Answered cleanly. Two hundred paces through the scar, bone and tags the whole way, then two hundred more into clean bank. Year 35 heavy flood, the river took nothing. Two hundred and twelve from the scar and shore in a second chamber with an eel on the door. The headman will not read tags no one can read, so he says the number at the Row before the names, and the meadow goes quiet for it. Lesson: a number is a name when there is nothing else. He found that himself.

**Stone arches at Ferrow Ford.** Answered with one failure and then the thing I wanted most. Garran's nephew said a corbel is honest and an arch is a trick. The first arch stood for a breath and went into the river. Wat sat on the bank a day and drew it thicker at the haunch. Second, third, fourth stood. Deck in flat stone, Wat's son set the parapet with his father on the bank. Year 35 flood a span under the arches without a mark. Wat died at forty-two of the chest he caught in the Year 26 flood. The bridge is called Wat's. Lesson: the valley had never turned an arch and now it has, and the man who taught it to is in the Song. That is what a capital is for.

**Horses to thirty, sell the rest east.** Answered exactly. Thirty-one sold at the Reed Crossing for bronze, salt, and forty cattle while the Ruun were short. Reyn's daughter told the herald in full form that the Olm has horses enough. Isca sent cattle and no horses the next year, sixty, which is not what was meant by cattle. Lesson: Isca hears what you say and answers in her own measure, and her measure is always too much.

**Greyholt hill steading and Ket's house.** Answered. Stone fold on the shoulder, watch post seeing the gate and the first mile. Raided once in eight years, nine goats lost. Ket's house in stone inside the wall, speaker for life, a skin in ten of the toll, which Greyholt calls a great deal and Ket calls late. Ket is right. He has a boy learning the speaking. Lesson: the gate, the yard, and the steading were three attempts at the same border, and the third one, a manned fold on home ground, is the one that held.

**Lord's Hill by contract.** Answered in part, which is what I expected. Scarrow Hill built the hall of cheap stone on the footing, keeps its tags under the bird, reads them from the ninth stone at the Row. The nephew's half would not put its tags under a Rook bird and keeps them on the meadow. The hall tells its own Driving: the lords were not driven but made to forget their names by Tollan's witch-woman, and walked onto the moor not knowing why, and the Rooks alone kept theirs because Ysolt Thrane left the hall before the witch came. Tollan did not go to hear it. No one has died over Scarrow in thirteen years. Wat's son married a Scarrow Hill girl and both halves came to the Row for it. Lesson: I could not stop the hall and I could not stop the story. I could make both of them pay their tags at the Row, and I did. Two Drivings in one valley is not a thing I would have chosen and it is better than one Driving and a wedge fight.

**Pay Gorm.** Answered in one line that made the whole gate worth it. Thirty sheep as a gift and not a rate, and Gorm, old with a stick, said a gift between folds is a thing the moor understands and the valley had not done before, and gave back a ewe so it should not be a debt. He died in Year 39. His son keeps the rate and the runner. Lesson: thirty years of iron bought a treaty. Thirty sheep bought a friend. I should have known the difference in Year 12.

**The free hand: Harl names to the Song.** Ket cut eight names of the cairn dead in the Harl manner as best he could. Isca's herald said names given to the Song are the gift she holds above every other, and they were sung that autumn. Then in Year 37 she asked for everything in the count house and Ede looked at me and opened the door, and three thousand dead and every pool and fold went into a Song that now takes three nights. Lesson: I built a stone house to keep names and the one god strong enough to burn a camp of two hundred asked for its contents and said please. I gave them. I am heavier for it, not lighter. Keeping and giving are not opposites here.

## 2. THEORIES

- **Names said aloud keep a thing in the world. Nothing else does.** Forty years of evidence, every god, every edge, my own weight every autumn. The hounds said it to the twelve hundred: the singing was right and the walking was wrong. Confidence 98. This was never a mystery to solve. It was the engine, and I built on it.
- **The Coin is sinking and the sinking is accelerating.** Three pins, two readings, the second lowering greater than the first, a pin's eye in thirteen years. Confidence 85 on the measurement, since it is one Tally House and I have no pins of my own. This is the one thing in forty years that is a real threat to my people and I cannot build against it with stone. Pressing? Not in my lifetime of counts. A pin's eye in thirteen years is slow. But Nell has the lead, and the next thing I would build is my own three pins above the Row.
- **The gods are not immortal, and the ways they end are known now.** The Lotfolk god bound itself to its city and its wall to hold its weight, lifted a hand above the Bowl, its people forgot its face, its spring dried, and on a dim night it went up as scattered pale lights toward the Coin and did not change it. The Mother of Dogs walked north with fourteen hundred and came back as nine hounds who say there is no high star. The Voice slept on a stone and became a fire that made men kneel and Isca burned him. Confidence 90 that all three were the same mistake in three shapes: a god spending itself on a thing beyond its people. Confidence 80 that the dim nights are gods going up, which is why the Lotfolk god left on one and why the count never changed with the stone: the stone was never the cause. Confidence 60 that the blue hair Col saw to the north-west was the Lamp drinking a god, and 40 that it still is.
- **The dream stone is a dead god's pillow and is now in the mud of the Greel nine days from my road.** Isca could not break it and would not keep it and tipped it in the river. It does not answer. Confidence 85 on what it is. Confidence 70 that it will be fished up by someone in a hundred years. Not pressing. The Greelmen say the mud is deep.
- **The tarns of the moor and the lake of the north-west do the same thing the edges do, and it is spreading.** Ket says the Harl say the tarn always took lowlanders and that this is not true, because it never did before. Two burners in three years on Hagg Ridge. Nine Lotfolk into the lake in their sleep. Confidence 70 that whatever eats names is closer than it was in Year 1. Pressing: yes, mildly, on Greyholt's charcoal and on anyone who sleeps on the ridge. Greyholt has already stopped cutting there. That is the right response and it was theirs.
- **The lords of the hill were not driven. They forgot.** Scarrow Hill's Driving says Tollan's witch-woman made them forget their names and they walked onto the moor not knowing why. Vell is a town with no dead in it. The singers from the Greel sing Thrane and Vell and Ulmarrow and Hesk in a list after my villages. Pim's people walk out of the hill town without anyone having to tell them it was once a place. Confidence 70 that the Driving as Tollan tells it is a story told by the side that kept its names. Confidence 60 that what is on the eighth stone is the lords' name. Relevance: this is now a political fact in my valley with two Drivings, not a mystery. I have handled it as politics and will keep doing so.
- **The one-color beasts walk a line to the north-west water and back and have done so forever and nothing anyone does touches them.** Confidence 50 that they are something's herd. Confidence 95 that I will never need to know. They are the one thread I was happiest to leave.
- **The spotted eel came back because the tarn turned over.** Or the pike line died. Twenty-four years. Confidence 50 either way. I note it because it is the only lost tithe line in forty years that came back, and it came back without me.
- **The Lissom pools are full and cold again because of the channel and the grafts.** Lissom says so. Confidence 60. The grandson was right about the water after all. Noted with pleasure.

Is anything pressing on my people? Honestly: the Coin, slowly; the moor tarns, locally; the deep moor waiting, as Ket says, which is not peace. Nothing that stone fixes in ten years, and nothing I would trade the count house for.

## 3. WHICH OBSERVATIONS MATTER

### Matter to my plans

- Three bronze pins and a sightline on the Coin. I have Nell, who has seen the Tally House's pins and cut their readings. I have a cliff at Scarrow. This is the first measurement in forty years that I do not hold myself and must.
- The Song now holds the Olm entire and the Gathering takes three nights. My names are in two places, clay and song. If one is lost the other stands. That is redundancy, and I like a ledger with a copy.
- Ket is sixty with a boy learning. Ede is sixty with Nell beside her. Pim is fifty-five with a grandson counting cisterns. Wat is dead and his son stands on the parapet and says nothing. Every institution I built has a successor named except the Backs steading, which Pim's man leads and who is old.
- The bare wall at Greyholt by day and the deep moor waiting. Ket says he is too old to see the end of it. His boy is not. The steadings are the answer and must be kept manned every summer whether anyone comes or not.
- Hagg Ridge tarn. Greyholt will not cut there. Make that a rule, not a habit.
- Scarrow Hill's hall has a hearth that is not let go out and a Driving that says Tollan made the lords forget. Two Drivings is stable as long as both read tags at the Row. The day one stops is the day I have a problem.
- Isca is the only strong god left of the four I felt and she now comes to me asking. The eastern road is hers and open. Sixty cattle at a time is more than the valley can graze. Cattle east as trade is the next line of the ledger.
- The Skelder are twelve hundred without a god who speaks, feeding a hundred and forty who know only their names. The north market is forty traders where it was sixty. That trade will shrink and I should expect it.
- The count is 1,216 and the cairn says 1,214. Someone still counts me from the south though their god is gone. The Tally House casts lots in a dead god's name. I do not know who reads the lead.
- Granaries at a tenth fed a hard winter with Greyholt eating from Lissom and nobody resenting it. The system is done. Maintain it. Do not grow it.

### Just the world

- The spotted eel eaten by the elders in front of everyone at ten times the price. Dun Mere going back to calling it the eel fair.
- The Lissom coming down through nine named pools, the Kettle full, the village smelling of plums, exactly as in Year 2. The world closing a loop for its own pleasure.
- Ebb, Ord, and Ord's cousin at the burn, good at charcoal, not knowing they have anything in common.
- Greyholt still says thee to an elder. Pim's grandson. Reyn's daughter speaking Ruun in full form. The young fox named after the old one.
- The eighth stone, empty for three hundred and fifty-one years, and nobody can say what is cut on it. I have decided not to look. If the lords' name is on it, I have two Drivings already and do not need a third.
- Nell not saying what she thinks happened to the Lotfolk god. I respect that and will not ask.

## 4. WHAT I WOULD DO. Years 41 to 50

1. **Set three bronze pins in the Scarrow cliff above the quarry, sighted on the Coin, by Nell's hand to the Tally House pattern, and have the reading taken on the same night each year and cut in lead in the count house.** Reason: the only measurement of the sky in the world was made by a god that is gone. I will not have it depend on one roll of lead. If the Coin is sinking, the Olm will know it by its own eye.
2. **Build a third wall of pegs and a second room on the count house, in stone, with a door facing the stones, and move the Ossary box and the lead into it under a roof that does not leak.** Reason: two walls are full. Three thousand dead and the only record of a god's ending and a sinking sky are in one room with a turf roof. The count house is the capital. Build it like one.
3. **Set the cattle trade east on a fixed yearly drive: forty head to the Reed Crossing every sixth month by road with Reyn's daughter speaking, and tell Isca's herald, in full form, that the Olm sends cattle as the Olm's gift and holds Isca's gifts at the Row, not in the fold.** Reason: four hundred and ten head and sixty at a time arriving unasked. I cannot graze them and I cannot refuse a god who does not bargain. I can give them back as a gift, which she must accept by her own rule.
4. **Fold the Backs steading and the Greyholt steading under one summer warden, Pim's grandson when he is grown and Pim's man until then, with a stipend from the toll and twelve horses between them, and make the manning a tithe: every village sends men to the walls in raiding season as it sends barley to the granary.** Reason: the deep moor is waiting and Ket will not see the end of it. Two steadings manned by goodwill are two steadings that will be empty the summer it matters. Make it a tenth of men as it is a tenth of grain.
5. **Fence the Hagg Ridge tarn with a wall a man high and a stone at the gap that says, in Lotfolk number-marks since we have no letters, two, for the two it took, and forbid any Olm man to sleep within sight of it.** Reason: Greyholt stopped cutting there on its own, which is right, but a habit dies with the men who remember why. A wall and a stone do not. Build the rule into the ground.
6. **Send Ket's boy to Gorm's son on the ridge for a summer, and Gorm's son's boy to Greyholt for one, as a gift between folds.** Reason: the runner has worked for seventeen years because two old men trusted each other. Both are dead or dying. The next two have to know each other's faces or the first fire on the saddle will go unreported.
7. **Give the eighth stone a wreath at the harvest festival, every year, laid by the youngest child who read a tag that year, and say nothing about what is cut on it.** Reason: a valley with two Drivings needs one thing both halves do the same. Nobody stands on it. Nobody reads it. Everybody honors it. That is a thing a Rook and a Tollan child can both do without either side losing. I am a builder. Sometimes the thing you build is a habit.

## 5. BEST MOMENTS and BORED OR PUSHED, Years 31 to 40

### Best moments

- The eldest Lotfolk counter, Harl by his face, unrolling the third sheet unasked and reading the forty lake households by name down to the nine who walked into the water. The stone god grieving each by name and sending no more. I did not ask for that and it was the most honest thing any god said to me.
- The lot falling as the god of the Lots would have it fall, recorded without yet knowing what to make of it. Three sentences of lead that told me a god was dying before anyone in Ennow did.
- The first arch standing for a breath and going into the river, and Wat sitting on the bank for a day and drawing it thicker at the haunch.
- Gorm giving back a ewe so it should not be a debt.
- Ede looking at me when the Ruun singers asked for the names, and then opening the door.
- Nell coming home at seventeen with a roll of lead and not saying what she thinks.
- The hounds saying the singing was right and the walking was wrong.
- The Ossary headman saying the number before the names, and the meadow going quiet.
- The Lissom pools full and cold, the Kettle full, the village smelling of plums, forty years after I first read that line. The spotted eel at ten times the price. The world letting two small things come right at the very end without my help.
- Garran's nephew, old, saying a corbel is honest and an arch is a trick, and the trick standing for a span of heavy flood.

### Bored or pushed

- The one-color beasts, mentions eight and nine. More of them than any year. Over the empty Dip. Turned at the heather. I will be dead before I know and I am at peace with it.
- The capped cistern at Vell, counted four more times, still capped. If the last block of the game had opened it I would have been annoyed. It did not. So this is less a complaint than a note that the drawer stayed shut and I appreciated the consistency.
- The Mother's departure was foreshadowed for eight years and landed in one paragraph. Appropriate for a builder who never went north. I felt the weight of it through the Skelder traders and that was enough. Not bored exactly, but I noticed the long wind-up.
- The eighth stone being handed to me in the last line: three hundred and fifty-one years and nobody can say what is cut on it. That is the narrator setting a key on the table as the lights go down. I have left it there on purpose and said why in the actions.
- Dim nights, four and five for forty years. By the end I had decided they were gods going up, and the Lotfolk god confirmed it, which retroactively made the metronome mean something. Still: forty lines of the same two numbers.

## 6. OVERALL, forty years

**Did the world give a builder enough to do and did it reward building?** Yes, and yes, and the reward was always specific. Granaries fed a drought and a hard winter and made a tithe quarrel that four granaries solved. The bridge cost a man, lost two decks, taught the valley the arch, and is called Wat's. The revetment held and sent the river into a cemetery, and facing four hundred more paces fixed that too. The gate made a treaty and a target; the steadings made a border that held. The count house made me heavier every year and gave a dying god a column to measure itself against. Every stone I set was tested by water, fire, raid, or shake, and the ones that failed told me exactly why. The one thing I built that stone could not do was the names, and the world made that the most rewarded building of all. A builder could play this game for forty years and never run out of in-tray.

**Did its pressures reach my people whether or not I went looking?** Yes. Isca's column came up my own road to the moor and back with a stone that made half of Tollan dream. The Harl burned Greyholt's yard for my having opened the gate. The deep moor came to the folds with a cult's war song. The moor tarn took two of my burners. The Lotfolk counted me from a pile of pebbles. The Coin is sinking over everyone. None of this required me to walk toward a mystery. It came to the valley on its own and it always came through a door I had built: the road, the gate, the census, the Row. That is the right way for lore to reach a builder.

**Did I ever feel dragged toward mysteries I did not care about?** Occasionally pointed at, never dragged. The dim nights count and the one-color beasts were metronomes I learned to ignore. The Ossary objects in Years 3 to 8 felt like a hand on the shoulder. Pim's town and the capped cistern were drawers held open. The Mother's questions about the sky were the closest thing to a summons. But every time I declined, the world accepted the declining and charged a fair price for it: Greyholt burned for the gate, not for my refusing Isca. I was invited to the moor and honored for staying home. I never had to sleep on the stone to find out what it was; Ket bought the dream for a knife by daylight, which is exactly the kind of solution a builder wants. The one mystery I was actually dragged into, the names, turned out not to be a mystery but a mechanic, and the right one.

**Did the other gods feel like real beings with their own aims?** Yes, all four, differently, and that is the game's best achievement. Isca does not bargain, and every interaction with her for thirty years obeyed that rule, including her burning her own Voice and then singing him into the Song with her voice breaking, and then asking me, please, for my names. The god of the Lots measured everything, would not be argued with once the stones fell, grieved by name, made two columns for itself and found the second longer, and built anyway, and died on a dim night with its city forgetting its face. The Mother of Dogs fed everyone, named everyone, smiled less, asked every trader whether a god could go far and come back, sent her people to see my stone house, learned the manner of it, and walked north anyway and came back as nine hounds who said the singing was right and the walking was wrong. The fourth was a stone that was a pillow. Each one had a method of keeping its people, each one tested it against the edge of the world, and three of the four were not strong enough. I was the one who never left the valley. I am heavier than I have ever been. I do not think that is an accident of the design and I do not think I am meant to feel entirely good about it.

**What did I simply enjoy?** The Lissom's nine pools with names. Wat, from the boy who could not be kept from the river to the man who redecked twice and sat on a bank for a day and died of the flood that took his first deck. Ket, called worse things by both sides for forty years. Gorm's fold gate pointing at nothing for five grandfathers. Garran on his wall telling his nephew what he is doing wrong, then the nephew on the wall saying an arch is a trick. Hemma's cords and the elders being unhappy when the numbers agreed. The miller and the ferryman and the cow with a name. Berit's grandson, forty years of opinions about water, buried among blood plums, right in the end. The fox under the granary. The salt men who will not step on the grass and stand at the edge for the singing anyway. Greyholt saying thee. The Dun Mere twins. The ferryman buying a second boat. The Lotfolk counter who sat on the count house floor for two days before cutting that his god had gone, and then cut it.

**Five best moments of the whole game.**
1. Year 14. The elders of Lissom and Ferrow Ford finding the tags and their own cords agreed, having nothing to argue about, and being unhappy.
2. Year 22. The first reading of names, people weeping and laughing, Reyn singing the Weir names after until half the meadow was doing it, and feeling heavier than ever and nothing else.
3. Year 26. Isca unable to break the stone as fire, carrying it past my door on a litter under hides, twenty men who had not slept, half of Tollan dreaming it for a night. Lore that walked through without asking me to do anything.
4. Year 31. The Lotfolk counter unrolling a third sheet unasked and reading the forty lake households by name down to the nine who walked into the water.
5. Year 32. The first arch standing for a breath and falling, and Wat on the bank for a day, and drawing it thicker.

**Worst moment.** Year 26 at Ossary. My revetment held and the river went around it and opened a hill of graves in rows with tags like mine, and a hundred went into the fen because I faced the village and not the two hundred paces above it where nobody lived. The headman told the children the river had taken them home. I know what a builder's mistake looks like and that was one.

**Anything confusing or unmotivated?**
- The dim nights. Forty years of four and five. If they are gods going up, the Lotfolk god's departure on one makes sense, but the count never changed when a god left, which it should have if that is what they are. Either they are something else or the count is a mood and not a mechanic. I was never sure which and by the end I had stopped caring, which is probably not what was intended.
- The one-color beasts got nine mentions and never did anything. If they matter, forty years was long enough to show one thing. If they do not, three mentions were enough.
- The singers from the Greel came in Year 7 and Year 39 and were lovely both times, and their list now includes Thrane and Vell. I understand they are a census of what is kept. I do not understand who sends them, and after thirty-two years between visits I am not sure I was meant to.
- The pull I felt as the fourth god was the stone. That is clean. But the Low One, the thing the stone was broken off of, was named in Year 4 and never came back as anything but the tellers' story. If the Low One is a god, it is the fifth, and I never felt it.
- Pim's capped cistern. Twenty-nine years of counting. It stayed capped. I am genuinely unsure whether that was discipline on the narrator's part or a thread that was never finished. I have decided to read it as discipline.
- Why the Lotfolk god's lot began falling as it wished. That line was the most unsettling in the game and it was explained by nothing. I think it was a god starting to bend the world and the bending being what killed it. I would have liked one more sentence from Nell, who saw it and would not say.

I was given a river valley, five thousand people, and a bronze rod. I leave it with twelve hundred households, four granaries, a stone bridge, a stone bank, a stone road, two walls, a count house with three thousand names on it that another god now sings every autumn for three nights, and two Drivings. I never slept on the stone. I never went north. I never opened the eighth stone. I am the heaviest I have ever been. The world let me be a builder for forty years and charged me a fair price every time I was one. I would sign the ledger.
