# The Olm country: the fixed world

Everything here is locked before day one. The Keeper runs it as written. Nothing is added after the lock; the only things that change are what the player and the NPC gods do and what the world does in answer.

## 1. The map in words

Distances are days on foot for a healthy adult with a pack, in good season. Double them in winter or flood.

**The Olm valley** runs north-west to south-east, six days from Greyholt at the top to Ossary at the bottom. The Olm rises in the Caddon Hills, brown in spring flood and clear green by autumn, fast and stony above Tollan, slow and willow-lined below. The valley floor is a day wide at its widest, at the great bend at Tollan. Hills close it on both sides: the Caddon Hills to the north-west, grey rock and goat paths; the Backs to the east, a long bare upland of sheep pasture, wind, and small folds; the Scarrow heights to the west, where the grey stone shows in a scar above the quarry village.

Downstream in order:
- **Greyholt** (400). At the hills' foot. Charcoal burners and goat keepers. The headman's hearth-stone is a round grey cap carved with a snake. Four days above it by goat path, the ruin of Vell, which no one visits.
- **Lissom** (450). One day below Greyholt, on the Lissom stream where it comes down from the Backs to join the Olm. Orchards: the yellow plum, the blood plum, the small grey plum. Plum wine. The stream has nine named pools between the orchards and the river.
- **Ferrow Ford** (600). One day below Lissom. The upstream ford, stony, where children make their first crossing. Shepherds who summer on the Backs. The high fold at the Backs' crown is called the Crown Fold.
- **Tollan** (1,300). One day below Ferrow Ford, at the great bend. The largest village and the meeting place. The Stone Row: eight standing stones on the river meadow where the elders stand to speak, the eighth with Mother Tollan's mark. The autumn count is held here, and the autumn salt market, and the spring market when the Skelder come. The ell of Tollan, a bronze rod, hangs in the count house.
- **Scarrow** (700). A half-day west of Tollan and up. Quarry village under the grey scar. Swifts nest in the cuts. Above it, Lord's Hill: a ring of fallen wall, brambles, the footprint of a hall. No one digs there.
- **Weir** (400). One day below Tollan. The weir and the mill, the mill pond with its old carp, the ferry. The Greelmen tie up here. The miller and the ferryman have feuded for twenty years over a cow.
- **Dun Mere** (500). A half-day east of Weir, on a dark tarn under the Backs' southern end. Eel fishers. Smokehouses. The spotted eel and the plain eel.
- **Ossary** (350). One day below Weir. The last village. The spring flood exposes bone in the river bank every year and the villagers rebury it. Fenners come up to the landing to trade eels for grain.
- **The Merrow Fen** begins a half-day below Ossary and runs three days to the Olm's mouth in the Greel: reed, cut, mere, and the Fenners' stilted steadings. The Brann Cut is a side channel a day into the fen on the north side.
- **The Olm mouth**, three days below Ossary. A drowned stone jetty two spans under. The Greel.

About 300 more live in steadings between villages. Total about 5,000.

**The Greel** is the great slow river east and south of the valley, flowing from the north-east, where the Skelder Fell drops to the salt flats, south past the Olm mouth and on into dry country. Wide, brown, slow, and getting wider. The Greelmen live on it in flat boats. The Reed Crossing, five days east of the Olm mouth, is where the salt caravans ford it.

**North-west: the moor road.** From Greyholt up over the Caddon Hills by Hagg Ridge (two days; the Harl's summer fold with the Notch for its gate), then down onto the Harl moor (four days), a bare rolling country of heather, bog, and the Harl barrows in their rows. Cold Hand, a fallen stone used as a clan boundary, eight days. Fallow Dip, a hollow a half-day beyond it with a cairn in it, nine days. The moor runs on north to the Skelder Wood at twenty days. From Fallow Dip the high moor runs west-north-west fifteen more days to Hush Water: twenty-five days from Tollan altogether.

**Hush Water.** Two days across, flat, grey, unmoving. The Handfolk's reed huts on the eastern shore, by grass mounds they do not walk on. No ice in winter. No ripple in wind.

**North: Skelder Wood and the Fell.** Twenty days by the moor road through Harl country, twenty-five by the safer way along the Greel's west bank. Pine, birch, cold. Maudre's country.

**East and north-east: the salt flats.** Thirty days from Tollan by the Reed Crossing. White crust to the horizon. Dead Sennet's steps. The Whale Walk. The caravan road runs along the flats' southern edge. Beyond the flats, country thins toward the Pale; Ebb of Greyholt went that way.

**East-south-east: the Tallgrass.** Five weeks: across the Greel at the Reed Crossing, a week of wet lowland, then up onto the plateau. Long grass, wind, the Ruun bands, the Fire Hollow. Isca's country.

**South-west: the Terraces.** Six weeks: south along the Greel's west bank by the salt market road, then west into broken dry hills, then the stepped dry riverbeds. Ennow at the head, from Year 9. Pell's country.

**The Pale.** Ninety days from Hush Water in every direction. From the valley, about sixty-five days south-east past the Tallgrass's far edge, or past the flats to the east. In the Pale, things are still things and no longer particular things. Travelers come back plain.

## 2. The player's people

**The Olm folk.** About 5,000 in eight villages, farmers of barley and beans, keepers of sheep on the Backs and goats in the hills, eel fishers, quarrymen, charcoal burners, plum growers. No lord since the Driving, 312 years ago. Governed by the elders of each village at the Stone Row, who agree slowly. The god arrived on the river meadow at Tollan on arrival night, which the whole valley saw as the Lamp flaring and a weight settling on the bend.

**Customs.** The autumn count at Tollan, when every village brings its tally and the elders argue for three days, and the Driving is told on the third night. Naming at the first flood after a birth, with a knot tied in the child's wrist-string. The first ford at Ferrow Ford, when a child crosses alone and is given a river stone. The lamp-watch: on dim nights, someone at every hearth stays awake until the Lamp is bright again; no one knows why. Ossary's reburying after the flood. The Lissom plum pressing. Scarrow's silent day after a death in the cuts. Marriage by walking: couples walk from Greyholt to Ossary tied at the wrist and are married if they arrive. "He gave me his cord" for a promise. The lantern walk on the longest night from the Stone Row to the great bend and back carrying no light but the Lamp's.

**Speech.** The Olm folk say "sennet" for a long wait and say a stubborn girl "has Aud in her." Greyholt says "thee" to an elder and nowhere else does; this goes in Year 30 if the fading runs.

**Beasts of the valley.** Glass eels in the Olm's upper reaches, clear as water. The spotted eel and the plain eel of Dun Mere. Hill lynx on the Backs that take lambs in a hard winter and leave blood. Swifts in Scarrow's cuts. Grey geese that come to the fen in autumn in thousands. Otters at Weir. A black-legged fox. Red deer in the Caddon Hills. Marsh lights in the fen that have always been marsh lights. The old carp of Weir's pond. Bees in the Backs' heather, a dark honey. Rooks at Scarrow, which is where the family got its name.

**Weather.** Spring flood on the Olm, three to five days, reliable. A dry wind from the east in late summer off the flats, which the valley calls the salt wind. Autumn fog that fills the valley to the hill line. Winters that freeze the ford most years and Weir's pond every year. Snow on the Caddon Hills by the count. Dim nights four or five times a year when the Lamp fades for an hour, mostly in winter.

## 3. The godless peoples

**The Harl** (about 2,500). Moor clans north-west, herders of hardy sheep and raiders of the valley's upper villages in lean years. Descended partly from Vell's emptying and partly from the Thrane walked onto the moor, which they do not know. Clan barrows in rows. They summer at Hagg Ridge (Gorm Longhand's clan) and winter in the moor's folds. They saw the Warm Stone fall on arrival night and cairned it at Fallow Dip; they sleep beside it before raids and dream the kneeling crowd. By Year 20, undisturbed, they have stone-priests who recite the dream. They say the Low One was hung by the first men as a lamp against wolves. Their chief clan in Year 1 is Brude's; Brude is the hunter who opened the pale beast in Year 5. The Harl leave a bowl of whey on the Greyholt wall some nights and sometimes take the bowl Greyholt leaves.

**The Fenners** (about 600). Stilt-house people of the Merrow Fen, eel and goose hunters, boat-poling, intermarried with Ossary but their own. They remember the singers would not take fish. They tell of a drowned trading post in the Brann Cut. They say the fen was once a lake and will be again, which is wrong both ways.

**The Greelmen** (about 1,500). Boat families on the Greel, trading salt, bronze, grain, and news from the flats to the dry south. They tie up at Weir. They say the Greel is getting fat from northern rain. They tell of a flat boat lost long ago in the fen with bronze in it. They carry anyone anywhere for a price and keep no god.

**The Sennet caravans** (about 2,000, scattered). Salt traders of the flats, descended from Sennet. Come to Tollan's autumn market every year by the Reed Crossing. Say the sea was angry and left. Say "as Aud at the steps" for stubbornness. Count tides in their songs without knowing it. Can read a little of the old Sennet script. Regard dead Sennet's steps as unlucky and will guide a god there for a high price and not go down them.

**The Handfolk** (about 400). The people of Hush Water's eastern shore. Fish the lake's edge, not its middle. Point instead of calling each other; have words for everything else. Will not walk on the mounds. Do not know they were the Ulm. An elder will say, if a stranger stays a night, that their grandparents had names and the water did not want them.

**The Lotfolk settlers** (thirty families, Year 11; forty by Year 14). Pell's shore experiment. Settle a half-day north of the Handfolk. Their children are pointing by Year 14. By Year 25 they are Handfolk.

## 6. Keeper neutrality (one page, for whoever runs it)

Read before every session.

**Everything is already placed.** Every clue, treasure, and history in these files exists from the year marked and is findable from that moment by anyone who goes where it is or asks the one who saw it. You never hold a clue back because the player is "not ready," and you never bring one forward because the player is stuck. If the player goes to Vell in Year 1, Vell is there. If the player never goes, it is there anyway. The schedule above is a forecast of when the world pushes things toward a god who sits still; a god who goes finds them sooner. (Rule 8.)

**You neither guide nor rescue.** When the player concludes something wrong from honest facts, you let the facts fall. You do not plant a second explanation beside a true sighting to reopen a closed question, and you do not drop a new clue to revive a line that went quiet. If the player believes the lake is a hot spring and builds a bath house, the bath house is built and the water is warm and nothing corrects them until they act on the belief in a way the world answers. If the player believes the Rooks are rightful lords, the Rooks are lords until the board is read. A god discovers its own errors by acting on them. (Rule 9.)

**You never correct a belief about the world.** Not in narration, not in an NPC's mouth, not by tone. A mortal who explains a cause is wrong about something that matters, every time, and you let the mortal be wrong. Out of game, you may ask the player a question about their reasoning ("What would you expect to see if that were so?") so they think again. You never supply the answer or lean on the question. (Rule 10.)

**Tone marks nothing.** A clue item in a morning story is written in exactly the register of the plain items around it. If you find yourself lingering on a detail because you know it matters, cut the lingering. The test: which three things would a bored farmer repeat at the well? If the clue is among them, it is too loud.

**The NPC gods act by their sheets.** Isca does what Isca does in the year she does it, unless the player's actions have changed the world she acts in. Maudre is wary of everyone, including a player who has done everything right, until the years are counted. Pell takes the offers in Year 30 whatever the player has told it. You do not soften a god because the player likes it or harden one because the player was rude. Their reactions to contact are written; use them.

**Wrong branches cost and teach.** Every wrong branch in the response table costs time or people and teaches something true. None is permanent ruin. If the player's action is not in the table, ask: what would this world, with these beings in it, do? Answer that, and write it down so the same action gets the same answer next time.

**Restraint is an action.** A player who waits gets the clocks. The clocks are not punishment; they are the world continuing.

**Some things stay buried.** The silver, the plates, the roll, the stone under the lake, Marrit's name. If no one finds them, no one finds them. Do not steer anyone toward them. Richness, not failure. (Rule 11.)

**When in doubt, say less.** The world is mostly not a clue. Describe the valley. Let the player decide what matters.
