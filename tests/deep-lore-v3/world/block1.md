# Block 1. Years 1 to 10

## Year 1. The 312th year after the Driving

- You came down on the river meadow at the great bend, where eight standing stones stand in a row with the grass worn flat in front of them. The whole valley saw the Lamp flare. At dawn the elders of Tollan came across the meadow with bread and salt and stood on their stones to look at you, one to a stone, and the eighth stone stood empty because that one is never stood on.
- You felt the others arrive. Three of them, far: one east and a little south, one north, one south and west. And a fourth, nearer, north-west, weaker than the others, that does not move and does not answer when you lean toward it.
- The valley is six days long, the river brown with the last of the flood. Eight villages. Tollan at the bend is the largest; Greyholt at the top under grey hills; Ossary at the bottom where the fen begins. A bronze rod hangs in the count house at Tollan by which they measure cloth and timber; the elders say it is the length of Mother Tollan's arm.
- In the autumn every village brought its tally to the Stone Row and the elders argued for three days about who had under-counted their sheep. On the third night they told the Driving: how three hundred and twelve years ago Mother Tollan rose, and the villages went up Lord's Hill with torches, and the lords fled into the moor and never came back. Children act the lords and run away shrieking.
- After the count, the salt caravans came in from the east by the Reed Crossing: forty camels, white salt in skins, bronze pins, dye. The caravaners will not sleep inside a wall, will not step on a threshold with the left foot, and think every old place is unlucky. They were gone in four days.
- Autumn fog filled the valley to the hill line for a week, so that from the Backs the hills stood up out of it like islands. Grey geese came down to the fen in thousands.
- Winter froze the ford at Ferrow Ford and the mill pond at Weir. On the longest night the people of Tollan walked from the Stone Row to the great bend and back carrying no light, because the Lamp is enough. On one night in the twelfth month the Lamp faded for an hour and at every hearth in the valley someone sat up until it brightened. They call it the lamp-watch. No one knows why it is kept.

## Year 2

- A mild flood. At Ossary the bank gave as it does every spring and bones came out of it, and the villagers gathered them in baskets and buried them again on the hill above the village with the others, which they have always done.
- The miller at Weir and the ferryman have not spoken in twenty years over a cow. Each sends his children to the other's house with messages so that neither has to say a word to the other. The whole village knows the cow's name.
- Col, who summers with the Ferrow Ford flocks on the Backs, came down in autumn and mentioned, when asked whether the high pasture had been good, that on the dim nights up there you can see a blue hair come down out of the Lamp far off to the north-west, over the moor. He thinks the Lamp drinks from a river up there. He has seen it four times in his life and does not care for it.
- A Lissom girl and a Weir boy walked the valley from Greyholt to Ossary tied at the wrist and arrived, and are married. It took them nine days because she stopped to look at everything.
- The Skelder came to the spring market from the north with pelts, as they do every few years, forty of them with dogs. They would not sell a dog at any price and laughed when asked.
- The Lissom stream comes down out of the Backs through the orchards in nine pools, each with a name: the Kettle, the Long Pool, the Pool of the Drowned Ox, and so on. The plums were pressed in the tenth month and the whole village smelled of it.
- A dim night in the eleventh month. The lamp-watch again.

## Year 3

- A heavy flood, five days. The ford at Ferrow Ford moved a stone's width downstream and the children had to learn it again. At Ossary a great deal of bone came out of the bank, and among it a bronze needle as long as a hand with an eye at each end, which the headman brought to you because he did not know what it was for.
- Harl raiders came off the moor in the fourth month and took thirty sheep from the Ferrow Ford folds. One Harl man was caught, Ket, a young one with a broken ankle. He is held at Ferrow Ford, eats more than two shepherds, and has told everyone who will listen that the Harl keep a lamp-watch too and that theirs is older.
- At the count a Greyholt elder complained of a herd-child, Pim, nine years old, who followed a goat up into the hills last summer and came back saying there is a town in the hill with wells of cold water and nobody in it, and will not stop saying so. The elders have forbidden the hills, which are unlucky, and the elder wanted it known that the child lies.
- At Scarrow the swifts came back to the quarry cuts in the fourth month as always. The stone master, Garran, lost two fingers under a block in the seventh month and the village kept its silent day though no one died.
- A dry wind came off the flats in late summer, the one the valley calls the salt wind, and the barley came in early and hard.
- Dun Mere held its eel fair: the smokehouses opened, the plain eel in strings, the spotted eel sold singly at twice the price because it is sweeter. A Dun Mere man will tell you the spotted eel can be told from the plain by the way it looks at you.
- Greyholt leaves a bowl on its north wall some nights. In the morning it is sometimes gone and sometimes there is a Harl bowl of whey in its place.

## Year 4

- Drought. No rain from the fourth month to the ninth. The Olm could be walked across at Tollan without wetting the knees. Greyholt's goats died on the hill for want of water. [Keeper: if Vell's cisterns were reached in Years 2 or 3, the goats were driven up and lived.] The elders came to the Stone Row and asked you, formally, for rain.
- Ket the Harl, after a year at Ferrow Ford with his ankle mended, has said that on the night you came a stone fell out of the sky onto the moor at a place his people call Fallow Dip, and was warm for a season, and his clan built a cairn over it. He says men who sleep by it dream of a crowd kneeling. He thinks it is a piece of the Low One, broken off when the gods came through.
- Ebb, a Greyholt charcoal burner, went east with the salt caravan after the market "to see the edge of the world." His wife Nan did not see him off.
- A hard winter after the dry summer. Weir's pond froze to the bottom and the carp were dug out of the mud alive in the thaw. The ford froze for two months. Two old people died at Ferrow Ford steadings.
- There is a boy at Ferrow Ford, Wat, who cannot be kept from the river. He has been pulled out four times. His mother has given up and taught him to swim.
- The rooks at Scarrow left their trees in the ninth month all at once and came back in the tenth, and the quarrymen say this means a long winter, which it was.
- A Scarrow girl made her first ford alone in the fifth month and was given her river stone.

## Year 5

- The thaw came late and all at once, and the Olm ran brown for three weeks. Ferrow Ford lost its footbridge and built it again a span higher.
- At the count, Aldo Rook of Scarrow stood on a stone and held up a gold torc as thick as a child's wrist and said his family has held it since the Driving, that they are the blood of the lords of the hill, and that the coming of a god to the valley is the sign that Lord's Hill should have a lord on it again. He claims the hill and the old tithe. Tollan's elders shouted him down. Scarrow's did not.
- Lambs have been going from the Backs for two years, one or two a month, with no blood and no drag mark, and Ferrow Ford has blamed the Harl. This summer Col found a knot of twisted grass tied to a fold post where a lamb had gone, in a pattern no herder ties. He thinks it is a Harl sign and wants a raid.
- Twins were born at Dun Mere, which has not happened there in living memory, and the whole village has an opinion about which is the elder.
- The grey geese came to the fen in the greatest numbers anyone remembers, and the Fenners took them with nets at night and sold goose at the count for the first time.
- Hemma of Tollan, who is seventy-nine and keeps the count on knotted cords, one cord to a village, says this year's cords are the longest since she began keeping them at twelve.
- A dim night in the second month and another in the twelfth.

## Year 6

- Ebb came back from the east with the caravan. He can speak and he can work. He does not know Greyholt's name or his wife's, and he does not know why Nan weeps when he is pleasant to her. The caravaners say the salt did it, that it happens to men who go past the last well. Nan came to the Stone Row and asked you to give him back.
- A Greyholt charcoal burn got away in the dry seventh month and took a hillside of gorse above the village. No one was hurt. The burners say gorse that has burned comes back thicker, and are looking forward to it.
- Dun Mere and Weir have fallen out over the date of the eel fair, which Dun Mere moved a week later because the eels were late, and which Weir says has been the same week since the Driving and should stay so.
- Otters have taken up residence under the Weir mill race and the miller's children feed them, against orders.
- The ferryman's cow, the one the feud was about, died of age at Weir, and the miller came to the burying, and the two men have spoken.
- The bees on the Backs made a dark honey this year that tastes of heather and smoke, and the Ferrow Ford women sold it at the count by the pot.
- The Fenners have abandoned three steadings on the north side of the fen, near a side channel they call the Brann Cut, because of a bell that rings under the fog there in autumn. They say it is the drowned trading post ringing for its dead. The eels in that cut were the fattest in the fen.

## Year 7

- In early summer forty strangers came up the Olm from the Greel, men and women, dressed in nothing particular, carrying nothing to trade. They came to the Stone Row at dusk and sang until dawn. Their songs are lists of names. They asked the name of every village in the valley and sang each one back exactly, then went on to names no one knew. They would not take fish, took bread, and left at first light upstream toward Greyholt and the moor road. Hemma, eighty-one, says they came when she was a child, and that they "sang the villages back to us" then too. [Keeper: if the god spoke to them, one of them said they carry names across the bare country, and a name carried long enough comes back.]
- At the spring market a Harl man sold a dog: grey-white, long-legged, silent, "a wolf-dog from the north," netted on the moor. It will not eat and will not look at anyone. The Weir man who bought it wants his salt back.
- The spring flood was small and the Ossary bank held, and Ossary, with no bones to bury, held a feast instead, and has decided this should be the custom in any year the bank holds.
- Wat of Ferrow Ford, who cannot be kept from the river, made his first ford alone and was given his stone, and then swam back across for the pleasure of it.
- The salt wind came late and strong and brought grit from the flats that got into everything. Lissom's plums were small.
- The swifts left Scarrow a week early. Garran the stone master says this means nothing, and that people who read weather in birds should try lifting a block.
- A black-legged fox has been seen in daylight in Tollan's lanes three times this autumn, and the hens are shut in early. Children have named it.

## Year 8

- A cairn of white pebbles appears at the boundary stone on the south road each autumn after the salt market and is gone by spring. Three years ago a Weir boy counted it for a dare and got the number of households in the valley that year, which the valley thought a good joke. This autumn a child saw the Lotfolk trader Oss building it at dusk. The Lotfolk are the traders from the far south-west who cast marked stones from a bag for every decision, including what to charge, and will not be argued with once the stones have fallen; their god, they say, is a child made of stones who lives in a bowl. Oss, asked about the cairn, said it is so his god knows how many of us to pray for.
- At Lissom, old Berit, who is ninety, says the small grey plum was the best of the three when her mother was a girl, and that it went bland first, before it went small, and that this year's are not worth drying. She blames the new grafting. Her grandson blames her memory.
- Pim of Greyholt, now fifteen, married a Ferrow Ford shepherd and has gone to live on the Backs. She no longer talks about the town in the hill. [Keeper: if the Vell path has been used, omit.]
- A middling flood. The usual bone at Ossary, and this year a fired clay tag the size of a thumb with marks scratched in it, on a rotted cord, which the headman sent to you with the eel tithe.
- The miller cut a new millstone from Scarrow and the ferryman has remarried, a Fenner widow who poles a boat better than he does.
- The autumn fog lasted eleven days. A Dun Mere man walked into the tarn in it and was pulled out by his dog.
- The lantern walk on the longest night. The Lamp was bright and the whole of Tollan went.

## Year 9

- A hard winter. Weir's pond froze to the bottom again and this time the carp did not all live. The ford froze in the eleventh month. Three old people died in the steadings above Ferrow Ford. [Keeper: if Marrit has not been found, she is one of them, unknown to anyone; nothing is said of it.]
- Scarrow quarreled with Tollan over the quarry tithe in the spring and did not come to the harvest festival at the Stone Row, though they sent the tithe. You were lighter that season than the tithe accounted for, as you were lighter in the spring after the hard winter of the fourth year, when no one came to the Stone Row, and heavier after every harvest festival since. You feel it as you feel your own weight. [Keeper: if the Rook claim is unresolved, Scarrow has also split, half the village for the Rooks, and the quarrel is about that too.]
- Harl traders at the Greyholt wall, trading whey and wool for iron, said the one-color beasts crossed the moor again this summer, which they do some years and not others, a herd of hundreds all the one grey, with no smell to them. The Harl do not eat them. Brude, who is a chief among them, cut one open some years ago and found "meat and nothing else, no heart you could find," and the Harl have not touched one since.
- A Lissom couple walking the valley tied at the wrist untied at Weir on the sixth day and walked home separately, and Lissom is divided over whose fault it was.
- The red deer came down out of the Caddon Hills in numbers in the hard winter and Greyholt and Lissom hunted them on the snow, and there was venison at every hearth above Tollan.
- Garran the stone master gave up the quarry to his nephew and sits on the wall now telling the nephew what he is doing wrong.
- The Skelder came to the spring market a week later than usual and complained the whole time of the walk. Asked about their god, they say she is the Mother of Dogs, that she feeds anyone who comes to her fire, that she speaks every person's name at every fire every night so that no one is forgotten, and that she has forbidden them to hunt the one-color beasts that cross the Fell or to walk south-west past the Fell's end, and will not say why. They think she is saving the beasts for a feast.

## Year 10. The 321st year after the Driving

- In the fifth month twelve riders came up from the Reed Crossing on tall horses with a herald before them in a red cloak, from the god of the Tallgrass, who is called Isca. The herald spoke for an hour at the Stone Row in a manner no one in the valley had heard before, and the sense of it was that the god of the Olm is invited, as a lesser fire is invited to a greater, to attend the Gathering at the Fire Hollow and to sing before the Ruun. The riders stayed three nights and sang every night, songs that were lists of their bands and camps and dead, and would not take payment for anything, and gave a horse to the headman of Tollan unasked.
- No spotted eel was taken at Dun Mere this year, for the first time anyone can remember. The plain eel are as thick as ever. The fishers say a great pike has got into the tarn and eats the spotted ones because they are slower.
- The ferryman's son made his first ford.
- The salt wind failed and the late summer was wet, and there was mould in the barley at Tollan and Weir, and the count was short.
- The Fenners took geese again in the tenth month and a Fenner girl married into Ossary, which the Fenners count a loss and Ossary a gain.
- At the count the Driving was told as always, and a new elder stood on Scarrow's stone, Garran's nephew, who is young for it. Hemma, eighty-four, said the cords this year were short, and said it to you directly, which she has not done before.
- A dim night in the first month, in the eleventh, and in the twelfth.
