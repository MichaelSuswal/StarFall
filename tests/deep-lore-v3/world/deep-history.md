# Deep history of the Olm country

Keeper material for the v3 blind test. Everything here was true before the gods arrived and is already in the ground on day one. Nothing is added later. Each site lists what remains, what fragments survive among the living, and how it might be found: HARD means it takes deliberate effort aimed at the right place, CHANCE means it surfaces only if someone happens to be there, and EASY means any god who asks will hear a version of it, wrong in an important way.

The region sits under a late star (phase roll 88, living radius 90 days). Its oldest places have faded worst. The order below runs from oldest to newest.

## 1. Ulmarrow, the city of measures (about 2,200 to 1,500 years ago)

**Who they were.** The Ulm built one city at the place where the rain of the Lamp is thickest, on the flat ground that is now the eastern shore of Hush Water. There was no lake then, only a shallow warm pool the Ulm called the Eye and did not drink from. The Ulm were measurers. They cut law into stone, counted their harvests in notched rods, and believed that a thing not measured was not owned by anyone, including the gods, of which they had none. Their surveyor Hesk set four sightline stones at the corners of their country, each a pair of uprights with a notched cross-stone, aligned on the Lamp so that any Ulm walking home could find the center by lining up the notch and the star. Hesk was a woman, which the Harl, who tell a version of this story, get wrong. She lived to be very old and could not, at the end, say which of the four stones she had set first.

**How they fell.** Slowly and first. Ulmarrow spent itself the way all the oldest places do, and its people became plain over about ten generations: the dialects went, the law stones were still read but no one remembered why the laws were as they were, and the warm pool spread a little every lifetime. The last people of Ulmarrow were visited by singers who asked for the names of things and were given them. The singers' name for the woman who gave the names is Teller. The Ulm did not leave. They became the Handfolk, who still live on that shore, and who no longer have names for each other.

**What remains.**
- The mounds on the eastern shore of Hush Water, grass over rubble, which the Handfolk will not walk on. Under the mounds, Ulm rubble. Under that, older rubble of a kind no one in the region has seen, with fitted stone and no mortar. Under that, a layer of blue-grey glass that rings when struck. HARD: requires a dig at a place the Handfolk avoid, under the eyes of the lake, 25 days from the player's valley.
- The Notch, on Hagg Ridge, two days above Greyholt. Two uprights and a notched cross-stone, used for five generations by the Harl clan of Gorm Longhand as the gatepost of a sheepfold. The notch now frames empty sky a hand above the Lamp. CHANCE or HARD: a shepherd will mention "the old gate that points at nothing" if asked about the ridge; a god must go and look, and must think to lie down behind it at night.
- The second sightline stone, under Hush Water's western shallows. Never findable in forty years without the lake's permission, which does not exist.
- The third, in the Harl moor at a place called Cold Hand, fallen and used as a clan boundary marker. CHANCE: a Harl captive or trader might describe it.
- The fourth is in the Pale and is no longer particular.
- The ell of Tollan: a bronze rod kept in the Stone Row, by which the Olm folk measure cloth and timber. It is an Ulm measure, exactly the span from the Notch's left upright to its right. No one knows this. The elders say it is "the length of Mother Tollan's arm." EASY to see, HARD to connect; only a god who has measured the Notch could.
- The Longwalker song, which contains "Ulmarrow" and "Hesk" and "Teller" and about forty other Ulm names, sung in order. CHANCE: the Longwalkers pass through once in the first decade and once in the fourth.

## 2. Caddon and the cistern town of Vell (about 900 to 550 years ago)

**Who they were.** The Caddonese came down from the north moor into the hills where the Olm rises and built Vell, a town cut into the grey rock around eleven cisterns, each a chamber hollowed from the hill and capped with a carved stone. They were the only people in the region ever to have a school of magic. The Cord House taught knotting: a cord tied in a certain way, held in a certain way, moved heat, cold, bleeding, sleep. The Cord House kept its knots as a library of cords, each with a clay tag in pictographs. At its height Vell held perhaps three thousand people and ruled the whole upper Olm.

**Imre Cordwright.** The last strong king of Vell. In a drought that lasted four years he went to the Cord House and tied a working no one had tied before, pulling the cold out of the Olm's spring flood and holding it in the hill so that the cisterns would fill from the melt all summer. It worked. The cisterns have never been empty since. The cost fell on the nearest particular things: a circle of ground a half-day across south of the town where nothing with a seed head has grown since, which the Harl call the Scald, and on Imre himself, who came out of the Cord House not knowing his name. He ruled another eleven years as a man his servants called "the king" and nothing else. He is buried under a stone that is blank, because when the cutters asked what to carve he could not tell them. The Harl call him the King Without. Over the next two centuries the Cord House produced more hollow people than knotters and Vell emptied into the valley below.

**Sele of Vell.** Imre's daughter, who left Vell with the charcoal burners when the Cord House refused to close, and settled at the foot of the hills where Greyholt stands. She carried a cistern cap from her father's town and set it as the hearth-stone of her house. It is still the hearth-stone of the headman's house in Greyholt, carved with a knot pattern the family believes is a snake.

**Marrit.** Not of the deep past but of its last thread. The knot tradition survived outside the Cord House in a thin line of hill women who taught one girl each. Marrit, born in a Ferrow Ford steading sixty-one years before the gods came, was the last taught. She lives alone on the eastern upland the Olm folk call the Backs, has worked the knots for forty years, and cannot say her own name or her mother's. She takes a lamb now and then to live. She has a clay tag on a cord at her neck with her name in Cord House pictographs, tied there by her teacher, which she cannot read. She knows the way to Vell, the Scald, and the sealed cistern. See the short mysteries.

**What remains.**
- Vell itself, four days above Greyholt by a goat path: eleven cisterns, nine open and cold and sweet, one collapsed, one sealed with a capstone carved with the Cord House's knot. In the sealed one: the cord library, cords rotted to dust, two hundred clay tags intact, and the Cord House roll, a set of thin lead sheets scratched with pictographs listing every knotter and what each "gave" (the word is the same as the one for a tithe). HARD: no one in the valley goes up there; the hills are "unlucky."
- The Scald, bare grass without seed heads, visible from the Vell path. CHANCE on the way to Vell.
- The blank grave of Imre, a flat stone in Vell's upper yard with a cut border and nothing inside it. CHANCE at Vell.
- Greyholt's hearth-stone. EASY to see; HARD to read without the tags.
- In the Olm folk's speech, the word "cord" for a promise ("he gave me his cord") and the custom of tying a knot in a child's wrist-string at naming. EASY, and no one knows why.
- Ossary, the downstream village, stands on the Caddonese cemetery of the valley settlers. Every spring flood exposes bone and the villagers rebury it, as they have "always done." Among the bones, grave goods: clay tags, a bronze knotting-needle, beads. CHANCE every flood year; EASY to hear about; HARD to connect.

## 3. Sennet, the port that lost its sea (about 700 to 340 years ago)

**Who they were.** On the eastern sea, thirty days from the Olm valley, stood Sennet, a harbor city of stone steps that went down into the water. They traded salt fish, bronze, and dye up the Greel to the Olm and beyond. Sennet kept the tide-book: bronze plates, one cast each year, recording the height of the tides at each new moon. For three hundred years the plates record tides. Then, over about eighty years, the tides on the plates grow smaller.

**Aud Tideward.** Last keeper of the tide-book. She was a young woman when the tide first failed to reach the fourth step and an old one when the water stopped moving at all. The sea did not go anywhere. It stopped being a sea: it lost its tide, its salt grew general, its fish went to a few plain kinds and then to none, and one summer it was a flat of white crust from the steps to the horizon with no wrack line and no shells, as if it had never held anything alive. Aud's last plate, cast when the city had already half-emptied, reads in the Sennet script: "No tide. No tide. No tide. I have kept the book. Whoever finds it, the sea was here." She buried the forty-two plates of her keeping under the harbor steps and walked inland with the rest. Her people became the salt caravans who still cross the flats, who tell the story that "the sea was angry and left," and who have Aud's name as a byword for stubbornness ("as Aud at the steps").

**Brann.** A Sennet trader two generations after Aud who dug up one of the plates to sell as bronze, carried it upriver as ballast in a flat boat, and drowned when the boat went over in a cut in the Merrow Fen. The plate lies in the mud of the Brann Cut, which the Fenners do not know is named for a man. See the short mysteries.

**What remains.**
- Dead Sennet on the salt flats, thirty days east: the stone steps going down into white crust, a city of roofless walls, and under the fourth step a bronze box with forty-one plates. HARD: a long dry journey with salt-caravan guides who regard the steps as unlucky.
- The Whale Walk: enormous bones on the flats a day from the steps, ribs taller than a man, that no one can match to any beast. The bone is general bone and tells nothing. CHANCE along the caravan road.
- Aud's plate in the Brann Cut, which rings under the fen in autumn fog. See the short mysteries.
- In Olm speech, "sennet" means a long wait, and Olm women say a stubborn child "has Aud in her" without knowing Aud was a person. EASY.
- Salt-caravan songs that count tides, meaningless to the singers. CHANCE at the autumn salt market in Tollan.

## 4. Thrane, the lords of the hill (about 520 to 312 years ago)

**Who they were.** A Caddonese warband that came down the Olm as Vell was emptying, took the hill above the present Scarrow, walled it, and ruled the valley as lords for two hundred years. They took a tithe of grain and eels, kept a hall whose great fire was never let out, held a law that ran to hundreds of clauses, and kept a knotter who held the hill's slope green in drought. They were the richest people the valley has known, and the only ones who ever ruled it from a wall.

**How they fell.** The Olm folk's founding story, told at every autumn count, is the Driving: that Mother Tollan rose, and the villages came up the hill with torches in one night, and the Thrane fled into the moor and never returned. The valley counts its years from it. The present is the 312th year after the Driving.

The truth is in the crypt. The Thrane spent heavily for two centuries and faded over the last three generations. Their last lord, Edric Thrane, could not name his sons. He had a tally-board of his house cut and re-cut, scraping the names off with his own knife and trying to cut them again from memory, and failing. When Tollan's elders came up the hill, led by the woman later called Mother Tollan, they found the gate open, the fire out for the first time in two hundred years, and the lords sitting in the hall not knowing why they were there. Mother Tollan walked them out to the Harl moor in a line, and they went, as plain as sheep. There was no fight. She ordered the story told as a battle, "so the children do not fear what we saw," and set it in the count. She had a line cut on the eighth stone of the Stone Row in the old Caddon pictographs, which she had learned from her grandmother: "We drove no one. They were already gone."

**The Rooks.** Edric's daughter Ysolt, who had kept her own name because she had left the hall young and married a Scarrow quarryman, is the ancestor of the Rook family of Scarrow. The Rooks keep a gold torc in a chest and have, for three centuries, said nothing about it. In Year 5 of the test, with a god now in the valley and lordship in the air, they begin to.

**What remains.**
- Lord's Hill above Scarrow: a ring of fallen wall, the hall's footprint, brambles. By custom no one digs there; the Driving story says the Thrane cursed it as they fled. HARD: digging means breaking a custom in front of Scarrow.
- The crypt under the hall floor: stone steps, a chamber, the Thrane silver (eleven torcs, a chest of Sennet bronze coin, a great dish), Edric's scraped tally-board, and the bodies of the earlier lords, who were buried with their names. HARD.
- The eighth stone of the Stone Row at Tollan, with its pictograph line, which the elders say is "Mother Tollan's mark." EASY to see; HARD to read: it needs the Vell tags, or Marrit, or a Harl elder who knows a few signs.
- The Rooks' torc. CHANCE: it comes out when the Rooks make their claim.
- The Driving itself, told at every count. EASY, and wrong about the only thing that matters.

## 5. Smaller places, older and newer

- **The Harl barrows.** On the moor north of the Caddon Hills, long mounds of the Harl clans' dead, each clan's barrow in a row, the newest end still used. The Harl came partly from Vell's emptying and partly from the Thrane walked out onto the moor, which the Harl do not know and would not like. CHANCE.
- **Cold Hand.** The fallen third sightline stone, in Harl country, a clan boundary. The Harl say a giant dropped it. CHANCE.
- **The Greelmen's drowned landing.** At the mouth of the Olm, where it meets the slow Greel, a stone jetty the boat people built four hundred years ago is now two spans under water, because the Greel has risen as it has slowed and widened. The Greelmen say the river is "getting fat." CHANCE; EASY to hear.
- **The Hagg Ridge fold.** Gorm Longhand's sheepfold, five generations old, with the Notch for its gate. The Harl who use it summer there with six hundred sheep. CHANCE.
- **The singers' last visit.** The Longwalkers passed down the Olm seventy-one years before the gods, in the memory of two living people: old Hemma of Tollan, who was eight and remembers that they "sang the villages back to us," and a Fenner grandmother who remembers they would not take fish. EASY if asked about anything strange in living memory; otherwise never.
- **The Lamp's old names.** The Olm folk say "the Lamp." The Harl say "the Low One." The Sennet caravans say "the Coin." The Ruun say "the Ember." The Skelder say "the Eye." All the names are old. Children in every people are told not to stare at it, for no reason anyone can give.

## 6. What may never be found

The Thrane silver. Aud's forty-one plates. The Cord House roll and what it says about what each knotter gave. The second sightline stone under the lake. The blue-grey glass under Ulmarrow, and set in it, intact, a single notched Ulm law stone bearing the word "Teller," which is also in the Longwalker song. The true meaning of the eighth stone. Marrit's name. Why the ell of Tollan is the length it is. None of these must be found for the test to succeed. Each is there for whoever goes.
