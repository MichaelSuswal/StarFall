# Kessvale, Years 1 to 10

What reached you, year by year. Nothing here is explained. Some of it will matter and most of it is the valley being the valley.

## Year 1

You come down in the dark on the willow walk at Dorrin, and the first thing you know is that you are not alone in this country. There is a weight to the east, far off, like someone standing behind a door. There is a weight to the south, farther, restless. And there is a third, smaller, east and a little south, that does not move at all and does not feel like it is standing.

The elders meet you at dawn on the walk. There are eleven of them and they have no king and they have been arguing about the autumn tally since before the frost. They are not surprised to see you. They are surprised you came alone.

The Kess is brown and high. The ferryman says the spring flood will be four days this year, five at most, and that the miller is a liar about the pond.

Children in Dorrin are told not to stare at the star. No one can tell you why. It is a thing that is said.

The ferry rope is new this year, and the ferryman tells you so twice.

## Year 2

The headman of Brae comes down for wool washing week and stays to eat. When asked who heals in Brae, he says Old Tamsin ties knots for fevers and bleeds and has done since his father's time, and that she is not called Old Tamsin because that is her name, but because someone had to call her something. Her apprentice is a girl named Wenna who writes things down.

Blue cranes pass north over the valley in a line that takes a whole morning. They do not land. They never land.

A boy at Hollin Ford made his first crossing alone and was given his river stone. His mother says he will be in the river every day now and she has given up.

Quarry sends word that the stone master lost two fingers in the cuts and the swallows nested in the gap in the wall before the week was out. He has seven fingers now and says that is enough for stone.

The ford froze for a month this winter. The mill pond froze for three.

Pig weighing at Dorrin. The heaviest belongs to a widow who raised it on eel heads, and the men who lost to her say that should not count.

## Year 3

The autumn count at Dorrin. Three days of it. Saltmeet's headwoman recites every tally since she was twelve and is not wrong once, and two of the Dorrin elders hate her for it.

Cold Harbour sends a fisher with the lake tally. He says the perch are fine. He says the char have not run since before you came, and the lake is not cold the way it was, and he does not know what to tell his father about it.

A dry east wind in late summer. The Threnn people call it the Harrock's breath and shut their doors against it, which does nothing.

The red deer come to Saltmeet's lick at dusk the same as always, and no one hunts them, the same as always, and a Dorrin boy who tried was walked home by three Saltmeet women and did not try again.

Twins born at Wick. The heron the Wick people call the Judge stood on the roof the whole day of the birth and the whole village has decided this means something good.

A couple from Brae walk the length of the Kess to Wick tied at the wrist, as couples do, and arrive on the sixth day, and are married. The rope is kept.

## Year 4

The Cold Harbour fisher is back for the count. He mentions, because someone asked about the Rings, that the old standing stones on the east shore were set up to look at something, two of them and a notched one, and that they do not point at anything now. He says this the way he says the perch are fine.

A trader from Threnn says the Harrock have been quiet since the stone fell. When asked which stone, he says the one that fell into Cairn Hollow the night the gods came, that was warm all that first season, and that the clans have built a cairn round it and leave it alone. He had not thought it worth telling before. Everybody east of Threnn knows it.

The autumn fog fills the valley to the hill line for nine days. From Quarry the Combs stand out like islands in milk.

The miller and the ferryman have agreed on nothing for three years and will not be seated near each other at the count.

Snow on the Tallow by the count, as always, pink at dusk from the willow walk.

A man dies in the Quarry cuts. Quarry keeps its silence day: no stone cut, no hammer, no talk above a whisper until the next dawn.

## Year 5

A wool woman from Brae, down with the spring clip, tells the story of the winter. A man went under a sledge and bled badly and they carried him to Old Tamsin, and she tied her cords and the bleeding stopped. The wool woman says the water jar by the hearth had frost on it after, in a warm room, and that for a moment while Tamsin worked there was a light in the cord the color of the star, and that the dog would not go near the man for an hour after and did not know its master. She tells it as a story about a man who lived.

The glass eels run in the Kess in late spring, clear as water, and cook white. The whole of Dorrin eats eel for a week and is sick of it by the end.

A rock cat takes lambs above Threnn. The Threnn bowman kills it. That makes four. He wants it known.

The nine walled fields at Ninefield are the subject of a dispute between two families about a wall that has stood six generations. The elders are asked to rule. The elders do not want to.

Eleven children are named at Dorrin at the third spring, as is done here, and not before. One of them has been called Frog for three years and his mother is relieved to be rid of it.

A Hollin Ford girl brings a live glass eel to the willow walk in a bucket so that you can see the stones through it.

## Year 6

A dry summer. The Harrock come down on Threnn in the night and take forty sheep and leave the bowl on the wall untouched. Threnn wants soldiers. Quarry wants a wall. Dorrin wants to know who will pay for either.

The marsh lights at Wick are bright this year, which the Wick people say is the dry weather, which it is.

A trader from the Combs road mentions in passing that the Sellen, far to the east past the Harrock, have someone leading them now who speaks in sayings and has set a cut stone at each of their camps. He did not see this person. He saw the stones.

Bees in the Quarry cuts give a honey that tastes of stone and the Quarry people will fight anyone who says it is not the best in the valley.

The hot spring above Brae ran low in the dry and the wool came out grey, and Brae blames the sky, the Harrock, and Dorrin, in that order.

The Saltmeet deer are thin and come to the lick in daylight, and still no one hunts them.

## Year 7

A man from Brae comes to Dorrin to ask what is to be done about his wife. She went west over the Tallow in Year 5 looking for her brother, who had gone that way for furs, and she came back this spring alone. She can speak. She knows him. She cannot say the name of Brae, or her mother's name, or her own, and when he says them to her she agrees they sound right. He does not know what she has done to deserve this.

The spring flood is three days and gentle. The ferryman is pleased and says so to the miller, who is not.

Wool washing week at Brae goes well. The grey of last year washes out.

The cranes go south in autumn, a line a morning long, higher than in spring.

An elder dies at Dorrin, the oldest of the eleven, and the willow walk is quiet for a month because no one can agree who sits where.

A Ninefield family that has farmed the same walled field for six generations sends a boy to Dorrin to say so, and to ask whether their apples can be counted separately at the tally, since there are three kinds and they are not the same.

## Year 8

Forty strangers come down out of the Combs road to Saltmeet, men and women, dusty, carrying nothing to trade. They ask the Saltmeet headwoman the name of her village, and then the name of every village on the Kess, and she tells them, and that night they sing on the bank until dawn. The headwoman says the songs were lists, names after names, some of which were places she had never heard of and some of which were no places at all. In the morning they walked to Dorrin, asked the same, sang the same, and went on toward Wick. Four nights in the valley and then they were gone south. They took nothing and left nothing except that everyone who heard them has the lists in their head and cannot get them out.

The Saltmeet headwoman adds that they sang Saltmeet back to her exactly, in her own saying of it, and it unsettled her more than the rest.

Ninefield at the count: the old people say the third apple has gone bland. Not blighted, not small. It tastes of apple and nothing more. The young people cannot tell the difference and the old people are angry about that too.

A hard frost in late spring kills the early beans at Wick. The Judge stands in the reeds and is no help.

The Threnn oak has a Harrock arrowhead grown into it, a hand deep, from some raid no one remembers. Children are taken to see it.

Quarry has rebuilt the stone master's wall and left the swallows' gap in it on purpose.

## Year 9

A shepherd from the high pasture above Hollin Ford comes down for the count and is made to tell his story twice. On a dim night this autumn, one of the cold hours when the star fades, he lay on his back in the grass and saw a thread of blue light let down from the star toward the northwest, thin as a hair, and it stayed until the star was bright again. He has seen it before. He says the other shepherds have seen it and that it is a thing the high pasture knows and the valley does not, because the valley is asleep at that hour.

Old Tamsin's apprentice Wenna sends a message with the Brae clip. She asks nothing. She says only that her sister keeps a list for her of things she used to know, and that the list is longer than it was, and that she thought the god might want to know that there is a list.

A Dorrin child, asked at a lesson why one does not stare at the star, says because it stares back, and the other children laugh and the teacher does not.

The Kess is green this autumn, as it is every autumn, and the willow walk is yellow and the elders meet under it and argue about a bridge.

Carp are taken from the mill pond for the longest night, and the miller swears one of them was older than he is.

Three children make the first ford at Hollin Ford this year, one of them a girl, which was argued about and then allowed, and she has her stone.

## Year 10

An envoy comes up from the south to Wick from the Ostrel, the salt people past Ninefield, speaking for the one who leads them. The envoy is loud and wants salt trade and a road and says his lord is called Hask and is a wind with a face in it and that the sea will be back within the lord's lifetime, which is forever. He laughs at the ferryman's funeral, which falls during his visit, and Wick does not forgive it.

The ferryman is dead. The miller is seen weeping at the ferry and denies it.

Ninefield herders, following strays south past the walled fields, come back talking about the bare circle beyond the last farm. Grass grows there, but every blade is the same and none of it heads to seed, and the strays would not graze it. The old Ninefield people say the Ostrel did something there once and the Ostrel will not say what.

The lantern walk on the longest night goes from Dorrin to the willow walk and back by starlight alone, as it always has, and a girl says the light is enough to read by, and an old man says it was not when he was young, and they are both right.

A fox with black legs has been seen in the Dorrin yards three mornings running and no one has lost a hen yet.

Snow on the Tallow early this year. The Brae clip is in before it, barely, and Brae wants that noticed.
