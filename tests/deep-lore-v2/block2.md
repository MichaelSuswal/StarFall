# Kessvale, Years 11 to 20

What reached you, year by year. Nothing here is explained. Some of it will matter and most of it is the valley being the valley.

## Year 11

An Ostrel trader comes up to Wick behind the salt carts and stays for the reed cutting. Asked about the plain his people cross, he says there is nothing in it. No shells, no fish bones, no line of old weed where a tide would have left one. He says the salt eats all of that in a few years, and that his grandfather saw the sea from the last channel and he has not. He says it as a man describing a road.

You go north with the autumn count still arguing behind you, and at Cold Harbour the fisher rows you across to the east shore at dusk and waits with you among the mounds for the cold hour. When the star fades he puts you at the notched stone and you sight through the gap between the two uprights. The frame holds a hand's breadth of empty sky. The star sits just under it, bright even dimmed, and the gap is empty. The fisher says the old people set the stones for the sunset, and that the sun is not where it was in their day, and that the stones have never pointed at anything in his life. From the gap, the thread when it comes is a hair let down from under the star toward the far shore and beyond it, and nothing in the frame.

Rowing back, the fisher names the shallows for you and the mounds stand thickest on the shore behind each one. He says the Rings people built where the landings were good. The weed is gone from the shallows. He says that is the warm.

Dorrin has a new ferryman, a Wick man with a loud laugh, and the miller has decided to hate him before the month is out, and says so at the count, and the count is glad of something to agree on.

The spring flood is four days. The new ferryman says five. The miller says the rope is wrong.

## Year 12

You go up to Brae for wool washing week and stay in Old Tamsin's house. On the third day a shearer opens his hand to the bone on the blade and they bring him in. Tamsin sits him at the hearth and ties her cords, three knots and a fourth, and the bleeding slows and stops. For the time of the last knot there is a blue in the cord that is the star's blue, and you feel the room lean toward it, and you feel yourself lean too. The washing tub beside the hearth, drawn that morning from the sulfur spring, has no smell afterward, and the women will not use it. The shearer is well and hungry. Tamsin says the warmth has to come from somewhere, that is all, it comes out of the room, and she has always kept the house warm for that reason. Asked her name, she looks at Wenna, and Wenna says Tamsin, and Tamsin says that is what they say.

You noticed this, sitting at her hearth, and you have turned it over since. The pull on the cord is a pull you know. You have done it, without a cord, every time you have wanted a thing in this valley and had it: the rain held off, a fever broken at Dorrin, the ferry rope that did not part. You had not watched it from the outside before. You do not know yet what it costs when you do it, but you know now that it is the same motion, and that Tamsin finds it with her hands and you find it by wanting.

Wenna reads you the list that night. Her sister wrote it and keeps it. It is in no order. The name of the stream above the house, with the sister's note beside it: the Scald. Which of the two of them was born first. How their mother says the word for bread. The smell of the tanner's yard. The second verse of the wool song. The way home from the spring in the dark. Nothing on it is a skill. She can still tie every knot Tamsin has shown her. After the shearer, before she sleeps, she sits with her sister and has her write one more line, and the line is the name of their mother's village, which she says she had this morning.

A Harrock woman who married into Threnn three winters ago tells, at the oak, what the clans do before a raid. They sleep beside the cairn in the Hollow, all of them, and they dream the same dream: a crowd kneeling in rows and a river none of them has ever seen, broad and slow, with roads along it. She says it is the dead of the stone, who want to be followed, and that the clans go out in the morning feeling followed. The Threnn people had not asked her before.

The glass eels run late and heavy. Dorrin eats eel for nine days and a Saltmeet man says they have no flavor this year and is told he is wrong by everyone.

A couple from Quarry walk the Kess tied at the wrist and arrive at Wick on the fifth day and are married. The rope is kept.

## Year 13

Two shepherds from the high pasture go northwest in late spring along the line the thread hangs on, by the shepherds' track over the shoulder of the Combs and down, piling a stone at every camp, and they come back at the end of summer with all their names. At no point in twelve days out could either of them not say the other's. On the twelfth day they came down to a lake smaller than Lake Hollow and round as a plate, with the star straight overhead, so that a stick stood upright had no shadow at noon and none at night either. The wind blew their hair and did not move the water. One of them put his hand in to the wrist and it was not cold. There are people on the shore, three hundred or so, who fed them and gave them a roof. These people have a word for every grass the shepherds described and every fish in the Kess the shepherds could name, and asked for more words and kept them. They have no word for any of each other. They point. Told the shepherds' names, they nodded and pointed. Asked what the star is called, they pointed up. They do not fish the lake and would not let the shepherds wade past the knee. The shepherds say it is a taboo, the kind hill people have.

The shepherds waited there for a cold hour, which came on the sixth night. The thread came down from the star thin as a hair and touched the middle of the water, and there was no steam and no sound, and the water did not move, and it stayed until the star was bright again. Both of them, every night they slept on that shore, dreamed the same thing and said so to each other at dawn before either had told it: tall figures with no faces, standing in water on long legs, counting. They did not like it and they came home. They have left a cairn every day's walk from the high pasture to the shore, and anyone can follow it.

Ninefield hunters, after strays onto the white plain, come up on one of the pale beasts that walk there all one color and kill it with two arrows. They cut it open to see what it ate. There was nothing inside it that was a thing, they say: no proper heart they could find, no gut full of grass, only meat the same all the way through, like a loaf. It had no smell alive or dead and left nothing on the ground where it had stood. They say it was sick and they left it for the birds, and the birds did not come.

A rock cat screams for a week above Quarry and takes no lambs and is not seen. The Threnn bowman goes to look and finds nothing and is annoyed.

The willow walk votes on the bridge and votes against it and will vote again next year.

## Year 14

You go east through Threnn and into the Combs with two Threnn men who turn back at the second night, as you told them to, and you go on alone through Harrock country. The clans see you and do not come near. The third weight grows as you walk, east and a little south, exactly where you have felt it since the first night, and at Cairn Hollow it is under your hand. The stone is blue-grey and the size of a cart and warm the way a hearthstone is warm hours after the fire, and heavy to look at. You sleep beside it, because that is what is done there, and you dream the crowd kneeling in rows and the river with roads along it, and you know the river. You have seen it before, behind your own eyes, in the fog where whatever you were before this valley has gone. It is that river. You wake with your hand still on the stone and the weight is the same as it was and has not moved.

The Harrock watched you sleep from the ridge, a whole clan of them. In the morning an old man came down alone and asked whether you would take the Lamp away, and you had not said anything about that, and he went back up without an answer and the clan was gone by noon. On your way home the bowl on the Threnn wall had a Harrock knife in it, hilt toward the village, and no sheep were missing.

Quarry falls out with Dorrin over the tithe at the count, loudly, and the stone carts still come and the honey still comes, and Quarry has stopped saying your name at its table and at its cuts. You know this because you are lighter. It is not grief and it is not anger. It is a step down you did not take, and it stays stepped down all year, and the stone carts keep coming.

Twins at Wick, the second pair in a decade, and the Judge not on the roof this time, and Wick has decided this means nothing in particular.

A hard frost takes the early beans at Wick, as it did six years ago, and the Wick people say the frosts are coming later than they did.

## Year 15

The Saltmeet headwoman goes down past Ninefield with the old man who can name the apples, and a Ninefield boy to fetch them if needed, and stands at the edge of the bare circle. It is round, a long bowshot across, and the edge is as sharp as a wall: seeded grass on one side and seedless on the other, and not a blade leaning over. She recites the tally of Year 3 outside, then walks in to the middle and recites it, then comes out and recites it, and the old man follows the count on his fingers all three times and she is not wrong once. Nothing in her slipped. The old man names the three apples inside and out. What they both say is that nothing lives there: no beetle, no bird came down the whole hour, no mouse run in the grass. The grass is alive and the same. The boy threw a stone in and it lay there. She says it is a burn, an old one, and that the Ostrel salted it afterward out of spite.

Ninefield at the count: one apple strain. The second went like the third, the old people say, bland first and then the young could not tell and then it was not worth the grafting. The young people say an apple is an apple. The family on the six-generation field has stopped asking for separate tallies.

Quarry comes to the count with honey and a stone master who will not meet your eye, and the dispute is settled by the elders giving Quarry nothing it asked for, and Quarry starts saying your name again, and you feel it the week it happens.

A Harrock man, grey, unarmed, with two boys carrying a lamb between them, comes to the Threnn oak and asks to speak to the god's people. He says the clans will keep the bowl filled and take no sheep from Threnn or Quarry so long as the god keeps its hand off the Lamp. He does not say what he means by hand. He will not come inside the village. He waits three days under the oak for an answer from Dorrin and when none comes he leaves the lamb and goes.

Snow on the Tallow by the count. The Brae clip is in early.

The fox with black legs takes a hen at last, after five years of looking, and Dorrin is almost relieved.

## Year 16

The mill pond freezes in the first month of winter and does not open for three. The ford freezes and children walk the Hollin. Two Cold Harbour boats are crushed in the ice at the harbour, which the oldest fisher says has never happened, because the lake used to freeze slow and from the edges and this year it froze all at once and shallow.

The Dorrin elder you sent south in the spring comes back before the pond freezes, thinner, with salt and an answer. He stood thirty-eight days on the plain and found the Ostrel at the far edge among brine channels, fewer than the envoy said and leaving as he watched, a family a week going west along the channels toward nothing in particular. The lord Hask came to him on the second night as a standing wind off the salt, wet on the face, with a face in it, and laughed before the elder had finished the greeting. To the first question Hask said the circle was there before him. The Ostrel knotters made it in his grandfather's time, meaning theirs, by singing too hard to a dry year, and the sea took the grass in exchange for rain, and that is how the sea is, and the sea is coming back anyway. To the second question Hask said the singers were not his, that they came through in the eighth year and sang his people's names back to them and his own name too, in a way he did not like, and that he asked them to stay the night awake with him and they would not, and they went out onto the salt where there is nothing and did not come back, and he laughed at that for a long time. Then he said the god of the Kess should come south itself, and bring the woman who ties cords and anyone she has taught, because in a few summers he will call the sea back up the channels and there will be enough for everyone who helped. He kept the elder up until dawn every night of the visit. The elder says the lord cannot sleep.

The elder says one thing more, which he saw and was not asked about. An Ostrel woman tied a knot on the bank of the last channel to bring the brine up to a rake pan, and the brine came, and afterward she stood among four children at her feet and did not know which was hers, and the children knew, and took her home.

Twins at Wick for the third time, which Wick now says is the water.

The cranes go north late, after the ice, in a line that takes a morning.

## Year 17

Wool washing week at Brae. The sulfur spring runs full and the wool comes out white. Old Tamsin has stopped going out of the house and the shearers come to her. Wenna has a girl of her own learning the cords now, the shearer's daughter, and sends word of it with the clip and asks whether that is permitted, and does not wait for an answer before starting.

The shepherds' cairns are followed by a Hollin Ford man who wanted to see, and he comes back in the autumn with all his names and a word from the shore people for the kind of grass that grows on the high pasture, which the high pasture never had a word for and now uses.

The Harrock take the bowl from the Threnn wall and leave a horn cup in it. No sheep are missing. The grey man does not come back to the oak.

The miller dies in his sleep and is buried beside the ferryman, which the ferryman's widow allowed and the miller's sons did not want, and the mill goes to a cousin, and the carp are not disturbed.

The willow walk votes for the bridge. There is no money. The vote stands.

## Year 18

A rider comes in from the Combs road to Dorrin, a Sellen man with a hooded face and a horse that has walked a long way, and he waits on the willow walk for the elders without sitting. He says he comes from the one who leads the Sellen. He gives you a stone the size of a loaf with a single line cut across it, straight and shallow, and nothing else on it. He says one thing: a full cup is carried slowly. Asked what it means, he says that is what he was given to say. Asked what the one who leads them is called, he says Oldmark, and that Oldmark counts everything and keeps every bargain to the letter and advises others to do the same. He eats nothing under a roof and sleeps in the yard and is gone at first light.

The Brae woman who came back from the west in Year 7 has learned her husband's name again, by use, the way one learns a stranger's, and uses it. She has not learned her own. She asked him this spring whether she had always lived at Brae and he said yes and she said that sounded right.

A boy makes the first ford at Hollin Ford on the day of the spring flood and is pulled out at the bend below and is given his stone anyway, and the elders say that is the last time.

Red deer at the Saltmeet lick, thin, in daylight. A dry summer. Threnn shuts its doors against the east wind.

Pig weighing at Dorrin. The widow with the eel-head pig has died and her daughter wins with the same method and the same complaints.

## Year 19

A second envoy from the Ostrel comes to Wick, louder than the first, with a drum, and says the lord Hask will call the sea back up the channels in three summers and that the god of the Kess has been asked once already and is asked again, and that any knotter who comes south will be fed for life on fish. He says the lord does not understand why anyone would wait. He does not laugh at anything this time, and Wick notices that he is trying not to.

Cold Harbour at the count: the perch are fine. The fisher says the lake is warm enough now that his boys swim in it in the first month of spring, which he never did, and that there is a kind of weed gone from the shallows that his father cut for the pigs and he cannot remember the name of it.

A Dorrin child, asked at a lesson why one does not stare at the star, says because it stares back, and the teacher says that is what they used to say and tells the children to stop saying it.

Nine children named at Dorrin at the third spring. One of them is named for the old ferryman, by the miller's cousin, and nobody says anything.

The autumn fog fills the valley to the hill line for twelve days, the longest the Saltmeet headwoman has in her tally.

## Year 20

The Harrock still sleep beside the cairn before they ride, the Threnn woman says, and dream the river, and in a dry summer they rode against the Sellen's outlying camps and not against Threnn, and came back with fewer horses than they left with. The bowl on the wall is kept filled from both sides.

The third weight, east and a little south, has not moved since the night you came. You know it now the way you know a stone in your own yard. The weights east and south are heavier than they were. The one to the south is heavier by a good deal, and restless in a new way, as if it were pacing.

Ninefield has one apple and a dispute about a wall and six generations on one field, and the boy who used to come to the count about the apples is a man now and comes about the wall.

The Hollin Ford shepherds say there have been more cold hours this year than they are used to, and that the thread comes down on every one of them, and that it is a thing the high pasture knows.

The lantern walk on the longest night goes from Dorrin to the willow walk and back by starlight alone, and the girl who said the light was enough to read by is a woman and says it again, and the old man who said it was not is dead, and nobody argues with her.

The Kess is green in autumn and the willow walk is yellow and the elders meet under it, eleven of them again, and argue about the bridge.
