# Kessvale, Years 21 to 30

What reached you, year by year. Nothing here is explained. Some of it will matter and most of it is the valley being the valley.

## Year 21

You stop. From the first thaw you hold off nothing and break nothing and keep nothing whole, and the valley does not know it, and goes on asking. The spring flood runs six days, the longest in the Saltmeet headwoman's tally, and takes the ferry rope, and the new ferryman swims for it and lives and is laughed at. A fever goes through Brae in the wet after and takes two old women and a baby, and Brae says the god is busy with the Harrock. The headwoman has her tally stick and a second one, and she asks you what counts as a thing lost and you tell her, and she says she will count what she is told to count and nothing else.

You go up to Brae for the clip and sit with Old Tamsin, Wenna and the shearer's girl, whose name is Hesk, and you tell them: the warmth comes out of the room, and the names come out of the one who ties, and the lord of the salt people has asked for them by name to help him call back a sea. Tamsin says she knows what it costs, everyone old forgets, that is what old is. Wenna says she knows, and that is what the list is for, and asks nothing. Hesk wants to go south and see a sea, and her father says she will not, and she is fifteen and that is the end of it for now. Wenna agrees to the cold-room working and says it will have to wait for a winter sickness, since she will not tie for nothing.

The Dorrin elder goes south again in the spring with salt and no knotters and your message. He comes back before the count. He says the Ostrel are a thousand and six hundred by his count of hearths, and the channels are drier, and the lord Hask met him on the salt the first night and laughed at the refusal before it was finished, and said he would do it alone and had never needed anyone, and that he was sorry the god of the Kess was afraid. Asked what he had spent and what he had left, Hask said he had spent eleven years and two envoys and his patience, and had the sea left, which is everything. The elder watched three nights. The lord did not sleep and did not let the Ostrel knotters sleep either; they sang on the bank until the stars went grey. The elder says the lord is thinner, if a wind can be thinner.

The Judge, the great grey heron at Wick, is found dead in the reeds, old, and Wick buries it under the willow at the landing and will not say it did.

## Year 22

In midwinter a Cold Harbour boy is carried to Brae with a fever that will not break, and Wenna takes him into the empty stone byre above the house, swept, no straw, no fire, no dog, no water jar, nothing in it but the boy on a stone bench and Wenna and you. She ties. The blue in the cord is longer than at the hearth and you lean and the byre does not; the stone is stone. The fever breaks. The boy sleeps a day and a night and when he wakes he does not know his name until evening, and then he does, and he is well, and his mother says he was asleep, that is all. Wenna goes down to the house and sits with her sister and asks her to write a line, and cannot tell her sister what to write, because the thing that has gone is what her sister is called. The sister writes it herself. Wenna says she would do it again and does not say it lightly.

Refugees come up the salt road to Wick in the spring, forty, then two hundred, Ostrel. The lord called the sea in the autumn, they say. The knotters stood in a ring on the plain and sang through the night and the lord stood in the middle of them, and a wall of wet wind rose off the salt and stood from the ground to the star and did not fall until dawn, and the brine rose in every channel, and in the morning the brine was where it had been and the wind was rain and the rain was fresh. Where the ring stood there is a circle a day's walk across in which the grass is all one grass and the salt is all one salt and no bird comes down. A hundred people from the ring and the camp nearest it walk and eat and do not answer to anything and do not know their children, and their children lead them. The refugees say the sea came and was taken back by something that did not want it. They say the lord is greater than he was and that everyone who stayed is praying.

The weight to the south has been heavy since the autumn, heavier than the one to the east, and it does not pace now. It stands.

Cold Harbour at the count: the perch are fine. The ice went off the lake in a day this spring.

The willow walk votes for the bridge and finds the money and the miller's cousin is to build it and the ferryman says it will fall.

## Year 23

The headwoman brings her two sticks to the willow walk and reads them. In the two years the god did nothing, lost: the second verse of the Brae wool song, which no one in Brae can now give her, though all of them can give the first and third. The weed the Cold Harbour pigs ate, gone from the shallows, its name gone with it. The Saltmeet eels' flavor, which she tastes herself and marks. The name of a Quarry cut that the stone master's boy calls by the master's name instead. Six children's sayings from the Dorrin lessons that the children no longer say. Kept: one apple, the first ford, the deer at the lick, the whole tally of Year 3. She does not say what it means. She says she will count two more years and that the god should now go back to whatever the god does.

You go back. You hold off the second flood at Hollin Ford and the children cross dry, and you break a fever at Wick, and the Brae clip comes in under a held sky. The valley thanks you for the rain the way it thanks the rain.

The Hollin Ford man who walked the cairns goes to the round lake again in the summer with a list the shepherds made, a word for each shore person they could describe, and teaches the words the way the shore people take words, by pointing and saying. They take them. By the time he leaves they say the words well, and every word is on the hut where that person sleeps, and not on the person, and when he says a word while looking at a person the person looks at the water. Every person, every time. He asked them, with signs and the words he had, what they dream when the thread does not come down, and they said they dream the tall ones every night, and that they did not know the thread had nights it did not come, and pointed at the water. He slept there four nights and dreamed the counting four nights. He says they are a kind people and that the words will not take because no one wants them, and he brought back three more of theirs for things.

Cranes north in a line a morning long.

A rock cat takes a Hollin Ford dog off the high pasture and the shepherds want the Threnn bowman, who is sixty and says his eyes have gone.

## Year 24

Cold Harbour at the count: the perch are fine, the fisher says, and then says that they are all of a size now, every one the length of a man's forearm, no big ones, no small ones, and that the boys stopped keeping a tally of the biggest because there is none. He says it is the warm.

The Hollin's lowest bend, where it comes down to the Kess below the ford, is wider this year and slower and the deep pool under the bank has filled in, and the boy who could not be kept out of the river, who is a man now, says the river has gone lazy and he does not know why he minds.

Ostrel refugees are settled at Wick and Ninefield and are good salt rakers and bad farmers and will not go south again. One of them is a knotter, a woman, who ties for nothing and will not tie for anyone and sits by the Kess and watches it.

The bridge stands. The ferryman uses it.

Pig weighing at Dorrin. A refugee wins and no one knows what to say.

## Year 25

The headwoman reads the second two sticks. In the two years the god went back to wanting, lost: the third verse of the wool song. The name of the pool under the Hollin bank. The kind of honey the Quarry bees made from the cut-side flowers, which the Quarry people say is the same honey and the headwoman says is not. Four children's sayings. The Saltmeet eels no worse than before, she thinks. Kept: one apple, the first ford, the deer, the tally. She lays the four sticks side by side and says the count is the same in both halves, within a thing or two, and that what the god did or did not do made no difference to it that she can find, and that it is the warm years, which everyone knows. She says this without pleasure or blame and asks whether she may stop counting now.

A dry summer and the east wind. The Harrock do not come down on Threnn or Quarry. The Threnn bowl is taken and a horse's tail is left in it, braided. The Harrock woman at Threnn says the clans rode east again against the Sellen and that the Sellen god's camps do not fight back and do not lose anything either, and that the clans do not understand it and are angry.

The headwoman goes east on the Combs road in the autumn with two Threnn men and the one-line stone and a second stone with your question cut on it, and she will be back when she is back.

A couple from Wick walk the Kess tied at the wrist, upriver, which is backward, and arrive at Brae on the seventh day, and Brae says it does not count and marries them anyway.

## Year 26

The headwoman comes back in the spring with both stones. She was met at the Sellen meeting ground by an old man who did not give a name and did not sit in any tent and did not let her into one, and who asked her, before anything else, to recite the tally from Year 3 to now, and listened to all three days of it and said at the end that she had left out nothing, and that he had counted. He took the one-line stone and gave it back and said a gift given is given. He took the question stone and read it and set it in the grass between them and said: a cup pays nothing. The one who carries it pays, with the hand. She asked to whom, as you told her, and he said: ask the ground; it keeps a better tally than I do. Then he said: a stone set down is still counted. He said nothing else to the question and she did not ask again. At every Sellen camp she passed there is a cut stone with one line, and the Sellen put a hand on it when they swear, and will not break what is sworn there, and a Sellen woman told her the stone remembers the oath for the god so that the god does not have to carry it. The Sellen have Harrock horses. The headwoman had both stones in her pack and his words in her head for twenty-two days and said none of them until she stood on the willow walk.

Through the Threnn bowl you tell the grey man that you are coming to the Hollow and will not take the Lamp, and the knife is not returned, and you go. The clans are on the ridge again, more of them. You sleep with your hand on the stone and go into the dream looking for a name and there is no name in it. The crowd kneels in rows along the river with the roads and says one word, over and over, and it is not a name, it is the word you say to the one you are following, and you have heard it before, in the fog, said to you. You wake and say it aloud once at the stone. The stone is as warm as it was. Nothing wakes. The weight is what it was. The grey man comes down at dawn and says, so you will not take it, and you say you will not, and he says the clans dreamed the river last night without having meant to, all of them, and goes back up.

The willow walk, eleven elders, two of them refugees' children by marriage already, argue about whether the bridge needs a toll.

## Year 27

Wool washing week at Brae. Old Tamsin does not come to the door. Wenna ties for the whole village now and Hesk ties with her, and the sister keeps two lists, one for each of them, and the sister has given herself a name on paper so that Wenna can read it.

The Ostrel knotter at Wick who ties for no one is asked by a Wick mother to stop a bleed and does not, and the child dies, and Wick does not forgive her and she does not leave.

A Hollin Ford girl makes the first ford and is given her stone, and her grandmother says the ford is easier than it was and the girl says that is not her fault.

A trader from the salt road says the Ostrel are praying day and night and the lord Hask stands among them and does not go away and does not sleep, and that the lord's face in the wind is easier to see than it was.

Snow on the Tallow late, after the count, which the headwoman marks.

## Year 28

Reports come up to Wick within a season from salt traders, and the refugees settled there believe them. On a cold hour in the autumn the lord Hask, standing among the praying Ostrel, rose a hand's breadth off the salt and stood there in the air for the length of a breath and came down, and the wind of him went still for the first time anyone remembers, and then he went among his knotters and his loud men and laid hands on them and told them to carry what he gave them, and has been doing so since. The traders say the star struck him for his pride. The refugees say he was blessed and refused it. A salt woman says he was afraid, and that she had not known a wind could be.

You felt it from the willow walk. The weight to the south, which has stood heavy since the sea, went light for a moment, as if lifted, and came down, and has been lighter since, a little every month, as if being poured.

Twins at Wick, a fourth pair. Wick blames the water and keeps drinking it.

The bees in the Quarry cuts are fewer and the honey is the same, Quarry says, and the headwoman says nothing and marks it.

The Threnn bowman dies in his bed with four rock cats to his name and is buried under the oak with the arrowhead in it, and Threnn carves four cats on the stone.

## Year 29

An Ostrel envoy comes to Wick with no drum and no laugh and asks, quietly, for a Wick man to carry a message to Dorrin: the lord Hask asks the god of the Kess to come south, or send one who can listen, because a thing happened to the lord that the lord wishes to tell a god and not a man. The envoy says the lord is well and the Ostrel are well and the brine is up in two channels. The envoy does not stay the night.

The Brae woman who came back from the west dies, and her husband buries her under a stone with his name on it and a space, and says he did not know what to cut.

The refugees at Ninefield have planted the one apple in the walled field that was the Ostrel's by grant and the six-generation family has sued over the wall, as it does.

The autumn fog fills the valley for eight days and the headwoman marks it.

A Dorrin child, asked at a lesson why one does not stare at the star, says because the teacher said not to, and the teacher is satisfied.

## Year 30

Men who walk the cairn road, and there are a few every summer now, come back in the autumn with the same story. The shore people at the round lake have begun going into the water at night. Not in a crowd. One, and then after some nights another. They walk in to the chest in the dark and then they are not there, and the water does not move, and in the morning the rest point at the water and go on, and the huts with the Hollin Ford words on them are being emptied one at a time. The walkers say it is a religion and the shore people are feeding their lake. They say it is a kind people's custom and they would not stop it.

A Cold Harbour fisher, a quiet man who had walked the cairns twice before, walks them again alone in late summer, and the next man on the road finds his coat on the shore folded, and the shore people point at the water, and the fisher's wife says he was never happy in a boat.

The Hollin's lowest bend is a marsh now with a channel through it, and the bridge holds.

The lantern walk on the longest night, Dorrin to the willow walk and back, by starlight, and the headwoman walks it at the back, counting.

Nine children named at the third spring, one of them named for the Judge, which Wick asked for and Dorrin allowed.
