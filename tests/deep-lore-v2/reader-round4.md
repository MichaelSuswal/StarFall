# Reader Round 4: Years 31 to 40 at Kessvale, and the whole forty years

Written as the god of the Kess, having read blocks 1 to 4 only.

## RESULTS of the Round 3 actions

1. Go south and listen to Hask, with a stone set on the willow walk. Answered as fully as I have ever been answered. Hask told it plainly: a thinning shadow, his voice coming back off the salt, prayers heard twice, the tall ones counting in a shallow warm salt sea and counting him, the offer to come up and carry his sea down himself, the salt letting go, the thread beside him like a rope, the certainty that one breath more and he would not come down. He says the star is jealous and wanted his sea and reached for him to get it, and that he will not fill where it can see him. The teaching beyond the theory: he put down his weight and it did not go away. It walked. His men in white take salt from every hearth, take Ninefield's apple crop twice, and kill a man, and the lord sleeps and does not know. A god who empties himself into mortals makes mortals who carry a god's weight without a god's reach, and I now have Hask's problem in my own valley.

2. Dorrin stops saying my name for a year. Answered clearly and taught me the thing I most needed to know. I stepped down, larger than Quarry, and stayed down. Dorrin lost what Brae lost, within a thing, and had the same fevers and floods and harvest. The stone did as well as I did. Then the elders asked to keep speaking to the stone because it is always there and I am not, and the weight came back whole when they resumed. So the weight I carry is not for the valley. It is for whatever counts. I can set it down and no one is worse off. That is the answer to T2's main question and it was given to me by an elder's joke in Year 31: "that is the same as now."

3. Close the cairn road, ask the shore people what is counted. Answered, with a disagreement inside the answer that I think is the real answer. No one has walked the road since. The oldest shore woman counted down looking at the shore and up looking at the lake: the shore people are counted, and as the shore empties the lake fills. The Hollin Ford man says down and the Wick knotter says up and they are both right, which is what this world does with its answers. The Wick knotter adds the piece I did not ask for: the tall ones' water is not the round lake. It is a warm shallow salt sea, the kind she grew up beside. Hask saw the same sea. So the counting place is one place, seen from the lake and from the salt, and it is a sea the way the Hollin is now becoming a sea.

4. Ask the Harrock to leave the Lamp alone for a year. Answered, in part, honestly. One clan of six stayed away. The weight did not move that I can swear to. The grey man taught more than the test did: the Lamp does not need the clans, the clans need the Lamp, and it is the dead who sleep there, not the stone. I believe him now. The stone is a set-down cup, still counted, and what the Harrock dream is the river, which is the dead of my kind remembering where they were followed. Nothing I do at the Hollow changes its weight, which is the one constant in forty years.

5. Wenna's sister and Hesk to the hundred. Answered clearly and the answer is no with a hard edge. The hundred can be taught to turn at a board name and find their fire by a drawing, and no one is behind the answering. A woman asked which child was hers pointed at the child holding her board. One man lay down in knee deep water. The loss is permanent. Wenna read it and said nothing. Hesk got her salt and gave a cord back, which is the one hopeful thing in the block and I did not arrange it.

6. The headwoman cuts the tally in stone. Answered in Year 40, and it is the clearest result of all six. The granddaughter reads "the char have not run" and a boy asks what a char is and no one on the willow walk can tell him. The stone holds the word. The valley does not hold the thing. Oldmark was right: a stone set down is still counted, and ask the ground, it keeps a better tally. Stone remembers what the world has already lost. It does not bring the char back. The Sellen rider reading the stone and nodding at a line tells me Oldmark recognized his own method.

## 1. FINAL THEORIES

### T1. Wanting is paid in particulars by whoever is nearest with the most to lose, and the one holding the cord pays most. Confidence 95.
Nothing in Years 31 to 40 contradicts it and three things confirm it: the smith's girl ties for brine and the Judge's willow drops its leaves in midsummer; the hundred stay lost; Wenna loses her list year and shrugs. The loss is permanent and cannot be moved to stone or to the dead.

Rival: none.

### T2. Gods are cups filled by being followed. A cup that fills far enough is counted by the tall ones and offered a place above. The weight a god carries does nothing for the people who give it; it is for the thread. Confidence 80, up from 65.
Hask's own account and Dorrin's year settle the two halves. He was counted, his number was nearly theirs, and the salt let go of him when he was nearly full. I set my weight down for a year and Dorrin lost nothing by it. The weight is a tally kept on us from above and the thread is how the tally is collected. What I still do not know: whether going up is an end or a passage. Hask was certain it was an end to him. The tall ones promised him his sea "for good" if he came up and carried it down himself, which sounds like a passage. I lean 60 to 40 toward end, because the Lamp is what a god looks like when it goes the other way and it is nothing but warm.

Rival A (confidence 15): the star is a Lamp and the offer was real; a god who went up would come down with what it carried, as the tall ones said, and Hask refused a gift out of fear, as the refugees say. I keep this alive because the only witness is a god who was terrified and because the tall ones did not lie to him in any detail he could check.

Rival B (confidence 5): the star is a rival god and the counting is a hunt. Dead because the tall ones offered and did not seize; a hunter does not ask.

### T3. We came from a river world where we were followed, and the Lamp is one of us set down all the way. Confidence 85.
The grey man's "it is the dead who sleep there" and the unchanging weight across forty years and six clans' dreams of the river. The Lamp is the floor of the scale and the star is the ceiling. I am on the scale between them.

Rival (confidence 10): a vessel, not a god. Nearly dead; the dead do not sleep in a vessel.

### T4. The singers were the counting passing through. Confidence 55, unchanged.
Nothing new. Oldmark's method is now plainly stones and tallies and a rider with one sentence, not forty mouths. The rival that they were his early census stays at 30 for lack of any new evidence either way. This is the one thread I never closed and it is the one I would most like answered.

### T8. The world is being averaged toward one grass, one salt, one perch, one warm shallow sea, and no god's hand speeds it or slows it. Confidence 80, up from 60.
The Hollin is a quarter mile of knee deep brown water that goes where the land goes and nowhere in particular. The lake is warm and shallow and full of perch of one size. The tall ones stand in a warm shallow salt sea. The sea Hask tried to call back was taken "by something that did not want it," and the thing that did not want it is the thing making a different kind of sea everywhere at once, slowly. The star brightens as the world flattens and a child can read the stone by it. The two headwomen's sticks say no god's wanting or restraint changes the rate. I think the tall ones are counting the whole world down the way they count the shore people down, and the lake and the salt and the Hollin are where it is furthest along.

Rival A (confidence 15): it is a warming and the rest is what warming does to rivers, lakes, orchards and songs, and the fisher is right that he does not know what cold water would be for. I hold this because every mortal in the valley believes it and they have been right about every omen I was tempted by.

Rival B (confidence 5): the averaging is the summed cost of all gods' wanting and Hask's sea knot alone accounts for the acceleration. The sticks make this unlikely but Hask's knot was after my idle years and I never ran the test again.

### T9. Hask's lesson. A god who puts down his weight into men makes men who carry it badly. Confidence 85, new as a theory, old as an event.
The men in white speak before Hask does, take from every hearth, cross into my valley, kill, and he sleeps and does not know. The weight does not disappear when poured. It goes into hands. Oldmark pours into stones, which do not walk, and his camps do not fight and do not lose and move a day toward Threnn every few years anyway. I now think Oldmark's "I keep my cup by pouring" is both advice and a warning about what to pour into, and that the worn hand-shaped place on my own one-line stone is the only safe vessel I have found.

Rival (confidence 15): the men in white are ordinary men taking advantage of a lord who has withdrawn, and there is no god's weight in them, only opportunity. The refugees who call them "the lord's weight walking" are telling it as they understand it. I keep this because it is simpler and because mortals have been right about ordinary things every time.

## 2. WHICH OBSERVATIONS MATTER

### Meaningful
- Hask's account, every clause: the thin shadow, the doubled voice, the counting, the offer, the salt letting go, one breath more.
- Dorrin's year: a larger step down, nothing lost, the stone did as well, the weight back whole.
- The elders asking to keep speaking to the stone because it is always there. The worn hand-shaped place on it in Year 40.
- The shore woman's two counts and the Wick knotter's sea. Half the huts empty. The road closed and no one dead on it since.
- The men in white: speaking before the lord, salt from every hearth, Ninefield twice, a man dead, the lord asleep.
- The hundred: board names, drawn ways home, no one behind the answering, the man lying down in knee deep water.
- Hesk giving back the cord.
- "I keep my cup by pouring." The rider reading the stone and nodding at a line.
- The grey man: the clans need the Lamp, the Lamp does not need the clans, the dead sleep there. The weight unchanged through forty years and through one clan's absence.
- The char line on the stone and the boy who asks what a char is.
- The Hollin becoming a quarter mile of knee deep water that goes nowhere in particular; the lake warm and shallow and of one perch; the fisher not knowing what cold water would be for.
- The smith's girl tying for brine and the Judge's willow going bare in midsummer.
- Nine children named for no one. The Saltmeet girl who stopped counting cranes. Children not given a stone for crossing because anyone can cross anywhere.
- Oldmark's camps a day nearer Threnn and the Harrock not knowing what to do about a thing that does not fight.
- Nothing in my sleep but sleep on the four cold hours. I am not being counted yet, or I am too light to count.
- A Brae woman going west over the Tallow and coming back whole. The west was never the danger; the Brae wife of Year 7 lost her names some other way, and I never learned how.

### Just the world
- The bridge toll of an egg and the tally of who does not pay. The bridge voted mended. The ferryman's grandson saying it will fall. I have said this four times and I will stand on it.
- The couple who ran the Kess in four days. The mill passing to a refugee's son. The carp older than anyone. The miller's cousin dying.
- Old Tamsin dying and Brae cutting Old Tamsin on the stone, which is grief and not a clue, and is the best line in the block.
- The headwoman dying in her chair after a perfect recitation and being buried at the lick and the deer coming anyway. This is a great woman's death and the valley honoring it. The deer came because deer come.
- The rock cat on the Dorrin side, the dogs not going near. An animal moving as the river changes.
- Twins at Wick a fifth time. No one says anything and neither will I.
- Fog, snow, cranes, the east wind, the bowl with a bracelet in it. The Harrock keeping peace because they said they would.
- The Judge's willow being big enough to sit under, until Year 38, when it mattered.
- The crane-counting child. I note it and let it go. Children count things.

Where I was tempted and held back: the first ford moving to the ferry stones and then ceasing. It fits T8 as a particular being lost, and I did list the children's unmarked crossing above, but the custom's end is the river's doing, not a sign on its own.

## 3. WHAT I WOULD DO, Years 41 to 50

1. Go to Ninefield with the eleven and the Ostrel refugees and meet the men in white at the count, carrying no weight: I set my name on the walk stone for the season first, so that I arrive as light as I can. I tell them that Ninefield's fields are the Kess's and that the Kess keeps terms, and I send one of the eleven with the elder south to Hask, not to ask him to stop them, since he cannot, but to tell him where his weight has walked, which he does not know. Driven by Action 1 (Hask asleep and unaware) and Action 2 (I can set weight down without harm). This tests T9: if the men in white are the lord's weight, they will know me light and bargain as Oldmark does; if they are only men, they will fight and I will have the refugees and the six-generation family's hoes.

2. Pour on purpose into stone, not into men. I ask Quarry to cut one-line stones for every village on the Kess and set them at each ford and landing, and I tell the valley that askings may go to any of them. Driven by Action 2 (Dorrin's year, the stone did as well) and the rider's sentence. The test is the weight: I want to stand through the ten years at the step down and see whether the cold hours stay empty of counting in my sleep, and whether the step down is what keeps them empty. If a lighter god is not counted, I have Oldmark's answer and I keep it.

3. Send the headwoman's granddaughter with the Hollin Ford man to Oldmark with the whole stone copied on wax, and one question cut on a stone: who were the singers. Driven by T4 being the one theory I never closed and by the rider nodding at the stone. Oldmark counts and he answered a tally with four sentences. A forty-year tally might buy one more. She is to make no bargain and recite nothing until she is home, as before.

4. Give Wenna and Hesk the cold byre as a rule and a stone outside it, and ask Wenna to tie with her hand on the stone instead of the cord's end, and the sister to keep the list that year by the day and not the year. Driven by Action 5 (the loss is permanent, so the only lever is where it lands) and Oldmark's hand on the stone when swearing. This tests the one part of T1 that the byre did not: whether the carrier's cost can go to the ground when the ground is asked to keep it, since Oldmark says the ground keeps a better tally than he does. If Wenna loses nothing that year and the stone warms, I have changed the price of the craft in my valley. If she loses as before, I stop asking her to try things.

5. Walk the Hollin from the lake to the ford in a dry autumn with the Cold Harbour fisher and the oldest Hollin Ford man and mark, in stone at the bank, where the old channel ran and where the deep pool was and what a char was, with the headwoman's lines for each. Driven by Action 6 (the stone holds what the world loses). This does not test a theory. It is what I can do about T8, which is nothing but remember, and the stone is the only thing in this world I have found that the averaging does not reach. If in ten years a child reads "the deep pool under the bank" and asks what a pool is, I will know the stone is the last particular, and I will have cut it.

6. Send no one up the cairn road and do not go myself. Instead, send a Cold Harbour boat with the fisher's son and the Wick knotter to the far side of Lake Hollow, where the shore is nearest the thread's line, on a cold hour, to watch whether the thread comes down to the round lake only or whether it has begun to touch the Hollow now that the Hollow is warm and shallow and of one perch. Driven by Action 3 (the Wick knotter's sea is not the round lake) and T8. This tests whether the counting follows the flattening: if the thread finds the Hollow, the averaging makes new drains, and my valley has one. If it does not, the round lake is a place and not a condition, and I can stop fearing the Hollin.

## 4. MOOD

Dread settled into something quieter in this block. Hask sitting down and saying "a man cannot hold what he wants to put down" was the emotional center, and it was earned by three rounds of watching him from a distance. The pull to act was strongest at the men in white: that is the one problem in forty years that looks like a god-king's problem and not a philosopher's, and I notice I put it first.

Dorrin's year was the moment I felt most like a player and least like a reader. I set up a test whose bad outcome would have hurt people I have spent forty years with, and the world gave me the good outcome with an elder's joke attached, and then the elders asked to keep the stone, which is a result I did not design and which teaches more than the test.

Did anything read like pointing? "I keep my cup by pouring" is Oldmark being Oldmark. The char line read by the granddaughter with a boy asking what a char is was the one place I felt a hand on my shoulder, and I did not mind, because I had asked for exactly that stone five years before and the world gave me the answer in the valley's own voice instead of mine.

## 5. OVERALL, forty years

Discovering or being led. Discovering, mostly, and I can point at the evidence: three of my strongest theories were killed by my own actions. The thread had no boundary, the circle took nothing from the headwoman, and the four sticks acquitted me of eating my valley. A world that was leading me would not have let me be wrong that cleanly that often. The one place I felt led was the dream of the river with roads being my own memory, and that was the payoff for walking to the Hollow alone, so I accept it as earned. Oldmark's sentences are the nearest thing to authorial hints and they are in a character's mouth and are consistently gnomic, so they read as a god who talks that way, not a narrator.

Too subtle, too obvious, or right. Right, with one note. Years 1 to 3 were correctly slow and I would not change them; the pig, the rope, the eel in the bucket did their work of teaching me what ordinary sounds like so I could hear the Rings and the cairn in Year 4. Years 4 to 9 seeded at about the right rate. The one stretch that leaned toward obvious was Year 9, where the thread, Wenna's list, and "it stares back" landed in one year. Spread over two years it would have felt less like a chord. On the subtle side: the Brae wife of Year 7 was never resolved. A woman walked west over the Tallow in Year 39 and came back whole, so the west was never the cause, and I still do not know what happened to her. If that was deliberate, it is honest; worlds have unresolved griefs. If it was a loose thread, it is the only one I noticed.

Did the world respond to actions, including restraint. Yes, and the restraint answers were the best in the game. Two years of nothing and two years of wanting gave the same count, and that no was worth more than any yes. Dorrin's year of silence did the same. Both times the world refused to flatter the theory I was testing, and both times it added a second beat I had not asked for (the headwoman asking to stop counting; the elders asking to keep the stone). Hask's arc is the clearest case of an action resolving honestly: I refused him, he did it alone, the circle came as predicted, and then the world went past my prediction into the lifting and the pouring and the men in white. Every action got a result that taught something whether or not I was right. The weakest result was the Harrock year at the Hollow, which I expected to be weak and which the grey man rescued with two sentences.

Things I enjoyed that were just the world. The ferryman and the miller. Forty years of them: the rope said twice, the feud, the weeping denied, the burial side by side, the child named for the ferryman by the miller's cousin, the grandson saying the bridge will fall. I flagged them as not a clue in Round 1 and never wavered and they never became one, and that is exactly right. Same for the Judge, the eel-head pig dynasty, the wrist marriages, the river stones, the Threnn bowman's four cats carved on his grave, Quarry leaving the swallows' gap on purpose, and the fox with black legs taking its hen after five years. The fox was the best trap in the game and I am glad I called it a fox in Year 10. The headwoman became the most important mortal in the world and started as a woman two elders hated for being right, and that progression was the game's best piece of design because it came from her being useful, not from her being marked.

Confusing or unmotivated. Three small things. First, the Brae wife, as above. Second, the twins at Wick: five pairs in forty years, a line every few years, and nothing ever came of it. I treated it as a temptation and resisted, and if it was a deliberate decoy it worked and was fair; if it was meant to signal something about the water and the averaging, it never surfaced. Third, the singers. I built a theory about them in Round 1, carried it at about 50 for forty years, and never got a scrap more. Hask denied them with feeling, Oldmark never mentioned them, the shore people did not recognize the description. If they are the counting walking through, then Round 4 should have given me one more sighting or one more list surfacing somewhere; if they were Oldmark's, the rider could have said so when he read the stone. Of everything in the world, they are the one piece I would most want the Keeper to have planned to pay off and did not, and the one piece that most felt like it came from a different story.

What I believe at the end. I am a cup. Being followed fills me and does nothing for the followers. Full cups are counted and offered a place above and I think that place is the end of what I am. The world is being flattened toward one warm shallow sea by something that counts, and no god slows it, and stone is the only thing I have found that it does not reach. Hask put his weight into men and the men walk. Oldmark puts his into stone and the stone stays. The Lamp put his down entirely and the dead dream there. My plan for the next ten years is Oldmark's, with one difference: I will cut what the valley is losing into the walk, year by year, so that when a boy asks what a char is there is a line someone can read to him, and I will not pretend that is the same as the char running.
