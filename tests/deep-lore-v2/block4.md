# Kessvale, Years 31 to 40

What reached you, year by year. Nothing here is explained. Some of it will matter and most of it is the valley being the valley.

## Year 31

You set a stone on the willow walk, a loaf of Quarry white with one line cut across it, and tell the eleven that while you are gone their oaths and their askings go to it. The elders look at it and at you and the oldest says, and if it does not answer, and you say then it does not, and he says that is the same as now, and the others laugh and you do not. Then you go south with the Dorrin elder, who has walked it twice and says the third time is the one that kills a man and goes anyway.

The salt is the same and the Ostrel are fewer, and there is a ring of men in salt-white cloaks around the lord now who speak before he does and are not laughed at. The lord Hask came to you the first night, a wind off the plain with the wet in it and the face easier to see than the elder had said, and did not laugh, and asked you to sit, and sat. He said he had asked for a god because a man cannot hold what he wants to put down. He told it like this. For a year before the cold hour, his shadow on the salt had been going thin, so that his people could look at him longer, and his own voice had begun coming back to him off the plain a breath after he spoke it, and when his people prayed he heard the prayers twice. In his sleep, every cold hour, there were tall ones with no faces standing in water, shallow water, wide, salt, up to their long knees, and they counted, and one night they counted him and his number was nearly theirs, and they said he could have his sea back for good if he would come up and carry it down himself. On the cold hour in the autumn he was standing among his people and the salt let go of him. He was a hand above it for the length of a breath and the thread was beside him like a rope let down, and he looked down and the salt was under him and he was not on it, and he knew, more plainly than he has known anything, that one breath more and he would not come down. So he came down. He has given away everything he can find a hand to give to, and he sleeps now, some nights. He says the star wanted his sea and reached for him to get it, and that it is a jealous thing, and that he will not fill again where it can see him. He laughed then, once, and asked you to stay the night awake with him, and you did.

The elder, who sat apart, says the lord's men in white went among the camp while the lord talked and took salt from every hearth and that no one stopped them.

The Hollin Ford shepherds pull down the first three cairns above the high pasture, and the shepherd who saw the thread first, who is old now, tells the folded coat at the count and will tell it every count, he says, until he is dead. The cairn road is grass from the pasture to the shoulder of the Combs and then stone again.

Cranes south, high, a line a morning long. A Saltmeet child counts them and gets a number and is told that is not how cranes are counted.

## Year 32

Men in salt-white cloaks come up the salt road to Ninefield with a cart and say the lord Hask's sea tithe is owed by every field the sea once covered and that Ninefield's fields were once the sea's, and they take a cart of salt pork and the one apple's whole crop and say they will be back at the count. Ninefield sends to Dorrin. The Ostrel refugees at Ninefield say the men in white are the lord's weight walking, and that the lord does not stop them because he cannot, and that he does not know they have come north.

The Hollin Ford man goes to the round lake by the Combs road one last time, and the Ostrel knotter at Wick goes with him, which no one expected, and ties nothing on the way. They come back at the end of summer. A third of the huts with his words on them are empty. The shore people fed them and did not point at the water more than before. He asked, with signs and the words he has, what the tall ones count and whether the number goes up or down. The oldest woman there, whose hut word is one of his, took his hand and set it on his own chest and then on the water, and did it again, and then held up all her fingers and closed them one at a time looking at the shore, and opened them one at a time looking at the lake. He says they count the shore people. He says the number goes down. The Wick knotter says it goes up. They argued about it the whole way home.

The Wick knotter slept four nights there and dreamed the counting, and says she knows the water the tall ones stand in. It is not this lake. It is a sea, shallow and wide and warm and salt, the kind a person can wade a mile in, and she grew up on the edge of one going dry and knows it by the smell in the dream. She would not say more and has gone back to sitting by the Kess.

The bridge toll is set at an egg and nobody pays it and the miller's cousin keeps a tally of who does not.

Wool washing week. The spring runs full.

## Year 33

Dorrin stops saying your name. The elders tell the town, and the town does it, mostly, the way a town does a thing the elders have asked: they say it to the stone on the walk instead, at table, before the ford, over the sick, and children are hushed when they forget. You feel it inside the month, the step down, and it is a larger step than Quarry's was, and it stays down. Brae, Wick, the rest go on as before and nothing in the valley is different that you can see, and you hold off the second flood at Hollin Ford as you always do, and the children cross dry, and Dorrin thanks the stone.

The headwoman begins the stone. A slab of Quarry white the length of two men laid flat under the willows, and she dictates and the stone master's boy cuts, one line a year from Year 3, a line for what came in and a line for what was lost, in the valley's own saying. By the count she has cut to Year 12. The old people come to watch and argue with her about Year 6 and she is not wrong.

Through the Threnn bowl you ask the grey man whether the clans will leave the Lamp alone for one year, not sleep beside it, not go to it, not speak of it. He comes to the oak himself. He says that is a hand on the Lamp, and the god said it would keep its hand off, and the god has kept its word and so will he: his own clan will stay away from the Hollow for a year, as a gift between people who keep words, and the other five will do as they do, and he cannot speak for them and will not try. He says the Lamp does not need the clans; the clans need the Lamp. He says it is the dead who sleep there, not the stone.

Snow on the Tallow by the count. Nine children named at the third spring. One is named for the headwoman, who says she is not dead yet.

A Wick man and a Dorrin woman walk the Kess tied at the wrist and arrive at Wick on the fourth day, the fastest anyone remembers, and the old people say they must have run, and they say they did.

## Year 34

Dorrin's year ends at the count and the headwoman reads the sticks. Dorrin lost: a word for the mill race, a saying about the ferry rope, the name of the eldest carp. Brae lost: a verse, a knot name, a word for the grey of wool from a low spring. The same within a thing, as before. Dorrin had the same fevers and the same floods and the same harvest as Brae and no more or less of any of them. The elders ask whether Dorrin may go on speaking to the stone, since it is on the walk and the god is not always, and it has done as well. You feel the weight come back the week they start saying your name again, and it comes back whole.

Your seasons at the Hollow: the third weight is what it was, east and a little south, through all four. If it is a hair less by the autumn you cannot swear to it, and five clans slept there before they rode, as they always have, and the grey man's clan did not.

Wenna's sister goes south with the elder's salt party, and Hesk with her, carrying paper and ink and no cord, to sit a season with the hundred at the plain's edge. Wenna ties for Brae alone that year and does not have her list kept and says she will catch up.

A rock cat is seen on the Dorrin side of the Kess for the first time anyone knows, and the dogs will not go near where it stood.

The Hollin at the ford is wide enough this spring that the first ford is done at the old ferry stones instead, and the elders say it counts.

## Year 35

Wenna's sister comes back before the count with Hesk, who has seen the salt and no sea and has brought back a handful of it and says it was worth going. The sister sat a season at the edge of the circle, where the hundred live in a camp their children keep. She wrote each one a name on a board hung at the neck and the way home from the brine pans drawn on the back, and the children, who can read if she taught them, read the boards to them each morning. By the season's end most of the hundred turned when their board name was said, the way a dog does, and found their own fire by the drawing with a child beside them. None of them knew the child. Asked which child was hers, a woman pointed at the child holding her board. One man walked into a channel at night and the water was knee deep and he lay down in it. The sister says they can be taught to answer and to find the fire, and that no one is behind the answering, and she has written that down for Wenna, and Wenna has read it and said nothing.

Hesk says the men in white asked her at every pan to tie for brine and she said she had no cord, and one of them cut her a cord from his cloak, and she gave it back.

A Sellen rider comes to the willow walk, the same hooded man as before, older, and waits standing and says: I keep my cup by pouring. He says it is from Oldmark for the god of the Kess, who keeps terms. He says nothing else, and looks at the headwoman's stone for a long time, and reads it, and nods at a line, and goes.

The headwoman has cut to Year 22 and has slowed, and says her hands are the trouble, not her head.

The miller's cousin dies and the mill goes to a Wick refugee's son who married into it, and the carp are older than any of them.

## Year 36

No one from Hollin Ford or Cold Harbour has walked the cairn road since the first three came down, the shepherds say, and the old shepherd tells the coat at the count and is listened to. A trader who came in from the Combs the long way says the shore people's huts are half empty and the shore people are as kind as ever, and that he did not sleep there.

The Hollin is slow from the lake to the ford, wide and brown all year, and the Cold Harbour boats are poled now, not rowed. The fisher's son says the perch are fine.

The men in white take the Ninefield count again and leave a Ninefield man dead who argued. Ninefield sends to Dorrin again, and the Ostrel refugees say the lord sleeps and does not know.

The Judge's willow at the Wick landing is big enough to sit under. Wick sits under it.

Twins at Wick, a fifth pair, and no one says anything about the water or anything else.

## Year 37

The Saltmeet headwoman dies in the autumn, at the count, in her chair, after reciting Year 3 to Year 36 without an error, and the stone is cut to Year 30 and her granddaughter has the sticks and the rest of it by heart, she says, from hearing it, and the elders say they will see. Saltmeet buries her at the lick, which has never been done, and the deer came at dusk anyway.

Old Tamsin dies at Brae in her bed, and Wenna sits with her, and Brae does not know what to cut on the stone and cuts Old Tamsin, which is what someone had to call her.

A dry summer. The east wind. The Harrock do not come down. The bowl has a child's bracelet in it.

Hask's weight to the south has not moved in three years, not up and not down. The weight to the east has not moved in fifteen.

The willow walk votes to mend the bridge and the ferryman's grandson says it will fall.

## Year 38

Cold hours this year on four nights and the thread on every one of them, the high pasture says, and nothing in your sleep but sleep.

The Wick knotter who will not tie is asked by the Ostrel refugees to tie for brine in the Kess marsh, where they have cut pans, and she will not, and they cut a cord for the Wick smith's girl, who has been watching her, and the girl ties, and the pan whitens, and the Judge's willow drops its leaves in midsummer and stands bare.

The headwoman's granddaughter cuts Year 31 and Year 32 and the elders argue with her about Year 32 and she is right, and they do not like it.

Nine children named at the third spring, none of them for anyone.

The Hollin Ford shepherds say the high pasture has a word now for the grass that came from the shore people and have forgotten it did.

## Year 39

Ninefield asks Dorrin a third time what is to be done about the men in white, and the one apple's crop goes south again, and the six-generation family says they will fight the next cart with hoes and are told not to.

A Brae woman walks west over the Tallow after a strayed flock and comes back in a month with all her names and the flock, and says the far side is just hills and she does not see what the fuss was.

Wenna's sister reads her the list on the longest night, as she does every year, and Wenna says she remembers none of it and all of it, and the sister says that is what the list is for.

Cranes north, late, after the ice, a line a morning long, and the Saltmeet child who counted them is a girl now and has stopped.

Snow on the Tallow early. The Brae clip is in before it, and Brae wants that noticed.

## Year 40

The Hollin is not a river from Lake Hollow to the ford. It is a brown water a quarter mile wide and knee deep everywhere and no deeper anywhere, with reeds, and it goes where the land goes and nowhere in particular, and the stony ford is a place in it where the stones are, and the children cross anywhere now and are not given anything for it. Below the ford it narrows and remembers itself and comes down to the Kess as it always did. Cold Harbour poles out through the reeds to the lake, which is the same breadth on the map and warm and shallow and full of perch of one size. The Cold Harbour fisher says it is the warm, and that his grandfather told him the lake was cold once, and that he does not know what cold water would be for.

The headwoman's granddaughter reads the stone at the count, every line, from Year 3. At the Year 3 line she reads: Cold Harbour, the char have not run. A Cold Harbour boy asks what a char is. No one on the willow walk can tell him. The line is clean and deep and the stone master's boy, who is the stone master now, says it will be there when the willows are not.

The one-line stone on the walk has a worn place on it the size of a hand.

The Harrock woman at Threnn, who is old, says the grey man is dead and his son keeps the bowl and the clans still sleep in the Hollow before they ride and dream the river, all six of them again, and that the Sellen god's camps have moved west toward Threnn by a day, and the clans do not know what to do about a thing that does not fight.

The weight to the south is as it was. The weight to the east is as it was. The third weight is as it was the night you came.

The lantern walk on the longest night, from Dorrin to the willow walk and back, by starlight, which is enough to read the headwoman's stone by, and a child does, and is told to stop.
