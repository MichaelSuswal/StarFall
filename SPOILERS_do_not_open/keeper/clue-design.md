# Clue design (v2)

Companion to deep-lore.md. This file turns the eight truths into eight conclusions a player can reach, lists the clues for each, and sets the pacing. Names here are those of the test world (tests/deep-lore-v2/test-world.md); the structure transfers to any campaign with the nouns swapped.

Kind codes: SEEN means the god or its people witness it directly. REPORTED means a named mortal tells the god what they saw. RUMORED means it arrives secondhand or older, through traders, songs, or children. Earliest year is the first year the clue can plausibly surface; most surface later, and only if the god is where the clue is or has someone there.

The rule under every clue: it shows an effect, an object, or a behavior. It never states a cause. Any mortal who states a cause is wrong about something that matters.

In a campaign, these tables are written into the mystery template (`creation-runbook.md` section 7.8). Each clue row becomes a clue under `### Clues`: its location (with map id) from "How surfaced" and the place it sits, "What is shown" from the clue column, and "Readings" from "Also supports" together with the reading the clue points toward. Kind and Earliest stay in the clue's text. Each wrong branch and restraint branch goes under `### Branches` with what the world does. "Opens next" is the Keeper's own thread and needs no heading. Names in this file are the test world's and carry no map ids; names in a campaign file always do.

God-sense is set per campaign (DESIGN 7.2) and nothing here fixes it. Rows that rest on gods feeling one another at a distance are marked; they exist only in a campaign whose god-sense gives that.

## Pacing spine for 60 years

Years are bare numbers. What each year carries is the earliest the world pushes it toward the god; the god may reach any of it sooner by going.

1. Arrival. Whatever the campaign's god-sense gives on landing (in the test world, three pulls felt). Nothing else. Plain world.
8. First cluster: the char have not run, the Longwalkers pass through, Ninefield's apples. Still mostly plain world.
12. The Harrock cairn story reaches Threnn.
15. First star reading possible, if marks were set by Year 8.
18. Oldmark's first envoy, in proverbs.
22. Hask's great working. Refugees. The dead circle.
28. Hask lifts and sets down. The shedding begins in the south.
30. The Still begins taking the nameless, then one of the god's own.
34. Second star interval. The rate is visible.
38. If the god is near the ceiling, the voices. If not, silence, and the god may never hear them.
42. The Longwalkers return.
48. The Hollin goes general if the valley's spending has not slowed. If it has, the Hollin keeps its bends and the char return to the lake's north end, which is the world's clearest yes.
55. Consequence turn: whichever branch the god chose on the Still, the stone, and the ceiling pays out plainly.
60. End state. The star is lower. The god knows three or four things at 80 and the rest at 40 to 60.

Years 1 to 7 carry no clue the god has not gone looking for, except what god-sense gives on Year 1 and a shepherd's remark in Year 2. Random events are weighted toward weather, beasts, births, and quarrels in those years.

## Plain world

This material is not a clue and never becomes one. It exists because the world exists. Put it in every morning story.

**Places.** The Kess, brown in spring and green in autumn, with a stony ford at Hollin Ford that children dare each other across. The Combs, long grass hills to the east where the wind makes a sound like breathing through teeth. The Tallow Range to the west, whose snow goes pink at dusk. Quarry's white stone face and the swallows that nest in its cuts. The willow walk at Dorrin where the elders meet. The hot spring above Brae, ordinary, sulfurous, used for washing wool. Saltmeet's lick, where deer come at dusk and no one hunts them by custom. The high pasture above Hollin Ford where the shepherds summer. The reed beds at Wick. The nine stone-walled fields of Ninefield, laid out by someone's grandfather's grandfather for reasons no one remembers and everyone defends. The old oak at Threnn with a Harrock arrowhead grown into it.

**Customs.** The autumn count, when every village brings its tally to Dorrin and the elders argue about it for three days. The first ford, when a Hollin Ford child crosses alone for the first time and is given a river stone. Wool washing week at Brae. Saltmeet's rule against hunting at the lick. The Threnn custom of leaving a bowl on the wall for the Harrock, who sometimes take it and sometimes leave something. Marriage by rope: couples walk the length of the Kess from Brae to Wick tied at the wrist, and are married if they arrive. Quarry's silence day after a death in the cuts. Naming at the third spring, not at birth. The pig weighing. The lantern walk on the longest night, from Dorrin to the willow walk and back, carrying no light but what the star gives.

**Beasts.** Glass eels in the Kess, clear as water, that cook white. Rock cats in the Combs that scream like women and take lambs. Blue cranes that pass over in spring heading north and in autumn heading south and never land in the valley. The Hollow char, a fish of the lake (its going is a clue; its existing is not). Red deer at the lick. Bees that nest in the quarry cuts and give a stone-tasting honey. A kind of fox with black legs. Marsh lights at Wick that are gas and have always been gas. The great grey heron the Wick people call the Judge. Carp in Dorrin's mill pond, some older than the miller.

**Weather and sky.** Spring floods on the Kess, reliable, three to five days. A dry east wind in late summer that the Threnn people call the Harrock's breath. Autumn fogs that fill the valley to the hill line and leave the Combs standing out like islands. Winters that freeze the ford most years and the mill pond every year. Snow on the Tallow by the autumn count. The star, which is a fact of the sky and gives enough light to walk by on a clear night, and which children are told not to stare at, for no reason anyone can give. Dim nights, when the star fades for an hour; these are a clue when the thread is mentioned and plain world when they are simply a cold hour the shepherds dislike.

**People.** The miller at Dorrin and his feud with the ferryman. The Saltmeet headwoman who remembers every tally since she was twelve. The Brae wool women. Quarry's stone master, who has seven fingers. Twins born at Wick. A boy at Hollin Ford who cannot be kept from the river. The Threnn bowman who has killed four rock cats. Elders who disagree about the count. A Ninefield family that has farmed the same walled field for six generations and will tell you so.

## Ratio rule of thumb

In a morning story, four plain items for every one clue item, never fewer than three to one. In the first seven years, closer to six to one, and most of those clues should be clues only in hindsight. A clue item is written in exactly the register of the plain items around it. If the god can tell which item the Keeper cares about, the item is wrong. A good test: read the morning story aloud and ask which three items a bored farmer would repeat at the well. If the clue is among them, it is too loud. If it never is, it is too quiet. Aim for the clue to be the fourth thing a farmer repeats.
