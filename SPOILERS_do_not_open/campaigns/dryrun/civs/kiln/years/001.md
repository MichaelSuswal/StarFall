# The Kiln, Year 1

**The great kiln.** Labor set by analogy: a fitted-stone ring-wall of about 60 yards at 6 worker-days a yard (stone wall rate) is 360, and 400 cubic yards of clay dug and puddled at 0.7 doubled for clay is 560. Total about 920 worker-days, plus Ashwake working loud at 1.6 workers, which the people counted and did not mention. A crew of sixty, in rotation so the sowing and harvest lost no hands, needed about fifteen full days; weather, the spring flood and the autumn rate of 0.9 stretched it to a month of work spread from late spring to the first frost. Stone work stopped in winter as the rules say. The kiln stands finished and was fired once, a long low burn, on the night before the first frost. It draws well. The potters say it is the best they have seen and are annoyed that a god built it.

**Timber trade.** A party of eighty went north in force with charcoal and pots and bought beams and planks for the hall, felled the season before, to be stacked and seasoned until next year. The northerners haggled for nine days, called the pots thin and the charcoal damp, and took more than the Kiln wished to pay. The Kiln called them thieves and cheats to their faces, shoved a few shoulders, and bought anyway. No blows were struck. Both sides went home certain they had been robbed, which is how the Kiln know a fair price.

**The watch fire.** Six men keep the western rim. They have seen nothing but the sky-lights. They quarrel over who sits the cold watch and are proud of it.

**The coal custom.** Every steading banks a coal at dusk and brings one to the town hearth at the new moon. The first months were solemn. By autumn it is a market day, with quarrels, and Ashwake sits through it and speaks. Two steadings let their coals die once and sent apologies.

**Births and deaths.** 182 births, 167 deaths (nine in the spring flood-fevers, a dozen in winter). Population 5,015.

**Stores.** The bought grain carried them to spring, thin by the end. Charcoal and pots are plentiful; much was spent on the north. Grain for next winter must be bought again.

**Mood.** Warming. Pride in the kiln. A few still afraid.

**Factions.** Two. The Hearth-keepers, mostly old potters on the council, want everything done at the hearth, by his word. The Quarrelers, younger burners, say a god who needs timber is a god who needs the north, and ask why he does not simply burn it.

**The returned parties.** The first pair came home at midsummer, one of them limping from a sprained ankle taken on broken ground; the second pair came some days later, the young burner's arm broken in a fall on the way back and splinted by the clay-digger. All four brought the coals home alive. The people were disappointed that no god was found, then proud that the coals lived. The clay-digger's news of the notched poles and the hill people's refusal made the town uneasy and then loud. The Quarrelers want to go and ask the hill people's leave with spears; the council forbids it.
