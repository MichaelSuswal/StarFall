# Year 1

Late snow held the passes into the first weeks of spring. In those days the woman who had come among them walked out the west gate and up into the Carn hills. She gave no orders and took no one. The wardens said she walks the passes, and went back to their bars.

The passes opened by mid-spring and the toll-houses filled: salt and cloth from the west, bronze and pots from the east. A dry summer thinned the grass and the goats gave less milk, but the flocks held at about 3,200 head after the kidding and the autumn cull. The spring under the town never faltered. The north pass shut with early snow in late autumn, as most years.

About the twentieth day of spring two pairs of dusty strangers came to the east gate a day apart, carrying lidded pots of live coals, with bundles of fired pots and charcoal. The east warden asked who sent them and what the pots held. They said a god's word, for someone in the west, and would say no more. The warden would not let live fire inside the walls, thatch being thatch, so the coals were kept in the gate-yard under watch while the pair paid in pots and rested a night. Both pairs went west on payment, as anyone does. Some forty days later the first pair came back east through the town, one man limping, and paid again; the second came some days after with a man's arm splinted, and paid too. The second pair said hill people had turned them back, and would not say how. The council met once over this, called the pots good work, and judged it no business of the Gatefolk who carries fire where.

Births numbered 172 and deaths 163, so the town stands at 5,009. The dead were mostly old, taken by the winter cough, with two men lost to a rockfall in the north pass in autumn. Stores are one good winter's grain and cheese, a little more than last year, with toll goods in the gate-houses.

Mood is wary and steady. Two voices run through the market. The bar-keepers say the woman left because no one gave her a hearth, and that a god unwelcomed will not return. The toll-first folk say a god who walks away owes them nothing and they owe her nothing, and that gates stay shut at dusk regardless. The council has quieted both.

Of the Carn they say little and trade warily; they do not trust them. Of the sky-lights, the pale one at midsummer and another at midwinter, both in the west, they say the sky has gates too, and someone is opening and shutting them.
