> SPOILER WARNING. Keeper release to the god Thornmere, Year 1. Your year is held at a moment of contact; your decisions for Year 2 wait until the scene is played in the day session.

You walked west from the first days of spring, alone, by night where the ground was open, walking every notch, ford and fork (your mapped crossings are yours to keep in your record: the Keeper counts eleven notches and nine fords between the passes and the river valley, and the brook crossings along the hill foot). The Carn hold that country in stone-and-turf steadings with more dogs than people. As far as you know, no one saw you.

About the fifth week you came over the last hills and saw the river valley below: water meadows and scattered oak, a brown river running south to a wide marsh and a grey sea, a dozen villages along it, smoke. You crossed a brook above the nearest village (its people call it Wellbrook) in the sixth week at night and went north along the hill foot. The pull was in the valley, at that village, and then it moved upriver, slowly, a few miles every several days, and you kept level with it along the hills all summer.

What you learned from the herders' talk at the hill foot, overheard, never spoken to: the river people call themselves the Riverfolk and their god the Weir-King. He woke on a shingle bar at the river's mouth at the start of spring and has walked upriver since, wetting his hand at every staithe; he says little; he ordered a weir built at the mouth and sent two men up past the falls into the wood. The people are fishers and barley farmers, about a dozen villages, no walls. They count the lights in the sky at his asking.

Your senses: two sky-lights seen from the hills, one in late spring over the wood to the north, one at midsummer over the hills behind you to the east (a third, in early spring over the sea, you may have seen from the Carn hills before you reached the valley; the Keeper counts it as seen). The far pull to the east has not moved. The near pull came to the top of the valley, to the falls where the river leaves the wood, in the twenty-second week.

Week 23, evening: you came down the east bank path to the pool below the falls, openly, in sight of the village they call Fallstead. The god is standing knee-deep in the river at the foot of the falls, facing you: a tall man, river-grey eyes, never dry, water beading on him though he stands in spray. Two children on the village staithe three hundred paces downstream have seen you. You stopped at the water's edge ten paces from him on the stones. He has not spoken and neither have you. The scene is the day's; when the DM brings you in, speak as yourself from your decisions (one sentence of who you are; ask what it is and what it wants; nothing of your power; no oath, no bargain).

Nothing reached you from the Gatefolk this year; you have not been back.
