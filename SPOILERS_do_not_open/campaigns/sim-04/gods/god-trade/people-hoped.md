# People: unnamed

They call themselves the Ford folk, as one says "the folk by the water", and I leave the name at that. A name is better given late, by those who mean it. Their town is called the Ford and nothing else. Smaller places, wards and houses among them are left unnamed for play.

## Who they are
Human, as the world takes it. A river people and a road people at once: ferrymen, carters, drovers, salt weighers, granary keepers, weavers and potters, and the farmers on the low hills round about who bring their harvest in. Not one lineage but a good many, mixed by two centuries of strangers marrying in off the roads. They are neither old nor new at the ford. They have their own history and were not waiting for me; I arrive to a people already living. Plain faces, strong backs, careful hands. They count aloud when they work.

## How many
About five thousand, as the release sets. Most live within the wall and the rest on farms and in hamlets within a morning's walk. I know a larger people eats and burns more and moves slower, and I take that as the world's price and not a weakness of mine.

## How they live
In a walled town at the ford, with a gate for every road. A ferry for the slack months and a ford for the dry ones. Market every ninth day and a great market at the end of harvest, which is when I come down. Salt and grain, wool, hides, bronze goods, pots, honey and wax pass through. They keep the granaries full against a bad year and lend from them at the spring, to be repaid in kind at harvest with a measured share over. Houses of timber and wattle on stone footings, a few of stone. Each ward has its well, its oven and its shrine.

## Their code
"Weigh it twice and say it once." A promise made at the market stone is kept. A cheat in weights is shamed, made to make whole, and shut out of the market for a season, never locked up and never beaten. No one is held as a pledge, and no one is held for a debt: a debtor works it off in the open, among neighbors. A traveler is given water and a look at the scale before anything else. A gate is a thing to be passed through, not a thing to be guarded against. They say: "The honest man has nothing to bar."

## Skills and culture
Ferrying, cart building, droving, salt weighing, granary keeping, weaving, pottery, bee keeping, and a deep, tedious love of counting. They keep their accounts on knotted cords and notched sticks; I do not assume they have letters, and I will not bring them in before the town can build the use for them. They sing counting songs to the children. They hold the harvest fair with feasting, and the first rains are met with bread and salt laid on the shrine stones. Ordinary culture, which is free.

## Edges and their weaknesses
**Edge: the Ford's [f39] Scales.** Their weights and measures are agreed among the whole town, kept true by a keeping house of the wardens, and known along the roads for a day's walk and more, so that traders choose to cross and sell at the Ford and not at another crossing, and strangers' disputes are settled there with less quarrel. The edge is a little more custom than a like town would have, about as much as the smallest power on a card. It works without me: the keeping house does its work by hand, by standard stones and sealed measures, and it takes nothing from my Fair Weight and gives my Fair Weight nothing. If I hold a false weight it is made honest as the card says, and that is mine alone.
**Weakness offered: open gates.** The town keeps no standing watch and no paid fighting men, and no gate is barred against a peaceful comer by day. A watch is raised from the wards only when the horn is blown. The people are slow to rouse and soft to a hard raid, and a hungry band at harvest would find the ford thin. They would notice it the first season a stranger took advantage. I ask the Keeper to say what price it sees.

## Their magic
Little, and simple, as the world makes it, and it costs the worker. A handful of people in the town, three or four I hope, have what they call "the tongue for salt": by tasting a sick person's sweat, spit or water they can tell what is wrong in the body now, the fever or the rot or the dryness, though not what will come of it. It is what already is, never what is coming. It costs the worker her taste: for a day after each working she tastes nothing, and a worker who tastes too often goes ill in the mouth for a season. I give no number as more than a wish. I want no foresight among them.

## What they believe about gods
They believe the great ones keep the world's accounts: that the rains, the harvests and the lives of men are a ledger, and that a god, if one ever walked among them, would be the one who weighs it. They say, "A fair god weighs the same for every man." They do not expect any god to come, and are neither frightened nor eager. There is one old saying at the ford, which I report as theirs and do not claim as mine: "When the harvest is in, a tall one will come down the hill and ask to see the scale." They tell it as a joke at the fair, to children and to the drunk. I do not know that it is anything more.

## Rule and worship
Rule: a council of elders, as I lean. Seven elders sit: the salt weigher, the ferry warden, the keeper of the granary, the drover, the widow who holds the town's accounts, the beekeeper whom no one can out argue, and one elder chosen from the wards by the wards in turn. The chair passes each autumn. I am heard there and have no vote. A matter is agreed when none of the seven will stand against it, which is slow and which I like. I do not rule them. I will say what I think when asked, and when not asked I will wait for a gap and speak.
Worship: local shrines, as I lean, and for the same reason. Each house keeps a flat stone with a scale cut in it, and each ward one larger. A pinch of salt is laid on it on market days and a loaf at the fair. No priests; whoever keeps the house keeps the stone. I ask for no worship and distrust a people who offer more. If they come to pray to me, I will not turn them away but I will not ask.

## Hierarchy
The council advises and rules under the god in the sense that I am heard; it holds the town. The chair speaks for the council. Under it are the ward elders, who settle the small quarrels of each ward at its stone, with the neighbors as witnesses. A quarrel not settled there goes to the keeping house, where it is weighed: each side brings what it says is owed, and the keepers measure, and the parties then swear before the stone what they will accept. A quarrel not settled there goes to the council. A matter of blood goes to the whole council at once and is settled by payment in salt and grain, set by the dead one's kin, and by the offender's work for the kin; no one is killed for it. No cell, no stocks. Exile from the market for a season is the heaviest common penalty, and exile from the town is for the council alone.

## Helpers
The offices that run things when I am away:
- **The Tallyman**, who keeps the town's cords and sticks, including the cord of every bargain sworn before me and the count of those still standing. Held at present by the widow who keeps the accounts.
- **The Keeper of the Standard Stones**, who holds the keeping house, the stone weights and the sealed measures, and checks the market scales on the ninth day.
- **The Gatewarden**, one for each gate, who open and close the gates and keep the bars. The bars are set from within the town.
- **The Road Reeve**, from the drovers, who walks the roads, sees to the ditches and the ford stones, and carries my orders to the hamlets.
- **The Ferry Warden**, who keeps the ferry, the ford marks and the water depth, and sends word up and down the river.
- **The Granary Keeper**, who holds the stores and the spring loans.
Orders travel by cord and by runner. A knotted cord is carried by the Road Reeve's runners to hamlets and gates, and a runner says the meaning aloud while the cord is shown, so that the order is both told and tallied. When I am away the Tallyman and the chair together stand in my place at the council, and no order of mine is carried out that the council has not heard first.

## How the god meets them
I come down on the low hill above the ford at harvest, as the release says, and I come down the hill by daylight where they can see me, walking, with my scale in my hand and a cord at my belt, not hurrying. I do not announce what I am. I will let myself be seen and will not hide. I am as tall as two men and cannot pass as one, so I will say plainly that I am tall and that I come to trade and to count, and that I will answer what I am when I am asked by someone who wants the answer. I will not work any wonder in the first month beyond my own weighing. My first act, by name, is to ask to see the market scale and to weigh a sack of salt on it, in front of whoever is there, and tell them true what it comes to and whether the scale is honest. After that I will go to the Gatewarden and ask for the council. Where I stand and what they say to a giant coming down the hill is the world's. I would like to be named by them in time, and I will not ask for it.

## Questions for you
1. **A door fastened from the far side holds you. Does your people know it, and who in the council holds the bars of the town's gates?**
They do not know it at first. I am wary and a weakness is a thing a stranger can use. I will tell the chair of the council and the Gatewarden of the river gate, as a thing given in trust, and no one else until I have seen what they do with it. Every other gate has its bars set from within the town, by the Gatewardens, who answer to the council. I ask for one custom, which is not a written fence and which the town may refuse: the river gate has a small door in it, a wicket, whose latch is on the outside and is never barred, so that I may always come in from the road. If the wicket is barred against me I am held out until someone opens it, and I will say so plainly when asked. I hold no bars myself, and I want none.
2. **Twelve standing bargains sworn before you slow you, and forty stop your roads. Who decides which bargains of the town are sworn before you and which are not?**
Not I. A bargain is sworn before me only when its parties choose to swear it so, on a stone day, at the market stone, with my hand held out. The small deals of the market go on by the old way, a pinch of salt and a spoken word, without me. I ask the council, as custom and not as a rule I can enforce, to bring me only the bargains that are large: land, a loan from the granary, a debt of many days' work, a treaty with a neighbor. The Tallyman keeps the cord of those standing and clears each when it is done, released or mended. When the count reaches ten he tells the council, and the parties wait a stone day or two. I will tell them how heavy it is when I am slowed. I will refuse no oath that is honestly offered and fairly sworn.
3. **You may keep four roads. Which four ways at your ford do you mean to walk first, and who keeps the others?**
First the road I come down on, on the near side of the ford, out along the way a day's walk; then the same road on the far bank across the ford; then the road that runs along the bank upriver; then the bank road downriver. Each I will walk end to end, no more than a day's walk, and lay my hand on at the ford end. I will take them in that order, a road a season, so that I learn what the footfalls do to me before I take on the next. The other ways, the hill tracks and the lanes to the hamlets, are kept by the Road Reeve and the drovers by their own labor, as they are now, and I do not touch them. I will not take a fifth.
