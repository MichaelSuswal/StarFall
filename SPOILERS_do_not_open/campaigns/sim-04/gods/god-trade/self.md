# Self: the Weigher (no name of its own)

## Name
It has none. It reaches for one and finds a smooth place where one should be. It asks those below to call it the Weigher, and it asks to be filed under that: the Weigher, a giant of trade and counting. Whoever gives it a better word may keep the word for their trouble, but it will not answer to a name it has not been given by someone who meant it.

## Form
A giant, about twice the height of a tall man, broad through the shoulder and slow in the turning of the head. Stoops under a lintel and has learned to do it without being reminded. The face is long and heavy, plain, weathered like a gatepost, with deep lines about the eyes from counting. Grey hair worn short, no beard. Hands very large and very careful; it picks up a coin the way another man picks up an egg. Dresses in undyed wool and a leather apron with many small pockets. Its eyes and ears are those of a large man, a little better at a glance than most, and its nose knows salt, grain, and wax. It walks at a giant's unhurried pace, a long even stride that does not tire, and it does not run. It speaks low and slowly, and what it says is mostly old sayings turned toward the matter at hand.
### What breaks you
Great blows from many hands at once, fire kept up for a long time, being crushed or buried, being dragged apart. It can be wounded and made clumsy by hurt. It can be held: bound in heavy chain, walled in, buried, or shut in a room whose door is barred from the far side. Held is held, not broken. A shut door with no one on the other side to open it is the thing that would undo it most.
### When your body is broken
It gathers again, by the power Return to the Stone below.
### Things carried
A balance of plain wood with two bronze pans, a set of lead weights in a pouch, and a long cord of tally knots. All ordinary objects. The scale is not needed for any power below; it is only the habit of its hands.

## Traits
1. **Checks the hands, then leaps.** Reconciles Trust (wary, 86) with Temper (impulsive, 72) and its virtue of restraint. It will not take a stranger's word and it studies a stranger's hands, weights and proverbs for a long while. Then, with no warning even to itself, it decides, and the deciding is quick and whole. It holds its tongue and its fist until the moment the matter is plain, and afterward it cannot always say why that moment.
2. **Opens every way and will not walk through the last one.** Reconciles Order (freedom, 86), Making (builder, 20) and Regard (sovereign, 57). It builds roads, weights, gates and marketplaces, anything that lets a thing pass, and it hates a lock, a fence, a decree that shuts. It wishes to be heard in council and not to command it, and it is quietly sure it knows what the council ought to decide. It gives the people their liberty, and keeps for itself the last say on whether the liberty is being used well.

## Backstory outline
It remembers counting. A great many things, in a great many rooms, each room leading to another, each count checked against the one before. It remembers having made the place where the counting was done, turning by turning, and that it was careful and proud. It remembers the sound of a door. It does not remember who was on the other side of it, or for how long it counted after. It has a faint, unfinished feeling of having been called by something once, and of the word being gone. That is all. It does not look at that place long.

## What it wants
To find the people it expects and give them a seat at a place where two ways cross. To set true weights and measures among them so that no one is cheated and no one has to cheat. To hear what is counted in other lands. To have nothing shut behind it. And to be spoken to by something that sounds like a name.

## Proposed powers
### The Fair Weight
What it does: Whatever it holds in its hands or sets on a balance it knows to the true. It knows how much a thing weighs, how many are in a heap, how much grain is in a sack, how pure a lump of metal is as against the ordinary run of such metal in that land, and whether a measure, scale or tally has been falsified. It can lay its hand on a measure and make it true: a cheating weight, held by it, is made honest in its mass until next it is used by another hand. It shows effects, not causes: it knows the weight is false and by how much, not who falsified it or why. Works only on what it touches. "True weight" in this write-up means the weight as against the ordinary standard of that place, set among the people by agreement.
Why this god has it: Trade and counting are what it is. A stranger can only be trusted at a table with an honest scale.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While it weighs, and for the hour after, it cannot say anything it knows to be false, nor hold back what it has found. It would lose its one trick of the table, the bluff, and cannot play a hand of cards or a haggle with a closed face. The truth it has found comes out of its mouth.
Edge cases it expects: A weight falsified by honest mistake counts the same as one falsified by fraud, since it sees effects only. It cannot weigh a thing too large to hold or lift. A thing weighed does not stay weighed; the next hand may spoil it again.

### The Kept Road
What it does: A road, ford or bridge it has walked end to end, and then blessed by laying its hand upon, becomes a kept road. Mud, flood and drift trouble it less. The ford stays crossable a little longer in a flood, the verge stays firm, and travelers carrying goods go a little quicker, as though the way were shorter. It feels the weight of passage on any road it keeps, within about a day's walk of where it stands: how many feet, how heavy a load, and which way they go. It does not learn who they are. It feels a blocking of a kept road, from a toll bar or a fallen tree, as a cut. It can keep at most a few roads at once, and a road it stops keeping reverts in a season.
Why this god has it: A seat where two roads cross is its aim. A trader who knows when the caravan is coming has already sold the salt.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It cannot shut out the footfalls. A kept road with a crowd on it is a clamor in its body, and in a market season it will be dull with other people's walking, slow in thought, easily startled, unable to attend to one voice. When it sleeps by choice it does not rest.
Edge cases it expects: A road it keeps may be used by enemies as freely as by friends. It does not choose who is helped. A wall built across the road is a cut it feels but cannot undo. It cannot keep a road it cannot walk.

### The Reckoning
What it does: A bargain sworn by two or more people with the god's hand held out and the terms spoken aloud binds them in the body, not by force. If a party breaks its word, that party feels a weight at the chest until the matter is mended, and the palm of the hand it swore with darkens, as a bruise does, for all to see. It does not stop the breaking, and it does not punish more than that. The god knows when a bargain sworn before it is broken, and by which party, though not why.
Why this god has it: It wants a people for whom a promise can be seen to work. It says no god ought to have to enforce a fair bargain, only to witness it.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The Reckoning works on the god too. A bargain it swears itself and breaks, it feels twice over, and for the same time it has no use of its powers. And every oath sworn before it is one it feels the weight of kept, so it can never again be in a room where no promise is made. It is never idle of other people's debts.
Edge cases it expects: It cannot force anyone to swear. A bargain sworn under threat does not count. A party that does not know the terms is not bound. A dead party's bargain passes to no one. It does not judge whether the bargain was wise or fair.

### Return to the Stone
What it does: If its body is destroyed, it gathers again at a weighing stone of its own people (a stone on which it has set a true weight with its hand), out of the grain, salt and dust on that stone and the air above it. It takes a season to return, and costs it one item of memory or habit, picked by a logged roll from its own record, never chosen. It asks to find the gap itself and not be told.
Why this god has it: A god of trade expects its goods to be lost in transit and wants a place to be delivered.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The stone must be one it has marked, and while it is gone, whatever it keeps (roads, bargains) lapses. It comes back lighter in the one place it cannot afford to be.
Edge cases it expects: If the stone is broken or carried off, it does not return there. If it has marked more than one, it returns to the nearest. A body held, shut in or scattered is held, not broken, and the power does not apply.

## Trades
**The Barred Door.** It offers this weakness: it cannot open, break or pass a door, gate, hatch or lid that has been shut and fastened from the far side, by any means, for as long as it stays so. Someone on the other side must open it. It can still go around, by any other way. It can open any door fastened from its own side. Nothing it can do will force one barred against it.
What counts: any shut and fastened way, however slight: a bolted door, a barred gate, a latched hatch, a sealed chest too small to enter. It matters only when the thing is to go through, not when the thing is only to be passed. What does not count: a door merely closed, or a way that is blocked by something other than a fastening (a fall of rock, a crowd). How long it lasts: until the fastening is undone from the far side. What ends it: another's hand; the fastening being broken by others; the god going around.
What it asks in return: that the reach of the Kept Road be three days' walk and not one, and that the number of roads it may keep at once be doubled.
It would rather take a heavier drawback than a written fence, and has put this weakness forward as its own.

## The people it will lead
A small people of one to three thousand, settled at a ford where a river is crossed by a road and a second road runs along the bank, in a walled town. The wall has a gate for every road and a gate that is never barred from outside. The people are ruled by a council of elders: a salt weigher, a ferry warden, a keeper of the granary, a drover, a widow who holds the accounts, perhaps a beekeeper whom no one can argue with. The god sits in the council as a guest at the end of the table and speaks when asked, and is heard when it speaks without being asked. Their worship is by household shrines and one in each ward: a flat stone with a scale cut in it, kept by whoever keeps it, with a pinch of salt laid on it on market day. No priests. It does not seek worship beyond that, and distrusts any who offer more.
