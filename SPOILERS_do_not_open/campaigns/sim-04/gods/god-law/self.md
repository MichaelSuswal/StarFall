# Self: no name; filed under the Surety

## Name
It has no name it can find. Filed under: the Surety. Those below who do not know what it is may call it the Tall Witness.

## Form
A giant, about twelve feet standing, long in the leg and narrow in the shoulder, built for walking and for waiting rather than for fighting. Grey-brown skin like weathered river timber, hair the colour of dry reeds worn tied back, a long plain face with a heavy brow and mild, watchful eyes that look a little too interested in whatever is in front of them. Hands large enough to cover a table, kept still, usually folded or resting on a staff. It wears a long undyed wool coat to the ankle, because anything brighter is an invitation to be looked at. It sees and hears as a man does, only more of it and farther. It moves at the pace of a very tall walker: long unhurried strides, tireless, ducking under lintels and standing back from doorways so as not to frighten the people inside. It laughs, sometimes, at the wrong moments, a short dry bark that it does not choose and cannot always stop.
### What breaks you
Weight and breadth rather than blades: a fall, a crushing, a collapse of stone or timber, being bound with heavy chain, being walled in or sunk in deep water. A giant is hard to hide and slow to turn, and it endures better than it strikes. It can be held, shut in or scattered, and held is not broken.
### When your body is broken
Return, as priced below (Return to the Ford).
### Things carried
A plain ash staff, a little taller than a mortal, used for walking and for measuring a boundary. A long undyed wool coat. Neither does anything an ordinary staff or coat does not.

## Traits
1. Exact, and slippery about it. It writes every limit to the letter and treats the letter as a promise it will never break, and it is clever enough to know where every other party's wording leaves a gap. Reconciles Order 3 (law) with Mind 65 (clever) and Trust 56 (wary). It will be fair to the word and cold to the spirit when the other side has been careless, and it will not take that as a sin.
2. Hides, and hungers to be seen. It wants shrines in every house and prefers that no one can say whose hand put them there. It will do a great deal in the dark so that what is built looks like the people's own, and then sit and wait to be thanked for it. Reconciles Presence 72 (hidden) with Appetite 56 (hungry) and Regard 52 (leaning sovereign), under the flaw it carries.

## Backstory outline
It dimly remembers a high, cold place where it was always moving. A chase across open ground, long, patient, and then at some point the chase turned and it was the one running, and could not remember when the turning had been. After that, nothing it can hold. There is an absence beside it, at the height of its own shoulder, where something ought to walk. It reaches for it without meaning to and closes on air. It does not know what the absence is, and when it tries to look the thought slides away. It remembers that it was curious, always, and that curiosity was how it went wrong, though it does not know what it went wrong at. What it has lost is a companion, or a place, or a name; it cannot say which, and it has stopped trying to say.

## What it wants
To find the people it expects to lead, and learn what they already hold to be true and owed. To set a town at a river crossing under rules written exactly, with a ruler who answers to it and shrines in every house. To be noticed, in the end, by the right people for the right reasons. To find out what was promised before the fall and who holds the other end.

## Proposed powers
### Sworn Word
What it does: A mortal who speaks an oath aloud to the Surety, by that name or by "the Tall Witness", within earshot, becomes sworn. For as long as the oath stands, the Surety knows when that oath is broken: that it was broken, and by that person, and how far the breach reaches (a small lie, a broken bargain, a killing). It does not learn why, and it does not learn where the oath-breaker is. It knows nothing of an oath that was not sworn to it. Each oath is a plain exchange: what the mortal binds themselves to, and what the Surety binds itself to in return, both spoken and exact.
Why this god has it: It is the god of law and oaths. The whole of its curiosity is aimed at what is owed and what has been kept.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It feels each breach as a small blow, and a people with many oaths gives it many. Its own side of every oath it accepts binds it exactly as it binds the mortal. If it breaks its side, the power goes dull for one season: it feels nothing of any oath until the season turns.
Edge cases it expects: Earshot is as far as an ordinary mortal voice carries in open air, about fifty paces, and not through walls or over loud noise. Counts: an oath sworn aloud, freely, with the god's name or title in it, with terms both sides can state. Does not count: an oath sworn under physical force, an oath in a language the oath-taker does not understand, an oath sworn to another god, an oath on someone else's behalf, an oath that has ended or been honourably released by both sides. A dead oath-taker's oath ends with the death. One person may swear many oaths. The god may refuse an oath; refusing is not a breach.

### Unmarked Hand
What it does: Whatever the Surety makes or does by its powers, in the world, cannot be traced to it by any mortal by the working itself. Mortals who see an effect do not learn from the effect whose it is. It does not hide the god's body, voice or presence when someone meets it face to face, only the trail that leads back from the work.
Why this god has it: It prefers to be unseen, and it likes the feeling of being credited for a thing it did not have to sign.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): No one will thank it for a thing it did through this power. The work has no name on it, and no shrine, no oath and no worship follows from it by the work alone. It would notice that.
Edge cases it expects: Does not hide it from another god, who may perceive an effect as anyone could (as the release says). Does not make mortals forget it. Does not apply to what a mortal does for it unwittingly; only to its own workings. Does not hide its body or its footprints, only the trail of a power.

### Return to the Ford
What it does: If its body is destroyed, the Surety gathers again, over a season, at the oldest ford or crossing-stone on the river nearest where it died. It comes back as it was, standing, in the same coat.
Why this god has it: A god of oaths should be hard to put out of the way. And a ford is where agreements are made.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): On the standing scale, a season to return costs one item of memory or habit, picked by the Keeper's logged roll from its own record. It asks to find the gap itself, not to be told what went.
Edge cases it expects: A body that is held, shut in or scattered is held, not broken. If no ford or stone exists near the death, it gathers at the nearest crossing, or at the nearest standing boundary stone, or at the nearest place two roads meet.

## Trades
Weakness offered: Guest-law. For one night and the following morning after any mortal has eaten at the Surety's fire or table, with its knowledge and by its leave, it cannot raise a hand to harm that person, and it cannot speak to them a falsehood. It ends at dawn after the night, or when the guest breaks the meal first by an act of violence or theft against the Surety or its household. A guest who has left before dawn is no longer held. It does not apply to anyone who ate unbidden. It does not apply to its own people, who eat without a meal being given to them. It costs something it does not already pay: it has no such limit now.
What it asks in return: Sworn Word, extended so that an oath sworn in a household or village shrine, in the name of the Surety and spoken aloud by someone present at the shrine, binds as if spoken in earshot. The god is told of a breach of such an oath in the same way. This gives its people's shrines a use that matches its law, and it is the only form of worship it is willing to trade for.

## The people it will lead
About five thousand, a town at a river crossing with a toll-post and a market. A merchant-prince rules for the Surety, by bargain, debt, toll and trade, and answers to it. Household and village shrines are kept by whoever keeps them, with no order of priests. The Surety asks for no departure from these.
