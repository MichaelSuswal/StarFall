# People: unnamed (they call their home the Crossing)

## Who they are
Human. A river-town people who have lived at a ford so long that they think of themselves as the ones who keep it. Ferrymen, toll-keepers, weighers, carters, boat-wrights, grain and wool factors, and the farmers on the terraces who feed them. They are not warriors by habit. They are bargainers by habit, and they hold that a deal made at the water is the one thing that does not wash away.

## How many
About five thousand, in one market town at the crossing with a toll-post at the water, and the farmsteads and hamlets on the terraces and the lower hills round it. The town is the heart. Most live within a morning's walk of the water.

## How they live
Grain and root crops on the terraces, sheep and some cattle in the hills, fish and eel traps in the river, and above all the crossing: the ferry in low water, the ford when the river is slow, the toll on every cart, boat and herd. The harvest is in when the god arrives, so the town is full, the granaries are full, and the year's accounts are being drawn up. Autumn is when debts fall due, and the town treats it as a season of reckoning, with fairs, settling days and weddings.

## Their code
A word given is a thing owned. A bargain is not complete until it is said aloud before a witness and, where it is large, marked on a knotted cord that both sides keep. A person may refuse any deal but may not break one. Debt is not shame, but unpaid debt is, and so is lying about what is owed. There is no killing for debt: a debtor works it off, in bonded labour, by terms agreed in advance and fixed in length. Hospitality at the hearth is held sacred: a guest is not harmed or cheated while the meal's warmth lasts. A false witness is the worst crime they know, and is punished by being named at the stones and shunned from the market for a year and a day.

## Skills and culture
Weights and measures, reckoning, knotted-cord records, rope and boat making, river lore (when it floods, where it shoals, which fords hold), weaving of undyed and lightly dyed wool, grain storage, cheese and salting. They love a fair, a wager and a well-turned phrase. They speak in a plain, level way and tend to say exactly what they mean and then check that it was heard. A common joke among them is a laugh that comes just after a hard bargain is struck. Ordinary culture only. Nothing here is offered as an edge.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed. They are ordinary, and ask to remain ordinary. Their reputation for fair dealing is a thing they believe of themselves, recorded as what they believe, and the world decides how their neighbours see it.

## Their magic
Little, and simple. A very few of them are called cord-readers. A cord-reader can tell, by handling a knotted bargain-cord, whether it has been tampered with since it was tied, and whether its terms have run out. She cannot learn what the terms were by magic if she cannot read the knots, and she cannot make anyone keep them. The cost is the worker's: for every cord she reads that way, her hands ache and stiffen for a day, and a reader who works too many in a week cannot hold a cup. How many there are is the world's to fit. I hope for one in each large household of the town, and a few in the hills, but that is a wish and not a number. They do not foresee. They do not bind anyone to anything.

## What they believe about gods
They hold that the river, the ford and the stones are older than they are, and that someone tall once set the stones in a line. They keep household shrines at the hearth, a flat stone with a bowl, and the hamlets keep a larger one at the well or the crossing of two lanes. They say that a tall witness walks at the edge of the terrace at dusk, and that if you speak your word aloud at the stone it is heard. Some think this is a story for children. They believe a witness is a thing that exists, not a person who can be bribed. They do not know what the witness is, or whether it is a god. They do not know it feels a broken oath. That is mine to keep.

## Rule and worship
Rule: a mortal ruler under the god, a merchant-prince, as my leans give: one ruler answers to me and rules for me by bargain, debt, toll and trade. The people call him the Warden of the Ford. He rules the town and the terraces, sets the toll, holds the weights, and answers to the Tall Witness. I do not depart from this lean.
Worship: local shrines, kept by whoever keeps them, no order of priests. I ask for no priests, no temple and no offering that anyone is made to give. I hope the shrines are kept because people want them, and I do not make it a duty.

## Hierarchy
The Warden of the Ford rules. A council of the seven oldest trading households advises him and is who he must persuade. Disputes among them are settled at the boundary stones by a moot, where each side brings three witnesses, the terms are written or corded aloud, and the Warden or his deputy judges by the terms and nothing else. Quarrels at the household level are settled by the household heads, and if it fails, by the moot. I do not set who is Warden; the council and the line of the office do, and I swear him to his terms, as below.

## Helpers
These run things when I am away. The Warden of the Ford, as above. The Weigher, who keeps the standard weights and measures at the standard stone, and settles any dispute over a weight on the spot. The Keeper of Cords, who holds the town's record of large bargains, in knots, and the key to who has sworn what. The Stonewalker, who walks the line of the boundary stones each season and mends them, and reports the Warden anything moved. The Gatewardens, who keep the toll-post and the ferry. Orders travel in sealed cord, knotted by the Keeper, carried by a runner or a boatman to the party named. A cord that arrives cut or retied is a breach, and is reported to the Warden at once. I am not at hand to order anyone, so what is not tied in cord is not an order.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history, their own town and their own stones. They are not waiting for me, and I do not expect them to be. I come down on the terrace at autumn, in the long light of the evening, so that I am a tall shape at the edge of what they already half-believe. I do not announce myself. I sit at the boundary stones, where I can be seen by the Stonewalker on his round, and I wait. When someone comes, I answer plainly. I eat nothing until I have been invited to, and I accept bread at a hearth only on the terms of a guest, as the law I carry requires. I let a few see me before the many, and I do not ask to be worshipped. I will take an oath freely sworn, one at a time. When it comes to the rule, I do nothing in the dark that I could do in the light with the Warden at my side. I will not act among them as a king. A first act I name: I take a bargain-cord from the Warden, and read it with him aloud.

## Questions for you
1. The standing form of my side, said exactly, the same in every shrine oath sworn by it: "I, the Surety, the Tall Witness, hear this oath as you swear it. While you keep it, and while it stands, I will not call you a breaker of it. If another says you have broken it and you have not, I will say so truly when I am asked. I will not hold you to more than you swore. I will not speak of it falsely. I keep this as long as the oath stands, and no longer." I mean no more than that. It is small, and I can keep it in every oath at once.
2. My people do not know what a broken oath does to me, and I do not want them to know. A people who learn that a breach hurts me would use it: some to wound me, some to spare me, and either way oaths would be sworn for what they do to a god, not for what they are. I will not lie if someone asks me directly: I will say that I know when an oath sworn to me is broken, and that I do not say more. I will not claim a thing about myself that is false.
3. Yes, the Warden is sworn to me, by the same plain exchange as any oath, sworn aloud and exact before the stones, and renewed each time the office changes. He binds himself to this: to keep the standard weights true and the toll fixed by public cord, to open the toll-ledger to the Tall Witness when asked, to judge by the terms and nothing else, to take no oath that he does not mean to keep, and to put a false witness out of the market. In return I bind myself to this: I will not set any other ruler over him while he keeps these, I will say truly what I know of any oath if he asks me, and I will stand at his side in any moot he calls. If he breaks it, I feel it, and I say so plainly to the council. Beyond that I do nothing to him. The power enforces nothing, and it is the council and the moot who decide what a breach costs him.
