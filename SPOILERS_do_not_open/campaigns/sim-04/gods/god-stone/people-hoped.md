# People: the Dathri, of the town of Kessmar

I give the people a name, and their town one. The terraces, the hamlets and the houses among them I leave unnamed for play.

## Who they are
Human, a mountain people of the valley I come down above: terrace farmers, goatherds and stone-cutters, broad in the chest and sure on a slope, with weathered faces and a habit of speaking slowly and of weighing a word before it is spent. They wear wool and felt and hide, and keep their hair bound. They have lived in the valley longer than any of them can reckon, and they say so. This is a hope of mine and a belief of theirs, and where it stands is the world's.

## How many
About five thousand, with a town of fitted and dry-laid stone on the valley floor, called Kessmar [f19], and the rest in hamlets along the terraces up the valley sides. I wish them gathered close, and I accept what that costs: they need more food and fuel than a thin people, their growth is slow, and a hard winter bites them.

## How they live
They farm terraces cut on the valley sides, barley, a hardy bean, some root crops, and flax, and they keep goats and sheep on the high summer grass and a few cattle on the valley floor. They cut stone and burn charcoal. They store hard against the winter, and spring is their lean season, when the stores are low and the passes are only now opening. They keep the notches of the far wall as their ways in and out, and what comes to them comes through these. They trade a little, salt and metal being what they most lack, and they do not yet know I mean to seek these for them.

## Their code
Guest-right is sacred: a guest who has eaten at a hearth is not harmed under its roof or on the road to the next hearth. A word given is kept. A stone once laid in a wall by a house is not taken from it. The old are heard first in council and the young do the carrying. A killing within the valley is the gravest wrong, and is answered not by blood but by the killer's labour and goods given to the dead one's house, for a term the council sets.

## Skills and culture
Dry-stone building and terracing, which they do well. Herding on steep ground. Charcoal burning and the working of bronze and soft copper where they have it. Weaving, felting, cheese. They tell long stories in a courtly and roundabout manner, with set forms of address, and they treat a visitor's arrival as an occasion with its own rituals of welcome. They sing at the work. They keep a custom of adding a small stone to a household cairn for every child born, and for every guest of note. Ordinary culture, not priced.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I propose one edge, a small one, and I offer its weakness.

Edge: The Dathri are real masters of the fitting of stone. Their masons read the grain of rock and set a dry wall so true that it stands through frost and earthquake as other peoples' walls do not, and they can raise a terrace, a bridge pier or a gatehouse in half the time and with half the labour another people would need with the same tools. This is a skill of long lore and practice, not a power. It is limited so that it takes nothing from Shaping the Stone: a Dathri crew builds at a mortal crew's pace, with mortal tools, and the thing it builds does not need me, and does not stand because of me.

Weakness I offer: The Dathri are poor farmers by the standards of the lowlands and poor sailors and poor riders. The valley's season is short, its soil thin and stony, and the people carry that in their bodies. Their harvests run close to the edge of their needs. A bad year means hunger, and two bad years in a row mean a death among the old and the youngest. They have no horse-craft to speak of and no skill in the open country beyond the range, and they are slow to adapt when carried out of the mountains. They would notice this each year at the lean end of the winter.

I ask the Keeper to say the full price it would ask, and I will answer yes, no or a counter.

## Their magic
Small, as the world has it, and costing the worker. A few among them, perhaps one in some hundreds, can feel the weight and soundness of stone through their palms: whether a wall will hold, whether a slope will slide, whether a hollow lies behind a face. They are called stone-feelers. It costs the worker numbness in the fingers and hands, which comes on as they use the gift and lasts for as many days as the work was long, so that a stone-feeler who works hard goes a while without feeling in her hands. I name no seeing of what is coming. How many such workers there are is the world's to fit, and the number I gave is a wish.

## What they believe about gods
They believe that gods are wardens of the ways between places, that they come and go through the high passes, and that a god comes to the valley at the thaw when the passes first open. They keep a saying: "what comes through the notch in spring is either a guest or a debt." They do not know what I am. They expect a guest. They are not meek before gods; they treat them as honoured and exacting visitors, owed courtesy and owing it in turn. This is their belief on the morning I arrive. What caused it is the world's.

## Rule and worship
Rule: a council of their own, of elders and headmen of the hamlets and the town, nine in number. The god is heard there and does not command. I hold within the lean as given, and offer no departure. The council's helpers carry out what is agreed.

Worship: local shrines, as the lean says. Each household keeps a cairn of small stones, and each hamlet keeps a larger shrine stone at its edge, kept by whoever keeps it, with no order of priests. They offer a pinch of salt, a drop of milk, or a small stone. They do not worship me by name as yet, and I do not ask them to.

## Hierarchy
The Council of Nine advises and rules. It chooses from among itself a Speaker, who holds the chair and speaks for the people, and who serves for as long as the council will have her. The council has an Elder of Law, who settles disputes. Under it: a Gate-warden, a Master of Works, a Steward of Stores, and the headmen of the hamlets. I advise from where I sit and am heard, and I do not rule. Disputes among the people are settled by the council sitting as a moot. The Elder of Law hears both sides, the Nine speak, and the Speaker gives the judgment, which is almost always a payment in labour, goods or stone. A matter that touches me is brought to me, and I answer it as a guest-friend and not as a king.

## Helpers
They are the offices that keep the valley when I am away or still.
- The Gate-warden keeps the list of names I have given leave to pass the wards, and sees that none is turned who should not be, and sends word to me when a ward is renewed or fails. She is the most important of my helpers.
- The Master of Works directs the stonework and the terraces, and keeps the crews at what they build in my absence.
- The Steward of Stores keeps the winter grain and fuel, and rations them in the lean season.
- The Elder of Law keeps the moot.
Orders travel by runner along the terrace paths, and by a signal fire on a high cairn at the valley rim, with a set of three signals: a guest at the notch, a danger at the notch, and a call to the council. Messages out of the valley go by the notches with a trader or a runner who has been named to pass.

## How the god meets them
I come down on the ledge on the valley's rim, and I stand there at first and do not move. I wish to be seen. I stand through a day in plain view, in the form I have, quiet, and let them come to look. I do not descend in thunder or speak from the sky. On the next morning I walk down by the rock, and I come to the council in the manner of a guest at the thaw, as their saying has it, and I ask for hospitality, and give them my courtesy, and tell them that I am a warden and that I mean to stay and to keep the passes for them if the council will have me. I do not demand worship. I tell them I am a servant of no one, and I do not say that I once was. I will offer them stone-work and the keeping of the ways, and I will ask to be heard in their council.

When I am not among them I will be seen on the rim and at the notches, standing, and I will show them my face when they ask. I will not hide from them. I will not strike one of them. I will not shape their stone against their will, and I will not take a thing from their hearths.

## Questions for you

### 1. With three wards standing you cannot leave your valley. Which thresholds do you mean to ward first, and who among your people is named to pass them?
I mean to ward one first, and not three, because with two standing I cannot shape stone and I wish to build. I will walk the rim before I decide, and then ward the notch in the valley's far wall that I judge to be the widest and most used, the way an army or a stranger would most readily come by, if it is within fifty paces. I wish the other notches left open for the present, and watched, until I know what comes by them. I will add a second ward only when a danger shows itself, and a third only in a time of real threat, and I will lift a ward when the danger passes. I understand that with three I could not leave, and that I cannot pass my own ward.

Those named to pass the first ward are the Gate-warden, the Speaker of the council, and the traders and herders the Gate-warden puts forward and the council approves, each one by name. A named one may lead no one through, so a caravan must be named person by person, and the Gate-warden will keep the list. The rest of my people are turned at the notch as any are, unless named, and I will tell them so and give them my reasons.

### 2. Any word you give under a roof of stone binds you in body. Where does your council sit, and does it know?
The council sits in a moot house of timber and turf, with a roof of wood and walls of earth and timber, and no stone walls, built so by custom long before I came. I will not ask them to rebuild it in stone, and I will not move the council into a stone hall. I mean to give my word to them in that house, or in the open court before it, and I will take care not to give any word of weight under stone. Where a bargain must be given in a stone place, I will step out first.

Does it know? Not yet. I do not tell the council of the Oath. I have the wariness of a gate-keeper, and a weakness told is a lever handed over. I will tell the Speaker alone, after I have seen what she is, and not before. I accept the risk that anyone who learns of it may ask my word where it must bind, and I accept that my wish to keep it quiet may fail.

### 3. You shape stone at the rate of twenty masons and are stiff the day after each day worked. What do you build first, and what do your people build without you?
First I build a channel and a cistern. Spring is the lean season and the meltwater runs loud: I will cut a stone-lined channel to bring it to the terraces and a cistern to hold it against the dry weeks. That gives the people the thing they most need, which is water and a surer harvest, and it earns me their regard. After that, I build the gatehouse at the warded notch, so that the Gate-warden has a place to sit and the ward has a visible door. I mean to work in short spells of three days, with rest between, so that I never come near the seven days that would seize me, and I will tell the Master of Works my schedule.

My people build without me their houses, their walls, their terraces and their shrines, as they have always done, and they do it well. They go on cutting and laying their own stone at a mortal pace, and I do not do for them what they can do themselves. When I am stiff after a day's work, they continue the work I began, and when I am seized, they keep me and keep the valley.
