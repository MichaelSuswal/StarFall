# People: unnamed

I leave the people and their camps unnamed for play. A name that is given is worn, and I would have them choose it. Smaller groups among them, the houses and the boat companies, I leave unnamed the same way.

## Who they are
Human. A river people of the braided country: ford keepers, boatmen, carriers, fishers and guides, who go where the water goes and do not own the banks they pass. They are at about the level the world begins at. They build plank and pitch boats, flat river barges, and skin or reed craft for the shallows. They have wood and bone, flint, copper and a little bronze from trade, and no steel. A seat is a place they come back to in a season, never a place they stay. They are ruled as the leans say, a sacred monarch behind a court, and they know me, if they know me at all, as a stranger who keeps what is told.

## How many
About two and a half thousand. This is smaller than the five thousand the release offers, and I give my reasons for departing. A people that moves must be able to cross a ford in a day, camp in a bend of a river, and feed itself from one reach of water. Every added soul makes the march slower and the camp heavier, and a larger people would be one that no longer moves, which is not what I lean toward. A small people also keeps each confidence close, and keeping confidences is the whole of what I am for. There is no weakness to price here, since a smaller people is not an edge.

## How they live
They move with the water in a long round. They winter at a seat on a sheltered bend where the river runs slow and the banks are high, with houses of timber and turf. At the thaw they take to the boats and the banks and follow the fish and the fords upriver and down. In summer they carry for others: goods, people, animals, across channels that no one else will risk. In autumn, when the rivers are low and clear, they gather their stores, count their dead, and come back toward the seat. They eat fish, river fowl, roots, grain from trade and from small plots sown at the seat and left to grow, and smoked meat. They keep dogs, a few goats and some pack animals. A house is a family of some size and a boat, and a company is the boats that travel together.

## Their code
What is told in confidence is kept. A word given binds, and a word broken is carried by the one who broke it. A stranger at the ford is carried across and asked nothing until they have been carried. The dead are carried to the nearest bank and buried at the water's edge, never left in the open and never taken far from the river. No one is turned from a crossing for owing a debt. A quarrel is not carried across water. These are what they say about themselves. What they do is theirs.

## Skills and culture
Boatbuilding, pitch and cordage, reading water and the shape of a ford, fishing and smoking, herb lore for fevers and wounds, and a long memory kept in cord. They keep their records in knotted cord, one cord to a house, one to a company, one for the line of the throne, and cord is what is carried when an order must travel. They sing in low voices on the water, and their songs are mostly for the dead and for the crossing. They have a saying for most things: that a ford is a promise the river keeps for a season, that what is said at night is carried across by morning, and that a quiet house is never an empty one. They are polite, slow to anger, and slow to forgive, and they are reluctant to be looked at.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed. Their skill at crossing rivers is what they believe of themselves and is recorded as that. How their neighbors cross is the world's. I ask for no edge and offer no weakness.

## Their magic
None asked. If the world fits them a little, I hope it is small and quiet: a worker who can take a fever or a pain into herself for a few hours, so that another may sleep. It should cost the worker what she takes, for as long as she carries it, and she should not be able to refuse the cost. How many there are is the world's, and anything I say about it is a wish. I do not ask for foresight. They would not want it.

## What they believe about gods
They do not hold with gods, as a people. They have a belief that the water carries what is lost to a far bank, and that those who carry the dead are not to be looked at too closely. They have a story that a stranger comes to the ford in some autumn, asking nothing, and that those who tell him a thing find it kept. They say nothing of what the stranger is. Whether they believe the story is theirs. What they think of me, if they ever learn it, is theirs. I ask no rites, and I will not accept any that are pressed on me.

## Rule and worship
As the leans say. A mortal line holds the throne in my name and is held sacred for it. I choose no one for it and I do not name its first holder. A court stands between the throne and the people, and I am reached through it. The line is called, among themselves, the Held, and is sacred for what it keeps: the people's cord, their crossings and their word, and not for what I am. There is no worship. There is no priest of me, no rite for me, and no shrine. Where the people hold something sacred, it is the keeping of what is told. If they ever bring rites, I will not stop them and I will not ask for them.

## Hierarchy
The Held rules, and rules for life, from within the line, which passes by the Held's own choosing from among the line's children and kin, with the court's assent. The court advises and stands between the Held and the people. It has the following offices.
- The Hearers: a small body, drawn by lot and sworn, who hear confidences and hold them.
- The Warden of the Ford: sets the route, reads the water, and names the crossings.
- The Keeper of the Cord: keeps the records, the line and the tallies.
- The Marshal of the Bank: guards the march and the camp.
- The Reeve of Stores: keeps the grain, the smoked stock, the pitch and the rope.
- The Boatwright: keeps the boats and the barges.
Disputes are settled by the Hearers. Both sides tell their account in confidence to a Hearer, who tells neither side the other's, and gives a ruling that names no secret. If the ruling is not taken, the Held gives the final word, and if the Held's is not taken, it is settled by lot. No one among them carries a feud across a ford.

## Helpers
The offices above run things when I am absent: the Warden of the Ford sets the course, the Keeper of the Cord keeps the count, the Marshal keeps the camp, the Reeve and the Boatwright keep stores and boats, and the Hearers keep the peace of the house. Orders travel by knotted cord. A cord is made at the court, handed to a runner or a boat, and carried along the bank or the water to each company, where the head of the company reads it and ties a return. A cord left unanswered is a cord not understood, and is sent again. Helpers who wish to leave may leave, and I will not hold them. New ones may come.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history, and they are waiting for no one. I do not arrive with them and I do not arrive as one of them. I come down on the gravel bar at the ford, in autumn, as a stranger with a pole, and I carry across whoever asks to be carried, and ask nothing. I am not announced. I do not say what I am unless I am asked and unless it matters. I let the court learn me slowly, in the way their own story says a stranger is learned, through what is told to me and kept. I do not take rites. I do not ask to be reached by anyone but through the court once it is known, and until it is known I am a stranger at the ford.

## Questions for you
1. A people that tells its court what it cannot carry: who among them hears a confidence, and which of those are brought to you to seal?

The Hearers hear confidences, and any of them may hear one, since they are drawn by lot and sworn. A person may also tell the Held. A Hearer does not tell me what was told to them. They bring me no one's secret. What is brought to me is the person: anyone who has a confidence that they would have sealed by one who cannot be questioned, and who wishes to be sealed, may ask a Hearer to take them to me. The Hearer brings them, and no more, and the person tells me themselves, within a spoken voice, and asks that it be kept. I take nothing that is not offered. I do not ask for confidences, and I do not ask the Hearers who has one. The court does not choose who is brought. The person does.

2. After you open what was shut, your face goes wrong for a time. Where are you in those days, and does your people see you then?

I go apart. I do not open what is shut in the midst of the people unless there is a reason that cannot wait. When I must, I go to a boat or a bank away from the camp, with my pole and my cord, and I stay there until the dawn or the days have passed, and I keep out of sight. I do not tell the people why. The court is told by cord that I am at the water, and no more. If someone sees me in those days, they see a man whose face has not learned itself, and what they make of it is theirs. I do not open the tombs of the people without being asked and without the court's leave. For the seven days after a tomb door, I stay away from the people altogether.

3. Your people moves and has no fixed seat. Where does the court sit when the people is on the move, and how are you reached through it?

The court moves with the people, in the lead barge of the march. When the people halts, the court sits in the middle of the camp, in a tent that is raised at each halt in the same pattern, with the Held's seat at the center, the Hearers at the right hand and the Warden at the left. A lantern is hung from a pole at the head of the march and kept lit. I am not there as a matter of course. I walk the banks and the fords alone, at my own pace, and I come to the lantern after dusk when I have reason to. Anyone who wishes to reach me goes to the Warden of the Ford, who asks a Hearer, who sends a cord to the lantern. If I am within reach, I come. If I am not, the cord waits. I am reached through the court and in no other way, and I am met in person and in no other.
