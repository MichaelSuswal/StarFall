# People: unnamed

They have a name for themselves, which we leave to them and to play. Among themselves they say they are the people of the Bend. We will call them that here and keep the name loose. Their town is unnamed, and its wards, houses and wells are left unnamed for play.

## Who they are
Human. A river people who have lived at the bend in the middle reach for longer than they can count, behind a wall they keep in good repair. They are a settled, close, careful folk, used to the river's moods and to each other. They farm the wet meadows, fish and trap, keep ducks, geese and a few cattle, and cross the river by ferry and by a ford in the low season. They are wary of strangers and generous to guests once a stranger has been seen to drink and eat among them. They are crowded, and they like it.

## How many
About five thousand, within the wall and in the fields and landings around it. We hope for more in time, and we know a larger people costs what the world makes it cost. We ask for none of that tonight. We ask for five thousand and for room to gather more.

## How they live
Within a walled town at the bend, built close, with narrow lanes running down to the water. The wells and a spring are inside the wall, and the ford is under the wall's eye, with a water gate where the lane meets the river. Houses stand in groups around their own well, and a group keeps its well in turn with the others. Outside the wall are the meadows, fish weirs, the ferry landing and a few outlying farms. They eat river fish, grain, beans, eggs, fowl and a little meat. They make cloth and baskets and pots, and they trade along the river with those upstream and down.

## Their code
Plain, short and spoken. Do not foul the water above where you drink. Pour back the first draw of spring. Give water to any who ask it plainly, friend or stranger. Keep your own house's well and take your turn at another's. Stand your place on the wall when the horn sounds. The captains do not fight each other inside the wall. A quarrel is settled at the water gate before the sun is down, and no one carries it past the dark. These are said aloud at the spring gathering by the law speaker, and each limit is said exactly, the same each year, so that no one may say they did not hear it.

## Skills and culture
Skill with boats, ferries, fish weirs, nets and ropes. Skill with wells, channels and ditches, and with the settling of water in jars before it is drunk. Skill with wattle and clay, timber and reed. Basketry and cloth. Song in the lanes when the river is high. The hearth bowl: each house sets a bowl of water by its fire and pours it back into the river at the turn of the year. Flood marks are cut into the stone of the water gate and read aloud each spring. They keep tallies by notches on sticks and cords, not writing.

## Edges and their weaknesses
**Edge: clean water by custom.** Their wells are kept, their drawing is done above the lanes where waste goes, and they settle and strain water as a habit. Because of this, the sicknesses that come in the water, flux and fever, are rarer among them than among their neighbors, and fewer die of them. We say this is a real edge, though a small one, and we price it as the smallest thing on a card.
**The weakness we offer: a people of the wet.** They do badly away from the river. In dry country they tire and sicken sooner, they have few horses and little skill with them, and their grain stores are poor for a long march. A summer when the river runs low frightens them more than an army would. They will not easily march far from water, and a war away from the river is hard for them to win. We think they would feel this, and so would we.

## Their magic
Rare and simple, and it costs the worker. Some few women among them, and now and then a man, are called bowl readers. A bowl reader sits with a bowl of water drawn from a well and can tell from it what has already befallen that well: whether it is fouled, whether something has died in it, whether sickness has passed through it, and how long ago. She sees only what has already happened to that water and cannot see what is coming. It costs her. For a day after she cannot drink, her hands are cold to the wrist for days, and if she does it often she grows thin. How many bowl readers there are is the world's to fit, and we wish for a handful and no more. We do not want them everywhere.

## What they believe about gods
They say a mist lives at the ford and is kind to those who ask it plainly. They set a bowl of water out for it at the turn of the year and pour back the first draw of spring. They do not think of it as a god, or as one that rules them, but as the ford's own keeping, like the stone at the spring. Old women say that if a child asks the river plainly for water at the river, it answers. They say it as a saying and a comfort. They do not know it as a binding on us, and we want it left that way. They do not know our nature, and we do not wish them to. Whatever began this belief is the world's.

## Rule and worship
Within our leans. A warlord rules the town under us, through a war band and its captains. Worship is local shrines: a bowl at each hearth, a stone at each spring, a mark at the water gate. These are kept by whoever keeps them. There is no order of priests. Our reason for accepting both is plain: we are cautious, we are hidden, and we would rather be the mist at the ford than a face in a hall.

We ask one thing. The captains are drawn from several houses, so that no one of them holds the wall alone. The warlord is chosen from among the captains by the captains, with our word, not by blood.

## Hierarchy
The warlord rules under us. Under him are the captains of the several houses, each answering for a ward and a share of the wall. The law speaker recites the code and holds a moot at the water gate to settle quarrels, with three well wardens as her fellows. The well wardens keep the wells, one for each group of houses, and hold the first say in any dispute over water. The ferry keeper holds the ford and the landing. The water warden stands between us and the warlord.

## Helpers
The offices that run things when we are away:
- **The water warden.** Our voice to the warlord. She meets us at the water gate at dawn and dusk, hears what we say, carries it to the warlord and carries his answer back. She keeps the stone token of the ford, which the warlord knows.
- **The warlord.** Holds the wall and the war band, and rules in our name.
- **The captains.** Each holds a ward and a share of the wall.
- **The law speaker.** Keeps the code, holds the moot at the water gate and speaks the limits aloud each spring.
- **The well wardens.** Keep the wells and settle water disputes at the first hearing.
- **The ferry keeper.** Holds the ford and the landing, and, in our wish, carries the Cupful.

When we are away, the warlord and the captains run the wall, and the law speaker and the wardens run the lanes. No one acts for us in the mist. We ask nothing of them in our absence beyond keeping the water and the wall.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history, their wall and their smoke. We come down at the bend in spring flood and do not go in. For some days we read the river, up and down as far as we can, and we say nothing to anyone. Our first act, once we have read it, is to clean the wells of the town one at a time, beginning at the water gate, without being seen. We will not enter the hall, we will not take tribute and we will not punish. We will let the water warden find us in the mist at the gate at dawn, and we will speak to her there. We prefer to be a thing they half believe in and cannot catch at anything. If someone cries out in the water, we turn to the cry, and then we are seen, and we cannot help it.

## Questions for you
1. Your Cupful may be carried by a mortal. Is there anyone among your people who would carry it for you, and what do they think they are carrying?

The ferry keeper, a woman of an old ferry house who has kept the ford through many floods and has not been known to boast. We hope she will carry it. She would think she is carrying a charm of the ford's water, a keeping gourd of the kind her house has always carried for a safe crossing, and we would let her think so. We do not say more to her than that. We do not hold her to it. We set who she is and we do not promise what she will do.

2. You cannot refuse a plea for water made in your hearing at water you have touched. Do your people know this of you, and if they do, who is allowed to ask?

They do not know it, and we hope they will not. They hold the saying, that if a child asks the river plainly for water the river answers, and they take it as a comfort and not as a binding. Anyone may ask water of the river and of the wells, as the code says, and it is no secret that water is given to any who ask. What they do not know is that we cannot refuse, and that anyone who asked aloud, plainly, in need, within our hearing, would have us. We would rather the water warden and the ferry keeper did not learn it. If they do, we will deal with it then. We do not name anyone who is allowed to ask, because we do not wish it known that there is a rule.

3. You mean a warlord to hold the wall while you stay in the mist at the ford. How do your orders reach him, and how does he know they are yours?

Through the water warden. She meets us at the water gate at dawn and dusk, we speak to her in the mist, and she carries what we say to the warlord and his answer back. We do not go into the hall. He knows the orders are ours because she carries the stone token of the ford, which was in the keeping of her office before we came and which he knows, and because he has seen the mist at the water gate and, once, our face in it. We keep the orders few and plain, and we say them the same way each time. We do not claim he will obey. We hope he will.
