# People: the Merrowen, of Vesthallow

The people call themselves the Merrowen, "the folk of the mere", and their walled seat Vesthallow. Smaller places and groups among them are left unnamed for play.

## Who they are
Human. A fen and forest people of the rise at the fen's edge, long settled, reed-cutters, eel-takers, charcoal burners, woodcutters, shepherds on the near hills, and the smiths, weavers and potters of a small walled town. They are dark-haired and pale-skinned for the most part, used to mist, damp and early dark, and unhurried in speech. They are not a conquering people. They are a keeping people, wary of strangers and generous to those who have been tried.

## How many
About five thousand, as given. About two thousand within the walls of Vesthallow, and about three thousand in the steadings scattered round it and along the fen's edge, each steading a day's walk or less from the next. I would have preferred fewer, for I lean toward a small people, but I take the number as given and ask the Keeper to price its cost in food and fuel.

## How they live
Within the walls, a market square, a hall of the court, a granary, a few score houses of timber and daub, and lanes narrow enough that two lamps held across them touch. Outside the walls, steadings of a longhouse and a few outbuildings each, with fields on the drier ground, eel weirs and reed beds in the fen, and charcoal stacks in the wood. They burn tallow, rushlight and oil in a quantity unusual among peoples of their level, and they know it is dear. Winter is hard on them and the long nights harder; the hearth is the center of every house.

## Their code
Keep your word. Keep your lamp lit for the one who walks in last. Do not turn from your door anyone who stands at the edge of its light and asks for nothing. Do not swear in the dark; an oath is sworn by lamplight or not at all. Do not wake a sleeper to settle a quarrel. Hospitality is a duty, and its abuse is the gravest wrong.

## Skills and culture
Eel and reed work, basketry, charcoal, tallow and rushlight making, fen-boat building, timber framing, weaving of a coarse dark wool, plain pottery, and the lore of the fen and the wood. They keep long, slow stories told at the hearth in winter, and they sing at their wicks. The household lamp is lit at dusk and kept through the night in every house, in memory of the dead and for the traveler who may come; this is a custom and not a worship, and it costs them oil and tallow they can ill spare in a bad year. They count the year from the first hard frost. Their craft is ordinary for the world's present level: no steel, no wheel beyond the cart.

## Edges and their weaknesses
**Edge: the dark eye.** The Merrowen see in dim light better than other peoples. They work, herd and move on fen paths by starlight and the light of a rushlight, and they are not lost in a moonless night or a dense mist. This is the smallest edge I ask, about as much as the smallest power on a card.
**Weakness offered: the glare.** In strong daylight, and in summer especially, the Merrowen are dazzled and slow at work in the open, squint, suffer headache and eye sickness in long bright days, and are worse at distance work and fine work in full sun than other peoples. They keep shaded hours and work early and late in summer. They would notice it every season.

I offer no other edge. Their lamps, their code and their hospitality are customs and I ask no price for them.

## Their magic
Few have it, and I leave the number to the world. They call it wick-singing. A worker, nearly always a woman of middle years, sits with a lit wick and sings low over a sufferer, and the sufferer's pain eases and a heavy, dreamless sleep comes on them. It does nothing more. It cannot heal a wound, cannot kill, cannot compel, cannot see what is coming, and it does not touch dreams. It costs the worker: for each night she sings, she loses a night's sleep of her own, and the cost builds, so that a worker who sings too many nights in a row grows grey, hollow-eyed, forgetful, and at the last sees waking-dreams she cannot tell from the day. A worker knows her limit and the wise ones keep it.

## What they believe about gods
They have no named god. They say there are watchers in the night who keep the sleepers, and that the lamp in the window is for them as much as for the dead. They believe the watchers come out of the wood in the dark, seldom and without announcement, and that one does not stare at them but leaves a light. They tell of old nights when a watcher came to a house and was not turned away, and what the house had of it after. What caused the saying is the world's. They know nothing of me. When I come they do not wait for me by name; they wait, in the way of a people who have always kept a light, for whoever comes.

## Rule and worship
I take the rule given: a sacred monarch behind a court. A mortal line holds the throne in my name and is held sacred for it, and a court stands between the throne and the people, so that I am reached through it. The line is called the Wick, and the one on the throne is the Wickholder, who sits in the hall of Vesthallow. The Wickholder is held sacred in the sense that their person may not be struck, their hall may not be entered armed, and they keep the great lamp of the town, which is never let to go out while they live.
I take the worship given: local shrines. Household and village shrines, a lamp on a sill or a niche, kept by whoever keeps them, no priests, no order, no temple. The great lamp in the hall is the only large one and it is kept by the court, not by priests.

## Hierarchy
The Wickholder reigns and holds the throne, and rules little by themselves. The court advises and carries out the Wickholder's word: the **Voice**, who speaks to the people for the Wickholder and for me and is the one who is reached when I am sought; the **Marshal of the Wall**, who keeps the walls and the muster; the **Reeve of the Steadings**, who speaks for the people outside the walls and collects and carries the tithe; the **Steward of Oil and Tallow**, who keeps the stores, the great lamp and the market; and the **Hearthward**, who settles disputes. A council of the heads of the oldest houses may sit with the court on grave matters. Under me, the Wickholder rules; under the Wickholder, the court; under the court, the steadings.

Disputes among them are settled by the Hearthward, in lamplight, at the gate of the hall. Both parties sit with a lit wick before them and each speaks in turn without interruption; no one swears in the dark, and no one is woken to give an answer. The Hearthward gives a judgement, and the Wickholder may confirm or soften it but not harden it. Blood-price is paid in salt, oil and eel, and exile is the heaviest penalty.

## Helpers
They are the court itself: the Voice, the Marshal of the Wall, the Reeve of the Steadings, the Steward of Oil and Tallow and the Hearthward. They run things when I am absent. They are loyal by default, and I reserve the right to dismiss any of them. Orders travel by runner on the fen paths, by cart on the dry road, and at night by beacon-lamps, a chain of shuttered lanterns on the steadings and the wall, which can carry a few plain signals from steading to steading in a night: all well, danger, come, stay.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history, and not waiting for anything in particular. I come down at the wood's edge above the fen, in the dusk, at the end of the day, and do not go in. I stand at the edge of the light of an outer steading, as the old saying has it, and ask nothing. I wish to be met first by one person, in the course of an ordinary evening, and not by the court. I show myself to them as I am, because I will not lie to the first mortal I meet. After that I withdraw to the wood, and I enter dreams only as the questions below say. What they do after that is theirs.

## Questions for you
1. When you land, no lamp is lit for you anywhere. How does the first one come to be lit: who lights it, and why would they?

   I hope it is lit by the one I first meet, at the edge of an outer steading, and not because I ask. I will ask only to stand at the edge of the light and be let be. A mortal who keeps a lamp for the one who walks in last, and who sees one stand at the edge of it, may light a second lamp on the sill for the stranger; I hope she says my name aloud as she does it, as one names a guest, and that is enough. I do not command it and I do not promise it. If it is not so, I wait, and I do not press.

2. Those whose dreams you enter feel you near for the rest of their lives. Among your people, whom do you mean to enter, and whom not?

   I mean to enter the Wickholder once, at the accession, and after that only when it matters and the court cannot carry it: a danger from the fen or the wood, a flood, a sickness, a stranger at the gate. I mean to enter the Voice, because the Voice stands between me and the throne and must know that the voice behind the court is not the court's own. I may enter the Hearthward in a grave judgement, if the matter is mine to know. I do not mean to enter children, whose lives are long and whose weight would be long, nor the sick or the sleepless, nor the wick-singers while they sing, nor any who have done me no wrong and asked nothing. I do not mean to enter the whole people, nor to speak to anyone twice who need not be spoken to twice, for a god who is felt by everyone is known by everyone, and I would be seen. I will not enter a sleeper to harm them or to compel them, and I will not use the dream to make them serve.

3. A mortal line holds the throne in your name with a court between. Does the one on the throne know you by sight, by dream, or only by the court's word?

   By dream, and then by the court's word. The Wickholder knows me from the one dream of accession, in which I show a figure and speak low, and not by sight; they feel me near, as a weight at the back of the mind, for the rest of their life, and are not told what it is. The Voice knows the same. All the rest of the court and the people know me by the Voice's word and by the lamp on the sill. No one but the first mortal I meet at the wood's edge has seen me waking, and I do not mean that to be known.
