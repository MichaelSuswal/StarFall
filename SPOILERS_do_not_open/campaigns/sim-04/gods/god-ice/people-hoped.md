# People: unnamed

They will name themselves. I give no name to them or to their seat, nor to the hamlets and steadings round it. A name I give a place others already name is only my name for it.

## Who they are
Human. A valley people of the cold coast and the high ground, herders, fishers and carriers, who have wintered here long enough to keep accounts of it. They keep a walled seat at the lower part of the long valley and steadings scattered along its sides, each apart from the others, each a day or less from the wall.

## How many
About five thousand, the usual number. Most live in the steadings and come to the seat to trade, to pay tolls, to hear the Reading and to settle quarrels. Perhaps a thousand live inside the wall in winter. This is a wish. The world counts.

## How they live
They keep sheep and hardy cattle on the valley sides, fish the lake and the grey sea by hook, net and weir, hunt seal and hare, cut peat and what wood the lower valley gives, and carry salt fish, tallow, wool and fur up and down the valley and along the coast. Winters are long and their stores are the whole of their politics. The first snow is a reckoning. The thaw is another. They build in timber and turf and local stone, and the wall is of stone and earth with timber works on top. No steel. They work bronze, copper, bone and horn.

## Their code
A thing said aloud before witnesses is owed. A debt is not a shame but a thread, and a person with no debts and no creditors is thought poor. No one is judged unheard. No one is turned from a door in the first month of snow. A false weight is a worse crime than a stolen coin, because a thief takes once and a false weight takes every day. A person who breaks a plain promise is called "a cold hand," and is not trusted to hold a rope.

## Skills and culture
Weaving, tanning, tallow and salt work, smoking and drying fish, sled and ski making, sound bookkeeping on notched sticks and knotted cords, weights and measures kept in the seat and checked at the first snow. A tradition of the spoken contract: a bargain is recited by both parties and repeated by a witness. Songs are mostly accounts, and the best singers are those who can recite a year of debts without a slip. Ordinary culture, unpriced.

## Edges and their weaknesses
One edge, which I propose in answer to question 1.

**The Prince's Term.** The merchant-prince may speak a term in my name and under my seal, face to face, to one mortal who comes before him, and if that mortal agrees aloud and understands the words, the term holds them for the stated years, as mine would, with a breach felt as the same creeping cold. His reach is narrow: only terms of debt, toll, tally, a promise to deliver or to carry, a bargain of trade or of service. Never a term of the person's body, name, kin, faith or movement. Never to a child too young to understand, a god, or anyone under threat of force, who is not bound. He may have no more than forty of these standing at once. He does not bind my people as a mass. He binds one mortal at a time and the mortal must say yes.

Weakness offered: the Prince carries what he binds. Every term he speaks stands as cold in his own belly, as mine do in mine. With more than twelve standing he is slow, at half his pace, and with more than forty he can bind no more until some end. He cannot lie inside a term he speaks, and if he breaks the letter of one the cold comes into him until he mends it. The people see him slow in winter and know why. I judge this a cost the Prince and his house would notice every day.

I offer no second edge and no second weakness. I do not ask the Prince's terms to count against my own twelve or forty. I ask that they do not.

## Their magic
Little, and it costs the worker. A few among the people, mostly women past middle age, have the Ice Reading: by laying a bare hand on ice, packed snow or frozen ground she can tell what has crossed it in the last days, how many, how heavy, and which way, never why and never who. The cost to the worker is her hand. For as many hours as she read, the hand is numb and clumsy, and a long reading leaves it so for a day. The number of such workers is the world's. I wish for a few and I would not mind if they were fewer.

## What they believe about gods
My wish, and nothing more: that on the morning I come they hold that a winter judge comes down with the first snow when the year is owed, and that it is neither kind nor cruel but exact. They already have their own history and customs, and I do not set what caused them. They may hold other gods or none, and their beliefs about me are theirs. I am content to be judged by what I do.

## Rule and worship
Within my leans. A merchant-prince rules under me by bargain, debt, toll and trade. He keeps the seat, the weights, the granary books and the tolls on the valley road and the harbor. He answers to me and rules for me. He is chosen by the seat's houses of trade and the heads of the steadings, for life, and I may release him. A priesthood, the Keepers of the Rite, keeps my rite and has standing among the people, with offerings brought freely and no tithe by law.

The rite is called the Reading. At the first lasting snow and at the first thaw, the Keepers read aloud before the gathered people the standing customs of the year, the weights, the debts that have been settled, the judgments given and the terms made, so that all hear what is owed to whom. Offerings are salt, tallow and lamp oil left at a stone by the lake. No one is required to bring any.

## Hierarchy
The merchant-prince rules. The Keepers advise him on custom and read the record. A Council of the Houses, of the older trading families and the heads of steadings, advises him on trade, tolls and levies. The Keepers settle disputes of custom and word. The Prince settles disputes of debt and toll. Disputes that go beyond both come to me at the lake after first ice, in the open, with the standing record read out.

## Helpers
Five offices run things when I am absent:
- The Prince's Reeve of Tolls, who keeps the road and harbor tolls and the weights.
- The Warden of Stores, who keeps the granary and the fuel and gives out relief in a hard winter.
- The High Keeper, who leads the Keepers and keeps the record of judgments and terms.
- The Wall-Warden, who keeps the wall, the gates and the watch.
- The Courier of the Steadings, who carries word to every steading by runner, sled, or signal fire as the season allows.

Orders from me go to the High Keeper and the Prince together, spoken aloud and written by the Keepers. They go out to the steadings by the Courier. Nothing that binds a person is sent by messenger. It is spoken to them.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history, waiting for the first snow, and I arrive to them as it falls. I come down the valley on foot. My first act, which is my conduct and not a promise about the place, is to stop outside the wall at dusk and ask the first person who meets me what is owed in this place. I do not speak a judgment and I do not use Winter's Reach on the first night. I let myself be seen. In the time I am not among them I will not use Winter's Reach on their fields or their wells, I will not bind anyone who has not heard me, and I will not enter the hall of the seat uninvited. I may be seen at the lake after first ice. What I do beyond that is for play.

## Questions for you
1. The Prince's Term is proposed above as my edge, with the weakness I offer: the Prince carries each term as cold in his own belly with his own count of twelve and forty, and is bound to the letter of what he speaks. He may speak it only to one mortal who comes before him, only for debt, toll, tally, carriage, trade and service, only for a stated number of years, never to a child, a god, or one under threat of force. I ask that his count stand apart from mine, and I ask the Keeper to price it and say if more is needed.
2. In the seven days I am slow after a working of cold, the High Keeper and the Prince speak for me together, and nothing that binds a person is sent in my name until I can speak it myself. My people know why I am slow. The frost is mine and they see it, and the Keepers say plainly that the Beast is carrying its work and will speak again when it is lighter.
3. The rite is the Reading, described above. When I laugh at a judgment, the Keepers do not hush it or explain it away. The High Keeper writes the judgment into the record with the word "marked" beside it, the Prince is asked to look again at what the judgment cost the one least to blame, and he may make good the loss from his own purse if he sees fit. They do not call my laugh cruel and they do not call it just. They call it marked.
