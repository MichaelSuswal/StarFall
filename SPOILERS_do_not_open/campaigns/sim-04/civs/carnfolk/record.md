# People: the Carnfolk

Agreed record, creation night 3a, 2026-11-22. The god gave no name; they call themselves the Ford folk and their town the Ford, and the roads call them the Carnfolk of Carnford [f39]. Wards, houses and hamlets are unnamed until play names them.

## Who they are
Human. A town people of Carnvale [r10] at the ford where the Carn Road [f33] meets the Bank Road [f34]: ferrymen, carters, drovers, salt weighers, granary keepers, weavers, potters, bee keepers, with a deep and tedious love of counting.

## How many
About 5,000, the number the god took as the release set it, most within the wall and the rest on farms and hamlets within a morning's walk. The usual number, so no cost of size.

## How they live
A walled town with a gate for every road. Market at the stone; accounts on knotted cords and notched sticks; a harvest fair. Grain, cattle, bees, the ferry and the roads.

## Their code
"Weigh it twice and say it once." A promise at the market stone is kept. A cheat in weights is shamed, made to make whole and shut out of the market for a season. No one is held as a pledge or for a debt. A traveler gets water and a look at the scale before anything else. "The honest man has nothing to bar."

## Skills and culture
Ferrying, cart building, droving, salt weighing, granary keeping, weaving, pottery, bee keeping, counting songs. Ordinary, unpriced.

## Edges and their weaknesses
Agreed in one round (ledger L-0026). One for one.
- Edge, the kept scales: weights and measures agreed by the whole town, kept true by a keeping house with standard stones and sealed measures. Traders choose their crossing and market where they can, and strangers' disputes settle there with less quarrel: the town's trade stands one step above what a town of its size and place would have. By hand, without the god; it neither gives to nor takes from the god's Fair Weight. How far the name has in truth travelled is the world's.
- Weakness, open gates: no standing watch, no paid fighting men, no gate barred by day against a peaceful comer. Against a raid or any surprise, every contest on the first day is one step against them; from the second day the wards stand as any town's would.

## Their magic
"The tongue for salt": by tasting a sick person's sweat, spit or water she can tell what is wrong in the body now, never what will come of it. It costs her taste for a day after each working, and her mouth for a season if she does it too often; under that the one cost every worker pays runs slowly. Fitted by the world: about ten among them, where the god wished three or four (ledger L-0027).

## What they believe about gods
That the great ones keep the world's accounts. "A fair god weighs the same for every man." A fair-day joke: "When the harvest is in, a tall one will come down the hill and ask to see the scale." They expect no god and do not know this one.

## Rule and worship
A council of seven elders, the chair passing each autumn; a matter is agreed when none of the seven will stand against it. A flat stone with a scale cut in it in each house and a larger one in each ward; a pinch of salt on market days. No priests.

## Hierarchy
The council and its chair; ward elders who settle small quarrels at the ward stone; the keeping house, where a quarrel is weighed; then the council. Blood is settled by payment and work. Exile from the market is the heaviest common penalty.

## Helpers
The Tallyman; the Keeper of the Standard Stones; the Gatewardens, one to a gate; the Road Reeve; the Ferry Warden; the Granary Keeper. Orders go by knotted cord and runner, told aloud while the cord is shown. The offices exist and are filled on the morning the god lands. Its people do not yet know it as their god, so each office runs on the people's own custom and becomes the god's helper as its holder hears the god, in play. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away. The wicket the god asked for in the river gate is the town's to keep or refuse; heard, not promised.

## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history. The god comes down on the Carn Road [f33] on the low hill above the ford at harvest, unknown to them, and by its own conduct means to walk down in daylight and ask to see the market scale.

## The world's side
History: the crossing is as old as the old roads; one of them runs straight under the Carn Road [f33], and there is a vault under it in the hills (Carn Vault [f40]) that drovers know only as a hollow sound. Neighbors: Dunmarrow [r07] to the north, whose kings once took toll here and no longer can; Carnmouth [f42] at the river's mouth; the shore village of Ammot [f68] up river; drovers from the valleys south over the Fell Way [f43]. The trouble the god did not ask for: Dunmarrow's [r07] court has sent a letter reviving an old claim to the ford's toll, and the council has not answered it.

- Carnmouth [f42] and the Western Sea [r44]: seen by boatmen. Ammot [f68]: seen by carters. Lake Hesh [r28]: reported.
- The Carn Hills [r08] and Averby [f29]: seen by drovers. Dunmarrow [r07] and Hallisk [f35]: reported.
- Heatherfell [r11] and the Fell Way [f43]: seen by drovers as far as Withyhead [r13]. Sallowdene [r12]: reported, as where the best tapers come from.

## What the old tell (added creation night 3b, 2026-11-22)
Written from `deep-history.md`. What is told, and where the telling is wrong. Nothing agreed on Night 3a is changed.
They tell that the ford is older than the roads and that the first scale in the town was set up by a king's tolltaker and kept true after the king's men left. Right in the main: Osmer of Dunmarrow [r07] took toll here about 200 years before the god came, and the footings of his toll-house are under the market. They tell that the straight road under the Carn Road [f33] was laid in one night. Wrong. The hollow place in the hills (Carn Vault [f40]) they hold to be a robbers' cellar, long since emptied. Wrong: it has never been opened. Their old remember the list-singers going down the Bank Road [f34] to Carnmouth [f42] twenty years before the god came, and the town tallied them: forty-one.
