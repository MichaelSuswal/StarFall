# People: the Tolleners

Agreed record, creation night 3a, 2026-11-22. The god gave no name; they call their home the Crossing, and the road calls it Tollen [f27] and them the Tolleners. Hamlets and houses are unnamed until play names them.

## Who they are
Human. A river-town people of the Lornlands [r06] who have kept the crossing where the Odran [f02] meets the Lorn [f01] so long that they think of themselves as its keepers: ferrymen, toll-keepers, weighers, carters, boat-wrights, grain and wool factors, and the farmers of the terraces. Bargainers by habit.

## How many
About 5,000, the usual number: one market town with a toll-post at the water, and farmsteads and hamlets on the terraces and lower hills, most within a morning's walk.

## How they live
Market, toll and ferry; grain and wool; boats on the slow rivers. The year turns on the autumn fair and the settling of accounts.

## Their code
A word given is a thing owned. A bargain is complete when said aloud before a witness and, where large, knotted on a cord both sides keep. One may refuse any deal but may not break one. Debt is no shame; unpaid debt is. No killing for debt: bonded labour by terms fixed in advance. A guest is not harmed or cheated while the meal's warmth lasts. A false witness is named at the stones and shunned from the market a year and a day.

## Skills and culture
Weights and measures, reckoning, knotted-cord records, rope and boat making, river lore, weaving, grain storage, cheese and salting. They love a fair, a wager and a well-turned phrase. Their name for fair dealing is what they believe of themselves. Ordinary, unpriced.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed, none priced.

## Their magic
Cord-readers: by handling a knotted bargain-cord, whether it has been tampered with since it was tied, and whether its terms have run out. It costs her hands, aching and stiff for a day for each cord; under that the one cost every worker pays runs slowly. Fitted by the world: about ten among them, fewer than the god hoped (ledger L-0027).

## What they believe about gods
That the river, the ford and the stones are older than they are, and that someone tall once set the stones in a line. That a tall witness walks the terrace at dusk, and that a word spoken aloud at the stone is heard. Some think it a children's story. They do not know the god.

## Rule and worship
A merchant-prince, the Warden of the Ford, rules town and terraces by bargain, debt, toll and trade, chosen by the council and the line of the office. Household shrines at the hearth and a larger one at the well or lane crossing of each hamlet, kept by whoever keeps them. No priests.

## Hierarchy
The Warden of the Ford; a council of the seven oldest trading households, whom he must persuade; a moot at the boundary stones where each side brings three witnesses and the Warden or his deputy judges by the terms and nothing else.

## Helpers
The Warden of the Ford; the Weigher; the Keeper of Cords; the Stonewalker; the Gatewardens of the toll-post and ferry. Orders go in sealed cord by runner or boatman. The offices exist and are filled on the morning the god lands. Its people do not yet know it as their god, so each office runs on the people's own custom and becomes the god's helper as its holder hears the god, in play. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away. The Warden is not sworn to the god at landing.

## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history, not waiting. The god comes down on the river terrace above the crossing in autumn, by the Tallstones [f28], unknown to them, and by its own conduct means to sit at the stones until someone comes.

## The world's side
History: a land divided and redivided; the Tallstones [f28] carry terms no one can now read. Up the Odran [f02] lies Lake Odran [f13], a made lake no one remembers making. Neighbors: Dunmarrow [r07], whose town of Averby [f29] is the next crossing east and whose kings claim the Lorn [f01]; coast fishers to the west. The trouble the god did not ask for: Dunmarrow's [r07] toll at Averby [f29] has been raised twice, the Warden has answered by raising his own, and trade on the Marrow Road [f32] is thinning for both.

## What they know of the land
- The Lornlands [r06], the Lorn [f01], the Odran [f02], Tollen [f27], the Tallstones [f28], the Marrow Road [f32]: seen.
- Averby [f29]: seen by factors. Dunmarrow [r07] and Hallisk [f35]: reported.
- Lake Odran [f13]: seen by boatmen. Sunken Odran [f30]: rumored, a story.
- The Hoarfells [r01]: seen far off. The Carn Hills [r08]: seen at their feet. Carnford [f39]: reported.
- The Turnings [f31]: rumored, by coast fishers.
- Tollen [f27] to Averby [f29], on foot, by The Marrow Road [f32]: 110.3 miles, 5.5 days; Tollen [f27] to Averby [f29], by wagon, by The Marrow Road [f32]: 110.3 miles, 11.0 days.
- Tollen [f27] to Hallisk [f35], on foot, by The Marrow Road [f32]: 264.6 miles, 13.2 days.
- Tollen [f27] to Carnford [f39], on foot, by The Marrow Road [f32], then The Carn Road [f33]: 399.2 miles, 20.0 days.

## Named mortals
None yet. Offices only.

## What the old tell (added creation night 3b, 2026-11-22)
Written from `deep-history.md`. What is told, and where the telling is wrong. Nothing agreed on Night 3a is changed.
They tell that the crossing was given to their forebears by a queen, with the Tallstones [f28] as her word. Near enough: the stones are a boundary award of the old kingdom, about 1,410 years before the god came, and one grants the toll to the keepers of the crossing; the Tolleners came to the crossing some nine centuries after and cannot read it. They tell that Lake Odran [f13] was made by a flood sent on a wicked town. Wrong: a queen dammed the river and drowned an emptied town on purpose, and it was forgotten. They tell that the lake was deeper and the eels fatter in their grandfathers' day; it is so.
