# People: the Ternings

Agreed record, creation night 3a, 2026-11-22. The god gave no name; the Ternings is what the coast calls them and what they call themselves, after their river. Their seat is Ternaby [f15]; they say "the wall". Steadings and houses are unnamed until play names them.

## Who they are
Human. A valley people of the cold coast: herders, fishers and carriers who have wintered in Ternstrand [r02] long enough to keep accounts of it.

## How many
About 5,000, the usual number. About a thousand inside the wall in winter; the rest in steadings along the valley sides.

## How they live
Sheep and hardy cattle on the valley sides; hook, net and weir on Ternlake [f14] and the Grey Sea [r41]; seal and hare; peat, and what wood the lower valley gives. Timber, turf and local stone. Bronze, copper, bone and horn. Winter stores are the whole of their politics.

## Their code
A thing said aloud before witnesses is owed. A debt is a thread, not a shame. No one is judged unheard. No one is turned from a door in the first month of snow. A false weight is worse than a theft. One who breaks a plain promise is "a cold hand".

## Skills and culture
Weaving, tanning, tallow and salt work, smoked and dried fish, sleds and skis, tallies on notched sticks and knotted cords, weights kept at the seat and checked at first snow. The spoken contract, recited by both and repeated by a witness. Ordinary, unpriced.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None of the people's own. The one thing asked was a power of the god's, the Prince's Term, priced on the god and written on its card (ledger L-0022). What the prince bears is fitting, not price: each term he speaks stands as cold in his belly; with more than twelve standing he is at half his pace; he cannot lie inside a term; a breach of the letter is cold in him until mended. His ceiling is twenty standing. The people see it.

## Their magic
The Ice Reading: a bare hand on ice, packed snow or frozen ground tells what crossed it in the last days, how many, how heavy and which way, never why or who. It costs her the hand, numb for as long as she read, a day after a long reading; under that the one cost every worker pays runs slowly (sleep, then memory). Fitted by the world: about ten such workers among them, mostly women past middle age (ledger L-0027).

## What they believe about gods
That a winter judge comes down with the first snow when the year is owed, neither kind nor cruel but exact. It is a saying, older than this winter. They do not know the god.

## Rule and worship
A merchant-prince rules, by bargain, debt, toll and trade, chosen for life by the houses of trade and the heads of steadings. A priesthood, the Keepers of the Rite, keeps the Reading at first snow and first thaw: the customs, weights, settled debts and judgments read aloud. Offerings of salt, tallow and lamp oil at a stone by the lake; no tithe. Before the god is known, the Reading is the people's own rite and the Keepers serve it, not the god.

## Hierarchy
The merchant-prince; the Keepers on custom and record; a Council of the Houses on trade, tolls and levies. The Keepers settle disputes of custom and word, the prince those of debt and toll.

## Helpers
The Prince's Reeve of Tolls; the Warden of Stores; the High Keeper; the Wall-Warden; the Courier of the Steadings. Orders go by runner, sled or signal fire as the season allows. The offices exist and are filled on the morning the god lands. Its people do not yet know it as their god, so each office runs on the people's own custom and becomes the god's helper as its holder hears the god, in play. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away.
The prince is not sworn to the god at landing; the Prince's Term sleeps until one is sworn in its hearing.

## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history. The god comes down at the valley head as the first snow falls, unknown to them, and by its own conduct means to walk down and ask at the wall what is owed.

## The world's side
History: they came along the coast some six generations ago, after an older people of the valleys failed; the old people's terraces and one straight road under the ice (the Iceway [f18]) are still there. Neighbors: sealers of Skarness [r03] east along the coast; Dunmarrow [r07] over Scour Gap [f16], who buy salt fish and sell grain. The trouble the god did not ask for: the prince's house owes two winters of grain to Hallisk [f35] factors, the debt is public, and half the steadings think the next prince should be chosen for it.

## What they know of the land
- Ternstrand [r02], seen: their own valley and the two beside it.
- Ternaby [f15], Ternlake [f14], the Tern [f10], the Scour Way [f17], Scour Gap [f16]: seen.
- The Grey Sea [r41], seen along their own coast. Skarness [r03], reported, by coasters.
- Dunmarrow [r07] and Hallisk [f35], reported, by their own carriers.
- The Hoarfells [r01], seen from below; the Harlfells [r04], rumored, "higher mountains east".
- The Iceway [f18], rumored: a herder's tale.
- Ternaby [f15] to Scour Gap [f16], on foot, by The Scour Way [f17]: 65.5 miles, 4.7 days. The gap is shut from first snow to the thaw.
- Ternaby [f15] to Hallisk [f35], on foot, by The Scour Way [f17]: 307.9 miles, 22.0 days.

## Named mortals
None yet. Offices only.

## What the old tell (added creation night 3b, 2026-11-22)
Written from `deep-history.md`. What is told, and where the telling is wrong. Nothing agreed on Night 3a is changed.
They tell that their grandfathers' grandfathers came along the coast under Torvald Aske and found the valleys empty, the terraces already cut, and no graves. They say the people before them walked north onto the sea ice after a light and did not come back. Wrong: the terrace people went south over Scour Gap [f16] as bondsmen for a debt to Hallisk [f35], about 150 years before the god came, and their descendants are in Dunmarrow [r07] still. Right: the terraces are older than the Ternings. The Iceway [f18] they call a giants' road; it is a road of the oldest builders. They tell that the great light has always stood low in the south and that it was lower in a hard winter; the second part is weather talk.
