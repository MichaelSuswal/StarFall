# The common past

Written on creation night 2, 2026-11-21, before the secret backstories and the hidden powers, which must fit it. It stands under the deep lore's fifth truth: the eight gods are what one far starfall threw, the remainder of a single country that died. They are not persons. Each is a pattern that country held hard for a long time: an office, a custom, a river's habit, a house. None carries one person's memory; each carries a people's memory of it. So everything below is true of the country, and what each god "remembers" of it is the worn shape of this, never a life.

The gods are named here by label, and the player's god as "the player's god". Events are named in lower case. Every proper name in this file is on the sealed terms list.

## The country

Halvenmar was a river country under its own star, far across the plane, farther than any entry of the plane section will hold. One great river made it: the Aunmere. It rose at Corrowe, a high tarn that stayed frozen most of the year, ran down through fen and old forest called the Nightwood, widened at the city of Tessaly where the great ford and the market were, and went out through reed country to a salt shore. Off the last reach of the river lay Orrin Holm, the island where the country laid its dead. In the eastern mountains a cut stair, the stair at Keshgate, climbed to the country's center, which in that land was a warm hill that the people did not farm.

It was an old country and a careful one. Its people did not trust easily and built their lives on sworn words and kept records. Its star was low for the last several lifetimes of its people, and the country knew it was thinning without having a word for it: fish kinds lost, dialects flattened, the reed country going to plain water.

## What the country held hardest

- The river's habit of being followed: roads, towns and fords all kept to the Aunmere, and everything that moved, moved along it. (god-rivers)
- The ferrymen of the last reach, who carried every one of the dead over to Orrin Holm, heard what the dying could not carry, and were sworn never to tell it. (god-secrets)
- The lamp-watch of the Nightwood towns: a lamp kept in every house through the night and one waker to each street, against a sleep that some did not wake from. (god-dreams)
- The winter hall on Corrowe: the high court, which sat on the ice of the tarn in the cold months and gave its judgments aloud, in measured speech, and was never known to take a gift. (god-ice)
- The standing pledge of Tessaly: the custom by which one person stood surety for another's oath, and paid if it broke. It had a twin custom, the pursuit: sworn riders who ran an oath-breaker down. (god-law; the pursuit is not among the eight)
- The wardens of the stair at Keshgate, who kept the door at the top and let up, or turned back, whomever the hill's keepers bade them. (god-stone)
- The counting-house of Tessaly, whose own name was Varrendy: the treasury and tally-hall the tally-masters built room by room, where every weight and measure of the country was kept true. (god-trade)
- The wax grove at Ilmarrow: a walled grove of hives where the country's sworn records were kept pressed in wax, under a woman who held the office and the name of Sennamel, one after another, each taking the name. (the player's god)

## The events near its end

In order. Each is written once here and referred to by its lower-case name.

1. The verdict on the pursuit. About three lifetimes before the end, the standing pledge and the pursuit came into dispute over who answered for an oath-breaker who died under the riders. The winter hall ruled, correctly by the letter, that the surety had already paid and the riders had killed a man whose debt was settled. The pursuit was outlawed, and the pledge's own sureties were set to hunt down its last riders. The custom that had run others down was itself run down, by its twin.
2. The cut at Corrowe. In a run of dry years the river towns, led from Tessaly, cut through the sill of the tarn to keep the Aunmere running. The tarn drained in three summers. The ice the winter hall sat on went down the river as meltwater, and the court never sat on Corrowe again. The river kept running. Nobody in the towns thought of it as a thing taken.
3. The unheard appeal. In the same years the sleep in the Nightwood worsened, and the towns of the lamp-watch sent an appeal to the winter hall for relief from their dues, so that they could keep more wakers. The hall received it at its last sitting on the ice and gave no judgment before the ice went. It was never ruled on.
4. The calling of the surety. The lamp-watch had been kept under oath, with the standing pledge of Tessaly as its surety. When streets in the Nightwood were found asleep and not waking, the watch was held to have broken its oath, and the pledge was called: the sureties paid, and were ruined, and the watch was left with no one willing to stand for it. In its last winter whole streets went unwatched.
5. The barring of the counting-house. With the pledge's sureties ruined, Tessaly's debts could not be settled, and the city council shut the counting-house with the tally-masters inside to finish a full reckoning. Wardens were brought down from Keshgate, as the country's keepers of doors, to bar it from outside. The name Varrendy was struck from the city's rolls until the count should balance. The door was not opened again.
6. The leaving of the stair. The wardens who had gone down to Tessaly did not go back up. The ones left at Keshgate, bidden by the hill's keepers to let up a great many people at once in the last year, stood aside from the door and came down the stair. No one stopped them. What went up the stair after that, and what the hill wanted with it, the country never learned.
7. The rolls at the landing. In the last season the keepers of the wax grove, taught by wanderers who sang lists of names, pressed into wax the names of every village and every living household they could gather, sealed the grove's door, and carried the rolls down the river to be taken over to Orrin Holm, which they believed would stand. The last Sennamel had died that spring and no one had taken the name. The ferrymen had sworn to carry the rolls. On the night the keepers came to the landing the ferrymen would not put out; they said the water was wrong. The star came down that night.

## What no god holds

None of the eight carries the hill, the star of that country, or the wanderers who sang names. The pursuit was not thrown as a god. Whether anything of it was thrown at all is not settled here and is not needed.
