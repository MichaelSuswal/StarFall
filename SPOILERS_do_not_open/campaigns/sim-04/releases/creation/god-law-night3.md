# Release: god-law, Night 3

This release is your whole instruction for tonight and your go for exactly the file it names. A `git pull --rebase --autostash` is always allowed. Build from your own sheet, `self.md` and power card, which are in your own folder, `SPOILERS_do_not_open/campaigns/sim-04/gods/god-law/`.

## Why you come down
Gods come down here to lead a people: to find or gather them, to build, to
rule, and to spread. That is what the world below expects of you.

## The land you come down into

### The season
Autumn. The harvest is in, the rivers are low and slow, and the evenings draw in early.

### The country
Wide slow rivers with towns at the crossings, and hills at the edges of the country. You come down on a river terrace above a crossing, with hills behind you.

### What the land shows of people
A market town at the crossing, with a toll-post at the water. Boundary stones set in a line along the terrace.

That is all the land shows from where you come down. Nothing here says who keeps any of it. No distance, direction or line of sight is given: those come from the map, after it is traced.

The sheet this world is drawn on holds one continent, planned at about a thousand miles across, and is cut into a grid of about twenty cells. A day's walk is a small fraction of one cell.

## What is so of the world for a people
The peoples you will find are at about this level: before the medieval era, no steel forges. That is where the world begins, not a ceiling. You may bring in what you know, but it is built here by the people, from what the world has, at the pace of the work, and you say how it is to be made; nothing is granted by naming it.

Mortal magic is simple, and it costs the worker. The Keeper fits a people's magic to the world on Night 3.

## The people you lead
You come to a people with a seat, a court or council, and helpers who run
things when you are not there: about five thousand, with a town or city.
Who are they? Rule: a mortal ruler under the god, a merchant-prince. One ruler answers to the god and rules for it: a merchant-prince, who rules by bargain, debt, toll and trade. Worship: local shrines. Household and village shrines, kept by whoever keeps them; no order of priests. Build within these, or give your reasons not to. A larger people
is not priced against a weakness; it costs what the world makes it cost (more
food and fuel, slower growth, harder to move, a longer gestation for a larger
kind of people).

## Options
Each is offered once, on the same terms to every god.
- Describe your people in any order and at any length. Whatever gives them a real edge over other peoples is priced.
- What you say about the land or its past is recorded as a wish or as your people's belief. Where anything stands is the world's, and you will see it when you land.
- The names of your people and of their home are yours to give, or to leave unnamed for play. Smaller places and groups among them (hamlets, houses, clans) may be left unnamed the same way.
- Your people are taken as human unless you say otherwise.
- A real edge has a price in weaknesses: something your people would notice. Name the weakness you offer for each edge. The Keeper then says the full price it would ask, with its reasons, and you may say yes, no or a counter to each price. For size: an edge is about as much as the smallest power on a card, and one weakness pays for about that much.
- A claim that your people are the best at a thing in their region is recorded as what they believe of themselves. How their neighbors do it is the world's.
- An edge that touches one of your own powers is given a limit, so that it takes nothing from the power. An edge that would let a mortal do what only you can do is a power of yours: it is priced on you, and goes on your card.
- Magic among your people, if they have any: say what a worker can do and what it costs her. How many workers there are is the world's to fit, and a number you give is a wish. Seeing what is coming is foresight, and costs the worker more than seeing what has already happened.
- Customs are not priced. A custom may still cost your people something in practice, and the Keeper says so where it does.
- You set who a mortal is, never what that mortal will do. What you hope mortals will do is heard and not promised.
- What your people already say or believe on the morning you arrive is yours to say, and so is how you meet them: already there with their own history, waiting for something, or arrived with you. What caused an old saying or custom is the world's.
- How your people settle a quarrel among themselves is yours to say, whoever rules.
- What you do toward your people in the time you are not acting among them is yours to say: whether you let yourself be seen, what you will and will not do. A first act you name is your conduct; where it happens is the world's.

## Questions for you
1. Shrine oaths within a day's walk of you bind as if sworn in your hearing, by the standing form of your side. What is that form: say it exactly, since it binds you in each.
2. You feel each breach as a blow. Does your people know what a broken oath does to you, and would you want them to?
3. A merchant-prince rules for you by bargain, debt and toll. Is he sworn to you, and to what?
