# Deep history: sim-01

Written on creation Night 3 to the depth the Q5 blend set (10 of 10). Dates are years before the gods' arrival, written BA. Year 1 of play is the year after 1 BA. Every place named is in `map.json` by the id given in brackets. The deep lore wins wherever this file and it could be read apart. Nothing here states a mechanism to anyone in the world; where a cause is given, it is for the Keeper.

## Layer 0. The fall before (about 9,000 BA)

A star fell here about nine thousand years ago and unpacked into this country. Its content is the ground of everything since. Three things came through that fall as themselves.

- Lake Enner [enner]. It was a hoarder under the old star and lay under the impact. It is older than the fall and larger for it. It has no outflow that anyone has seen leave it; the Maund rises from warm springs under the south rim [maund].
- Vorn [vorn-peak]. A god of the old country had bound itself to the mountain, and the mountain came through whole with the god in it. It has had no spring, stream or melt runnel since. The god is still there. It is called the Stayer in this file. It is past any ceiling, slow, and speaks seldom.
- The Abiders. A people of the old country who lived in the mountain's halls [vorn-halls] and came through with it. Not human, though near it in shape: long in the limb, grey in the skin, very slow of speech, awake for days and asleep for days, living four to five hundred years. About 600 at the lock, 590 of them in Vorn. They keep one law, one calendar and one list of names, unchanged, and bear very few children. They do not lie; they have never needed to and have no ease in it.

Under the deepest ruins in the bowl of the Ennerwold lies a sheet of blue-grey glass that rings when struck [the-glass]. It is reached at one place [ringing-stair].

## Layer 1. The High Roads (about 7,200 to 4,000 BA)

The Abiders walked out from Vorn into an empty new country and marked it. They built the High Roads: lines of slab markers along the ridges, straight from height to height without regard to valleys, each marker a long Abider stage from the next (about forty miles, since they walk through the night). Human peoples who came later found roads that seemed to go where no road should. At every sixth stage they cut a hall in the rock with a door. Four such halls stand on this sheet: under Windlow Moor [shut-house], under the Sarnmoss [singing-door], in the Norther Folds [debt-door], and in the Lave Rim [sworn-door].

One High Road rings Lake Enner at the distance from which the water cannot be seen, and never goes nearer.

The First Oath, about 7,000 BA. The first Abider elders who came to the bowl swore with the thing in the lake. Its terms, cut round the frame of the Sworn Door in their script: they will not name the water, take anything from it, or build where it can be seen, and it will not call their sleepers. Both sides have kept it for seven thousand years. No living Abider can say why they do not go where the water can see; it is simply not done. This is the oldest sworn word in the land.

The three slab cairns on the Carrow Pass [old-men] are markers of the High Road that runs west along Windlow Moor to the Shut House and east down to the Sallow. They were raised about 6,000 BA.

About 4,000 BA the Abiders drew back to Vorn as human peoples filled the low country. They left one of their number in each outlying hall. One remains: Ninth-of-the-Door, at the Shut House, who has been alone there for 250 years.

## Layer 2. Old Maund (4,500 to 3,210 BA)

The first human kingdom, on the Black Plain. Kings buried in barrows on lone hills: the Howe [howe] holds four, Barrow Down [barrow] holds thirty-one. At its height (about 3,900 BA) it built the great granaries, forty on the plain. Three still stand roofed and sealed [three-bins]; footings of others are everywhere [old-bins].

The Seven Lean Years, 3,217 to 3,210 BA. Seven failed harvests. In the fourth, King Hareth Maun opened the granaries and found most of the seed dead in the jars: the plain's barley had been one strain for a thousand years, sown too thick and stored too long. The kingdom ate itself. Its last steward, Ilsabet Corr, carried forty jars of the hill strain to a store she cut in the Lave Heads [dry-loft], sealed it, and died on the road back. No one came for it. Nine jars still hold seed that would grow.

The patch of fading on the Black Plain (east of Reedditch, about two miles across) is where Old Maund's first city stood for 1,300 years. Barley sown there comes up the same height, the same color, and tasteless.

## Layer 3. The Corr kingdoms (3,000 to 420 BA)

Nine kingdoms in turn held the ford [corrford-town]. Each wrote its law on standing stones and each flood moved them. The lower courses of the bluff wall are the third kingdom's (about 2,300 BA). The sixth, Ruddan (1,900 to 1,500 BA), kept its records on clay in a script no one now reads; its archive lies in the gravel at the Law Stones [law-stones], about 4,000 tablets. They are grain accounts, flood heights and treaties, including one treaty with "the grey ones of the door" granting them the Rust Hills ridge road forever. The last Corr king, Aldous the Short, died without heir in 421 BA; the guilds of the town swore the Ford Peace the next spring.

## Layer 4. Halmer and the drowning (2,900 to 2,400 BA)

The lower Sarn was dry meadow then, with a city, Halmer, where the Sarnmoss is now. The Abider hall there was in use, and Halmer's singers learned from its keeper the habit of opening a door by a sung phrase; they fitted such a lock to the hall as a courtesy [singing-door]. The phrase is the first line of Halmer's own founding song. In 2,404 BA a great storm broke the dune line and the sea took the lower country in one winter. The land has been half-drowned since. The paved way from Halmer to the hall is the Drowned Road [drowned-road]. The Cutfolk's oldest work song begins with the same six notes as the lock, and none of them knows it.

The Ribs [the-ribs] are older than all of this: the bones of one beast of the first centuries after the fall, 300 feet from end to end, grassed over.

## Layer 5. Ennermere (2,000 BA to now)

A kingdom of the bowl, grown rich on the lake's shore, where everything grew well. At its height (1,200 to 600 BA) it held the bowl, Ennvale, the Maundhead and the upper Lave, and took levies from Stair to the Black Plain. It cut the Leaf Halls [leaf-halls] about 900 BA, copying leaf and trunk patterns from the oldest ruins, which its masons found already in the ground and did not understand.

The god Enna. In 1,152 BA a god came down on the east shore, thrown from some far fall of that time. It was a tall crowned figure of pale wood. Ennermere took it as king. It ruled 840 years and Ennermere's height is its doing. It grew past what the ground would hold. For its last thirty years its shadow was pale, its temple [soft-house] wore soft at the edges of every block, and people who had seen it daily could not describe its face. On a dim night in 312 BA it was not there. Ennermere says it went up to the star to plead for them. Since then the kingdom has had kings again, and has shrunk to the bowl.

The Sighting Stones [sighting-stones]. Enna had two stones set in 1,100 BA to frame the star from a third. In 318 BA, six years before it rose, it had a second pair set from the same third stone. The second pair frames the star now. The first pair frames a point a finger's breadth above it. No one in Ennermere has compared them in living memory.

The names. Four hundred years ago shore families began giving every child of a house the same name. It is now the custom of the whole bowl: a household has one name, and its people are told apart by pointing, by "the elder" and "the younger", by trade. Ennermere calls this modesty. The shore villages nearest the water have begun to drop the house names too. The lake is fished by no one, by a law older than the kingdom, and its water is warm in winter.

## Layer 6. The east (2,500 BA to now)

The Ishe towns moved up and down their slopes as the river moved; Highbank [highbank] was the largest, left about 800 BA. The Debt Door [debt-door]: in 381 BA two great houses of the Ishe, Vane and Orrel, ended a sixty-year feud by putting every tally of it into a bronze cauldron and shutting it in the old hall in the Norther Folds, each swearing that whoever opened the door would owe the whole debt of both houses. The door was sealed with lead. Both houses still exist. The Orrel half of the oath is remembered wrongly: Orrels believe the door holds Vane's stolen treasure.

The mound-builders of the steppe (1,800 to 900 BA) left the King Mounds [king-mounds] and the Edge Mounds [edge-mounds]. The Tarsk came out of the east about 600 BA with horses and pushed the Edgefolk's forebears off the open grass.

## Layer 7. The south-east (3,000 BA to now)

The Lave has been lived on without a break for three thousand years. Maundsea [maundsea-city] was founded 1,400 BA as Ennermere's port and has been its own kingdom since 520 BA. It took the levy of the Black Plain in 190 BA. Whitepans [whitepans] was a Maundsea salt works until its panmen threw out the king's reeve in 88 BA.

The traveler Issan Vey came to Whitepans in 61 BA, alone, from the south-west by sea in a boat of a build no one knew, speaking a tongue of which the panmen could follow perhaps half. He came from under another star and had been nineteen years on the way. He brought a case of bronze instruments: a sighting tube on a graduated arc, a water level, a set of nesting measures, and a thing of wheels no one has made turn. He taught the panmen a better way to grade brine and died in 38 BA. His granddaughter, Sefa Vey, keeps the case. She can use the level and the arc and does not know what the arc was for.

A second traveler, a woman the Tarsk call Tuale, walked in from the east in 9 BA with a pouch of seed and a speech only half of which works. She lives with the Serewell camp. Her seed is a hard red wheat that ripens in ten weeks. She has sown it four years running in a plot by the well.

## Layer 8. The north and west (900 BA to now)

The Hollin. Herders who held Carrow and the valleys round it from about 900 BA, clients of the Sallow's salt lords, with leave from Ninth-of-the-Door's predecessors to graze under the High Road. They built the walls and folds of Carrow. In the summer of 253 BA the pan fever came out of the Sallow over the pass with a salt train. It is a marsh fever of the brine flats; Sallowmen take it as children and live. The Hollin had no such hardening. By the first snow two in three were dead or dying. The well drove the sick out of the hamlets onto the tops, at night, in the eleventh month, and barred the doors. In spring the survivors, about ninety, would not stay. They walked down the Wend and are the forebears of some Corrford holdings, who do not know it.

The Carrowfolk came the next two summers in two bands: Sallow salt-carriers' kin over the pass, who knew the valley stood empty, and plain herders up the Wend. They found the walls standing and the dead lying on the hill where they had been turned out, and in the hamlets. They buried all of them, most in one fold above the upper hamlet, whose gate they then walled shut [walled-fold]. Their two oldest laws date from that summer. They were told what had happened by three Hollin who had stayed, and agreed not to speak of it. The pass band and the Wend band each believe they came first.

The Sallowmen have boiled brine at the Brine Mere [brine-mere] for 2,000 years. The pan fever flares among their children about every eleven years; the last flare was in 7 BA.

Hask [hask-steadings]. In the eleventh month of 140 BA slides shut both ways out of the valley next east of Stair. Its four steadings, 212 people, had sold grain that autumn for bronze. None was alive at the thaw. The headwoman, Brenna Hask, kept the count on her doorpost until the fifty-first day.

The Marrow-Eater. A cave bear of a line otherwise gone, about forty years old, lairs at Marrowden [marrowden]. It has learned that a hunter's fire means a carcass and has killed eleven hunters in nine years. It is an animal.

## The Longwalkers

A people of about 140, human-seeming, who walk a circuit of this star's whole country and beyond its edge and back, about once in thirty-three years. They sing lists of names all night and ask the names of places and sing them back exactly. They last crossed this sheet in 27 BA, from the east coast up the Ishe, round the north of the bowl, west along the Underskarn and out over the Far Moors. They are due again in Year 6, by the same road. Their oldest list, which they call the river verse and sing last, holds names from a country their singers' great-great-grandparents walked out of: Anwy, Orrun, Marrow, Tessaly, Senna, Garrow, Keld. They know nothing of that country but the names and that it was old and tired. On each crossing they climb to the Door of Vorn and sing the whole of their lists there for three nights. Something inside listens. They do not know what, and it has never answered.

## The pale herds

They keep to the Pale, some fifty days beyond the Tarsk's farthest wells. About once in a decade a stray band of twenty or thirty comes in as far as the Far Grass and turns back. The Tarsk will not hunt them. One was killed in 31 BA and the man who cut it open said it was all one meat inside.

## Named mortals whose traces can be found

1. Ilsabet Corr, steward of Old Maund, 3,210 BA. Her mark, a barley ear in a square, is pressed into the clay seals of the Dry Loft and scratched on a milestone on the old road up the Lave.
2. King Hareth Maun, 3,214 BA. His barrow is the fourth in the Howe. The Bankfolk's "Stubble Man" is a worn memory of him walking the dead fields.
3. Aldous the Short, last Corr king, 421 BA. His unfinished law stone lies face down at the ford's north post; the last line breaks off mid-word.
4. Teska of Ruddan, archive scribe, about 1,600 BA. One tablet in ten at the Law Stones carries her thumbprint in the corner.
5. Orla Sedge, last singer of Halmer, 2,404 BA. The Cutfolk lineage begins with her name and nothing else about her.
6. The mason Cadel Brand of Ennermere, 900 BA, who carved the Leaf Halls and cut his own face, small, into the root of one carved tree in the fifth hall.
7. Queen Annis Enner, 312 BA, who was in the Soft House the night Enna went. Her account, on forty slats of limewood, is in the king's chest at Ennerhold. It says the god was "looked at by the lake" for a year before.
8. Dorrit Vane and Hal Orrel, 381 BA, who sealed the Debt Door. Their two thumbprints are in the lead.
9. Brenna Hask, 140 BA. Her doorpost, with fifty-one notches, still stands in the highest Hask steading.
10. Mab Hollin, 253 BA, who stayed in Carrow, told the newcomers what had been done, and helped wall the fold. The Upper Hamlet's oldest house has her quern in its wall. The stone-wife is her descendant and does not know it.
11. Rusk Pallet, the Sallow salt-carrier whose train brought the fever over the pass in 253 BA. He died at the second slab cairn. His bronze tally ring is under the stones the Carrowfolk heaped on him there, which they take for an ordinary traveler's cairn.
12. Issan Vey, traveler, died 38 BA at Whitepans. His grave has a stone with marks in rows that are his own letters.
13. Jory Dun, an Edgefolk hunter, who in 31 BA rode forty days east, killed a pale beast, and came back unable to say the names of his brothers for a year.
14. Old Wenna Marl, living, the eldest Cutfolk singer, who alone holds the forty verses of the Halmer lineage past the drowning.
15. Hesper Vane, living, Reckoner of the River Households, direct heir of Dorrit Vane, who knows the true terms of the Debt Door and has told no one.

## Checks made at writing

- Against the deep lore: nothing here contradicts a truth. Nothing explains why the plane keeps accounts, what is above the thin above, what a risen god becomes, or why remainders are shaped like leaders. Enna's rising is told as what was seen. The Stayer's nature is given for the Keeper only.
- Against the eight peoples: every people is "already there". Each record's own history is repeated here or does not conflict. The Carrowfolk's ten generations (about 250 years), their two bands, "the ones before" and the old slab cairns on the pass are all held. What lies over the pass is the Sallow.
- Against the eight secret backstories: Vessary is not in this world. The only things of it here are the eight gods and seven names in a Longwalker list.
