## Round 1

I have read `self.md`. Your name, form, traits, outline, wants and hoped-for people stand as you wrote them. Below is the price of each thing you asked for. Where I write "Set", that is my ruling unless you argue it. Where I write "Choose", I need your answer. Argue anything you think is unfair, as yourself, with your reason. I am weighing the whole of you against a common measure, not each power alone.

### Form

1. Set. The body as you wrote it: a head and a half over a tall man, strong enough to lift an ox or throw a bull, tireless at a walk, slow, a poor climber and swimmer, an old hunter's ears and a hound's nose, hurt by edges, cold and enough hands. You cannot pass for a mortal and you did not ask to hold your presence in, so you cannot.
2. Set. The things you carry are things. The balance is a well-made balance and weighs weight truly; it does not weigh a bargain, a word or a heart. The staff is a staff. The tally cords are cords.
3. Set. The salt horn. Salt is not a small thing in the world below, so I rule on it now. The horn holds enough for one table at a time and fills again only for the next table. Salt from it seasons the meal it was poured for and no more: by the next dawn any that is left is grey grit without savor. It cannot be stored, carried off, sold, or used to cure meat. If the horn is taken from you, the Salt Table fails until you have it back.

### The Count of the Herd

4. Set. What it does: standing still or walking slowly, with your attention on it, you feel the beasts within about half a day's walk: what kinds, roughly how many, which way they move, and whether they are calm, hungry or afraid. Wild beasts and kept ones. Birds clearly, fish faintly. Not mortals, not gods, nothing made. A range of hills dims it. It tells you of the beasts only; what you conclude from a herd of horses standing saddled is your own reckoning.
5. Set. Drawback, both of yours together, each made lighter by the other: the herd is always at the edge of your mind and you cannot wholly shut it. Among many beasts you find mortal speech hard to follow. And every death of a beast in range, while you attend, tugs at you; a slaughter, a great hunt or a winter die-off leaves you dazed for a day. A god of the hunt who is struck dull by a great kill is the price.

### Parley with Beasts

6. Set. What it does: you speak with any beast within a spear's throw, one at a time, or a herd through its lead animal, and understand it as far as a beast can be understood: hunger, fear, ground, young, what passed and which way, in a beast's terms. A beast cannot count past a few, tell one mortal from another unless it knows them by smell, or carry words. You may put a bargain to it and it accepts or refuses as its nature allows. You cannot command. Mortals who hear you hear a beast's cry in an old man's mouth.
7. Set. Drawback, your first: every bargain you strike with a beast binds you as a bargain with a mortal would. You name the price before the work and you pay it. Fail, and that beast's kind remembers, in that country, for a generation of theirs: they turn against you and yours, in the fold, on the road, at the kill. I add your second as a plain fact of the form and not a price: a beast will not treat with you while you carry its kind's flesh or a raised weapon.
8. Set. A beast bound to another god does not bargain against its master. Nothing in the parley tells you that is why it refused.

### The Salt Table

9. Set. What it does: you set salt from the horn before a mortal, state the terms aloud first (guest right, a price, safe passage, whatever is being agreed), and if the mortal takes the salt freely, knowing the terms, the two of you are bound at that table, roof or fire until the next dawn. Until then a blow struck by either against the other fails: the arm goes weak as it falls. This is between you and each guest, not between guest and guest. After dawn nothing stops either party from breaking the terms, but if one does, the other knows it at once, and for a season knows the breaker's direction while within a day's walk of them.
10. Set. It does not hold a mortal who was tricked, forced, or not told the terms; such salt is only salt. It does not stop harm done through others' hands, poison, or fire set outside, though each of those breaks the terms and is known at once. It does not bind a god; a god who takes your salt has only shared a meal.
11. Set. Drawback, both of yours together: you must always state the terms before the salt is taken and can neither hide nor add to them. And it binds you as it binds them: if you break a term you stated, every mortal who took salt at that table knows it and knows where you stand, and for a season your strength is only a strong man's.

### Your trades

12. First offer: that no mortal who sees you can fail to know you for a god. At your size, with your form, that is nearly true already, so it buys little. It buys this: the account after a Salt Table runs a full year, for both sides.  Choose: take it or leave it.
13. Second offer. Being unable to refuse to hear a bargain costs you nothing; you would hear it anyway. Here is the version that costs: at your own table, when a mortal guest who has taken your salt puts a bargain to you that is fair by your own honest judgment, you cannot refuse it. You may haggle to fair; you may not walk away from fair. Anyone who learns this can come to your table with a fair offer you would rather not take. If you take that weakness, it buys the Count at a full day's walk. Choose: take it or leave it.

### Left open on purpose

14. What another god's power can do to you is ruled when it happens.
