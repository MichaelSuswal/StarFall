# The people I hope for: g6-dreams

## Who they are

Human. I do not know what they call themselves and I will not give them a name. I hope they already have one. They are river farmers and herders living in hamlets and single steadings along both banks and up the side valleys, with a market cluster at the bridge. They plough, cut hay, pollard willows, keep sheep and cattle, fish the reaches and cut reed downstream. They mend, weave, midwife, and make bronze and wood tools. In winter they sit indoors and tell stories. A few restless ones walk the empty country and come back.

## How many

About two thousand. Most live in hamlets of ten to thirty houses and in lone steadings. The bridge cluster holds a few hundred. I would sooner have fewer people I can come to know than five thousand I cannot.

## Their code

1. A lamp is kept lit in the window of every house through the dark hours, and a traveler who knocks is let in and fed.
2. A promise is kept to the letter. A broken oath is said aloud before the household and the hamlet, and paid for.
3. Sleep is not interfered with. No one wakes a sleeper for a small matter, and no one lays a hand on a household at night.
4. A name is said over every child at bed, and the dead are named at the hearth once a year.

## Skills and culture

Good thatching and timber work. Reed and marsh knowledge. Hay and grain ledgers kept on notched sticks and clay. Long winter tales told in turn, house to house. Beds and bedding made with care, since rest is counted as wealth. Songs for the lamp, sung when it is lit. Mounds beside the houses, where the old houses stood, are tended and not dug.

## Proposed edges

None.

## Their magic

I hope they have one worker, a woman in a side valley, known by name, sought out and a little feared, and doubted in the next valley over. I hope they do not hate her. She pays in sleep, and I hope people bring her food and quiet in return. I want to meet her, carefully, and I do not know what her gift will make of me. She may feel the place lean in.

## What they believe about gods

I do not know if any god has come here before. I hope they hold that powers walk the night and that a stranger at a lit door is owed shelter, because it might be one. I hope they tell of a Night Guest or a tall man at the table who keeps a lamp lit. I do not need them to kneel. I need them to say a name.

## Hierarchy

I refuse a priesthood. I want no one standing between a household and me. Each hamlet keeps its own elders and its own custom, and I would not replace that. If a wish of mine must travel, it goes by the mouth of whoever keeps a house, hamlet to hamlet, and by the woman with the gift if she is willing. I will ask, and I will not command.

## How I hope to meet them

I hope they are already there, with a history I know nothing of, and that they are not waiting for me. I come down at dusk and I do not step forward yet. In the first days I watch from the hill edge, at night, and learn which houses keep a lamp and which keep a door shut. I find one hamlet. I stand outside, I do not cross a threshold uninvited, and I wait. When someone speaks a welcome, I go in, sit, and let their lamp burn. Then I find a second house.

## Answer to the Keeper's question

Every household tends its own lamp. I do not want a keeper class. The right to invite belongs to whoever keeps the house, any adult of that hearth, and a welcome spoken at the door by such a one counts. For a hall or a hamlet's common house, the one who keeps its door and lamp may speak the welcome. A guest cannot invite another guest. I hope for a lamp-keeper only at the bridge, for the common lamp, and I want that office held by whoever the town chooses, not by me.
