# The people I hope for: g5-fate

## Who they are

Human. A rooted river people of farmers, herders and boatmen. I hope they call themselves something like the River Households, or whatever name the water gave them before I came. They live in long houses along the banks and in the folds of the hills, one smoke to a roof, each house a household of several generations. They farm the terraces and orchards on the south-facing slopes, graze the hill pasture, work the flat boats and the ferry, and keep the towpath. Their days are work, weather and counting. The older hill-forts are their ancestors' places, and they know it.

## How many

About three thousand, in perhaps thirty households. They are spread along both banks and up the side valleys, with a landing stage and a meeting house at the crossroads on the far bank.

## Their code

1. A sworn word is kept, and the oath names what stands behind it. A poor man's oath is worth as much as a rich man's.
2. Disputes go to the open count, before neighbors and the tally, never to the knife.
3. A debt does not die with the debtor. The living finish the promises of the dead, and a household that refuses is not trusted at the ferry.
4. The flood is respected. No one builds below the highest mark on the post, and no one cuts the marks.
5. Shame is answered with a way back. A person who has failed and says so plainly is given terms, once.

## Skills and culture

Tally sticks notched and split so each party holds half. Clay tokens and marks for stores and debts. Flood marks, kept by generations. Boat building, orchard keeping, hill herding, weaving, bronze work bought or made at modest scale, bone and wood craft. Long evening storytelling in the house, with the dead's unfinished promises recited each winter solstice. Songs for crossing the ferry. Exact bargains, struck with a witness and a cup shared.

## Proposed edges

### Exact books

What it is: a habit of record keeping more reliable than most peoples have, through split tallies, token marks and trained memory, so that old debts and boundaries can be settled without war. It is skill, not writing as a full art, and nothing ahead of the bronze age.

Weakness I would accept: they are grudge-bearing and litigious. A wrong, once counted, is never struck out, and feuds over inherited debts can run for generations and tie up households in the count.

## Their magic

I hope they have one worker of their own, known by name, a woman or a man in a side valley, sought out in bad years and a little feared. They think of her as someone who pays, and they ask what she gave, not what she can do. I would like them to be gentle with her about the price and strict about the bargain.

## What they believe about gods

I do not know whether any god has been here before. I hope they believe in no named face, only that the water and the weather keep a book, and that it is, on the whole, fair. They thank the turning of the year and blame the flood, and they say there is a hand in it that cannot be pointed at.

## Hierarchy

I refuse a priesthood and I refuse a king. I want in their place a council of household heads at the crossroads, and one office under it, the Reckoner, chosen by the households, who keeps the common count of oaths, flood marks and debts and speaks at the post at the turn of the year. My wishes would not travel by command. They would travel as weather, as luck, and as the Reckoner's sense of what the book requires. Nothing is carried to them in my voice.

## How I hope to meet them

I hope they are already here with their own long history, and are mid-harvest, waiting out the autumn before the winter closes the river. In the first days I do not show myself. I walk the towpath in the fog and read the flood post. I sit near the landing and the ferry and listen for what they swear and what they break. I watch the crossroads, and I count who keeps faith. I leave the fog undrawn and my leans untested until I know their houses.

## Answer to the Keeper's question

I hope to be known only as something that keeps the book, with no name and no face. The Reckoner would know more than the rest: that there is a keeper, that it is patient, and that its terms are named once and not bargained away. I would not give her my name yet.
