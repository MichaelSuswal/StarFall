# The people I hope for: g4-harvest

## Who they are

Human. Farmers of the black river land, who have farmed it before me and farmed it badly in some years. I hope they have a name for themselves that means the people of the bank, but I do not know it and will not give it. They live in villages on the raised banks and old mounds, a few dozen households each, with ditches, dykes, threshing floors and granaries on staddle stones. Their days are plowing, sowing, cutting, threshing, mending dykes, tending flax and barley, and laying by. I hope they have a granary and a grievance. I hope they have wild scrub and wood to the west to push against.

## How many

About three thousand. I would take fewer. Nine or ten villages along both banks, each of two or three hundred, with the biggest on the raised ground nearest the great squared stone, and a few steadings out toward the western scrub.

## Their code

1. Seed is laid back first. No household eats the seed, and no one is shamed for refusing a hungry neighbor's asking if the seed is what he asks for.
2. The count is said aloud and true. A false count is the worst thing a person can do, worse than theft.
3. A sworn word is kept at cost. They swear seldom and hold to it.
4. Hospitality to the stranger is owed through one meal and one night. After that the stranger is counted among the eaters.
5. No one cuts the last sheaf alone.

## Skills and culture

Plain farming, well done. Crop rotation of barley, flax and fallow. Ox teams. Dyke and ditch keeping, shared by village. Weaving of flax and wool. Bronze tools bought or traded. Songs for the cutting. A midwinter meal where each house names what it has left. Stories of the old squared stone and shame at having forgotten who built it. Law by village moot, spoken, with a reckoner of precedent.

## Proposed edges

None.

## Their magic

I hope they have one gifted person in the country, known by name, sought out and a little feared, as the world allows. I would like them to treat her as they treat a hard winter: with care, and without worship. I would not make her mine. I would ask her to tell me no.

## What they believe about gods

I do not know whether a god has been here. I hope they hold that something walks the fields at the end of the year, the Last Sheaf or the Stubble Man, and that it is not to be thanked too loudly. I hope that if I come plain, they will weigh me by what I count and whether what I say is so.

## Hierarchy

I refuse a priesthood. I want no one between me and the soil. In its place I would like one Counter to each village, chosen by the households, who keeps the number and may be removed by them. Above them, I hope for a steady mortal, one who lays up, tells bad news and says no to me and is right. My wishes would travel as plain words said to the Counters, who may refuse them in the moot.

## How I hope to meet them

I hope they are already there, with their own history, and not waiting for me. I come down at the end of autumn, so I walk to the nearest threshing floor and stand where I can be seen. I will not announce myself. I will kneel, put my hand to a bin or a field, and say what is there. I will go first to the great squared stone and the hill, for the high ground. Then I go to the nearest village and ask to sit at a table, and I will eat last.

## Answer to the Keeper's question

The Counter of the village names the true amount, aloud, at the bin door, before the heads of three households who are not his kin, and again at midwinter at the meal. No one holds both the key and the count. The key goes to one house, the count to the Counter, and the heads stand witness. If I am not there, they say it to the moot and not to me. When I am there, I say it with my palms on the grain, and the number must match what they said. If it does not, I say so plainly, and the Counter answers for it.
