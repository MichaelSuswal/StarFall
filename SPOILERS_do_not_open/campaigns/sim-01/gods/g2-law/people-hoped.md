# The people I hope for: g2-law

## Who they are
Human. They call themselves the Ford People, or the Sworn, if they call themselves anything; I would take whatever name they already hold. They live in the walled town on the bluff, whose old fitted stone they did not build or perhaps did, and in steadings and small villages along both banks. They farm the flood plain, graze flocks on the hill slopes, fish the channels, haul stone, and keep the ford: tolls, posts, guides in flood. The town holds the market, the hall and the records. The villages send their disputes up to it.

## How many
About three thousand. Perhaps eighteen hundred inside and under the bluff walls, the rest in some thirty smaller holdings spread along the river for a day's walk either way.

## Their code
1. A sworn word is sworn aloud, before witnesses, with something named behind it.
2. A man who breaks his word is named in the hall and is not hanged for it. He is made to carry the name, and may earn it off.
3. Boasts, patter and promises in passing are not oaths, and no one is shamed for them.
4. A seal, door or boundary stone set by oath is not touched in anger. It is tested openly, or left.
5. The guest at the ford is not robbed there. Whatever else is done upstream, the posts are honest.

## Skills and culture
Stone and timber work, bronze, river craft, ferrying and ford reading, flood lore. Long memory: they keep tally cords, notched posts and oral genealogies. They have old law and newer law stacked together, and argue over which governs. Their proverbs are many. Their feasts follow the thaw and the first frost. Children are taught to say what they will do and then do it.

## Proposed edges
None.

## Their magic
I hope for one worker, known by name, living a little apart, sought out at the thaw and doubted by the next valley down. I hope they treat her with respect and a little fear, and that they know what she has paid for it. I would not give her to them. She is theirs, or she is not.

## What they believe about gods
I do not know if a god has stood here before. I hope they are not simple about it. I hope they think gods are real but late, and that a god is judged by whether its word holds, the same as a neighbor. I hope some of them say the old walls were a god's, or a god's promise, and some say no. Let them argue.

## Hierarchy
I refuse a king. I want a Bench of witnesses, a handful of elders chosen from the town and the holdings, who hear cases and carry my judgments aloud. Under them, wardens of the ford and the villages whose work is to bring broken oaths to the Bench. No priest speaks for me. If I have a wish, I say it myself in the hall, and the Bench is free to say I am wrong. A wish passed down through a chain of mouths is a wish that has changed.

## How I hope to meet them
I hope they are already there, with a long history and old quarrels that are not mine, and not waiting for me. I hope they have kept a few oaths older than their memory of why. In the first days I walk down to the ford in the open and stand at the posts where I can be seen, on my own feet. I look. I do not break the old walls, however much my hand wants to, and I do not swear anything. I go up to the town gate as a guest, and ask to hear one case. I listen to a whole day of it before I say a word, and I say "witnessed" to nothing until I have learned what they mean by it. The careful man is not yet afraid, they say, and I know that saying for what it is.

## Answer to the Keeper's question
Anyone may swear before me: Sworn, stranger, guest or enemy, since an oath that only my own can make is half a bluff. Where I say "witnessed" in the hearer's hearing is for a swearing in a set place, the hall at the town, and on the days the Bench sits, so the thread is deliberate and few. Anyone may bring a broken oath to the Bench: the one wronged, any witness who heard it, or a warden whose work it is. The wronged may speak first, but not alone. No one is made to wait on another's leave to say that a word was broken. Judgments are given in the hall, aloud, where all can hear them.
