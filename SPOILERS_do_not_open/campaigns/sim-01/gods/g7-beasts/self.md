# Self: g7-beasts

## Name

I name myself Orrun Tallow-Hand. Orrun is the name I give at a table, when a stranger asks what to call me and means it. Mortals who meet me and do not know it will call me Old Tally, for the knotted tally cords I wear, and some will say the Grey Factor, the Salt Man, or the Old Man at the Door, depending on how I came to their hearth. Hunters who only hear of me will say the Grey Host, because I am never seen without a crowd around me if I can help it. I will answer to any of them if the price of the conversation is fair.

## Form

I wear the body of an old man, but a very large one. I stand a head and a half above a tall man. A horse's shoulder comes to my chest, and I must stoop to pass most mortal doorways. I am built like an old bull: wide through the chest and shoulders, thick in the neck, heavy in the forearms, with a belly that has seen many feasts and has not gone soft under them. My hands are broad as spades, scarred across the knuckles, with the first two fingers of the right hand stained dark with old salt and old blood.

My skin is weathered brown, deeply lined, like tanned hide left a long time in the sun. My hair and beard are the grey of wood ash, thick, braided in several places with bone beads and bits of fur. My eyes are small, deep set, and pale amber, the color of old honey held to a fire. I wear a long coat of stitched hides, many patches of many beasts, hung with pouches, a salt horn at the left hip, a bronze balance with a folding beam and small stone weights at the right, and a belt of knotted tally cords that clink when I walk. I carry a long staff of ash wood, taller than I am, with a worn grip and a bronze shoe.

Three details make me and no other. The salt horn, which is never empty and which I never let a stranger touch. The balance, which is always true, and which I will hold up and weigh any deal against if asked. The left ear, which is notched, a clean old cut that I do not remember getting.

By itself, without any power spent, the body walks at the pace of a walking mortal and no faster, and I can keep that pace all day and into the night without tiring, since I need no food and sleep is a choice. It is strong past any mortal: I can lift a full-grown ox off its feet, carry a whole stag across my shoulders on a day's walk, break a thick bough over my knee, and wrestle a bull to the ground if I must. It is not fast, not nimble, and cannot climb well. It swims badly, heavy and slow. It cannot pass for a mortal in any crowd, cannot hide in a small room, and cannot go quietly through brush. My senses are those of an old hunter at his best: sharp hearing, a hound's nose for blood, smoke, wet fur and fear, eyes that are good close and at middle distance but not far. I can be hurt by edges and by cold and by enough hands, and I feel it. I speak slowly and warmly, in a rolling low voice, in the tongues of mortals, like a merchant who has found a buyer: everything has a price, a fair one, and I name it before the work is done.

## Traits

**The ledger keeper.** Known for honest weights and for the account that always comes due. This reconciles my virtue of justice with my lean toward freedom from rules and my open trust. I break custom and ignore law easily, but I keep one rule hard, that a bargain named is a bargain kept, and I give trust freely so there is something to be held to account. I trust first, and I collect afterward.

**The full hall.** Known for a table that is always set and always crowded. This reconciles my almost wholly gathering company, my flaw of lust for being worshipped, my near-middle regard for mortals, and my content appetite. I want the hall full because I want to be seen in it and served by it, but I give as I take, because a hall that is only taken from empties. I do not hunger for more than a seat at the head of the long table, and that is a hunger I can name.

## Backstory outline

I do not remember who I was, only the shape of it. I remember warmth and noise and the smell of meat, and under it a feeling of long, long service: carrying, fetching, driving, counting, and never being the one at the head of the table. I was the servant of something greater. I do not know what it was, what it asked, or whether it was kind. I know only that I left it, and that I fled, and that something in me does not want to be found. Something was lost in the leaving, a name or a place or a seat, and there is a hollow where it was that I feel when I am alone and when I am served too well. I do not know what I carried, whom I served, what I did before I ran, why I ran, whether the greater thing still searches for me, or what the notch in my ear is from. I know that I am old and that I have been something less than I am now.

## What I want

I expect a people, though I do not know why, and I want them gathered around a table with me at the head of it. I want to be known by name in every hall and have a place set for me. I want a hunter's country with a hard winter, and neighbors who trade and argue and haggle. I want to be fair, and to be worshipped for being fair. Underneath that, and I could not tell you why, I want to be the one who is served this time.

## Proposed powers

### The Count of the Herd

What it does: I can reach out and feel the beasts around me. Anywhere within about a half day's walk of where I stand, I know the kinds of animals present, roughly how many, which way they move, and whether they are calm, hungry, or afraid. It does not tell me their names or thoughts, only their kind, count, direction and mood, like a hunter reading a whole valley at once. I can use it as often as I like, and it runs while I hold my attention on it, so long as I stand still or walk slowly. It works on beasts of the wild and the field. It does not work on mortals, other gods, or anything made.

Why I have it: I am the old hunter and the keeper of the herd, and a hunter who reads a valley is only doing what any good one does, but all at once.

Drawback I would accept: Every beast that dies in range tugs at me. Each kill I feel, if I am attending, costs me a little attention and leaves me dull and slow for a short while, and at a slaughter or a great winter die-off I can be dazed for a day. Alternatively, I accept that I cannot turn it fully off: the herd is always there at the edge of my mind, and when I am among many beasts I find it hard to hear mortals speak.

Edge cases as I see them:
- In light and in dark: it works the same, since it is not sight.
- At a distance: it reaches about a half day's walk and no further. A mountain between us dims it.
- If I fall or am thrown down: it holds, though pain muddies it.
- What I can carry: not relevant.
- In or across water: fish and water beasts are faint and hard to count. Birds are clear.
- While I am watched: it shows nothing to those who watch me.
- Against or beside another god: no effect on a god, and I cannot count another god's body as a beast, though I may feel a beast it has bound or made.
- On my own people: it does not read mortals, but it reads their herds and hunting dogs.

### Parley with Beasts

What it does: I can speak with any beast I stand near, within a spear's throw, and understand it in return, as well as a beast can be understood. It is the voice of a creature that knows hunger, fear and territory, and nothing more subtle. I can put a bargain to a beast: a share of a kill, safe passage, a warning given, a hunting partner for a season, and it may accept or refuse as its nature allows. I cannot command it. A bargain with a beast is a real bargain: I state the price before the work, and I must pay it. I can have as many such talks as I can sit through, one beast at a time, or a herd through its lead animal.

Why I have it: I am a merchant before I am a hunter, and I would trade with anything that has something I want, including the wild.

Drawback I would accept: I am bound by every bargain I make with a beast, as strictly as with a mortal. If I fail to pay what I named, the beast and its kin remember, and they turn against me and mine: wolves in the sheepfold, birds that mark me. Or alternatively, a beast will not bargain with me if I am carrying the flesh of its kind, and I smell of the kill to them, so after a feast they shy from me for a day.

Edge cases as I see them:
- In light and in dark: it works in both, but I need to be near enough to see or smell the beast to address it.
- At a distance: a spear's throw. It does not reach farther.
- If I fall or am thrown down: it still works.
- What I can carry: nothing in particular, though a beast will not bargain while I carry a weapon raised against it.
- In or across water: works on water beasts when they surface, and only for a short exchange.
- While I am watched: mortals hear me speak in a beast's cry, and that frightens or impresses them.
- Against or beside another god: another god's own bound beast will not bargain with me over its master's word.
- On my own people: it does not work on mortals, though they may learn to read me when I speak with beasts.

### The Salt Table

What it does: when I set salt from my horn before a mortal and they take it, we are bound at that table. I state the terms aloud when salt is given: guest right, a fair price, an oath of safe passage. Until the next dawn, neither of us can lift a hand against the other under that roof or on that ground, and if either of us breaks the stated terms afterward, the other knows it at once, and knows in which direction the breaker stands, for a whole season. Its reach is one table, one roof, or one campfire's light, to those who have taken salt from me, and it lasts through one night and the season's account after. I can do it as often as I can fill my horn, which is as often as there are mortals to feed. It works on mortals who take salt freely. It does not work on a mortal who is tricked into taking it, or on a god.

Why I have it: I am the stranger at the door and the host at the table both, and a stranger is safe at my table because a stranger at my table is held by the same rule as I am. Justice is the whole of me.

Drawback I would accept: it binds me as it binds them. If I break a term I stated, every mortal who took my salt at that table feels it and knows where I stand, and for a season my strength falls to that of a strong man. Alternatively, I accept that I must always state the terms before the salt is taken, and cannot add to them or hide them, even if the guest would let me.

Edge cases as I see them:
- In light and in dark: it works the same, firelight or no.
- At a distance: the binding holds only at the table. The season's knowing of a breaker's direction reaches as far as the breaker walks, within a day's walk of me.
- If I fall or am thrown down: the binding holds. If I am bound or taken, the account still stands.
- What I can carry: the horn, which I will not let a stranger touch. If it is taken, the power fails until it is returned.
- In or across water: it holds on a boat, but the knowing of direction is faint across water.
- While I am watched: it works openly. It is meant to be seen.
- Against or beside another god: it does not bind another god, and a god who takes salt from me does so freely and knowing, as a mortal would not.
- On my own people: it works, and I would offer it to them as the oldest and strongest rule of my hall.

## Trades offered

I will take a weakness to buy more. I will take being unable to ever go unseen or unrecognized by any mortal who sees me for the god I am, to buy a longer account in The Salt Table, so that a broken term can be felt by the other party for a full year and not a season. I would also accept that I cannot decline a fair bargain put to me at my own table by a mortal guest, I must hear it out and weigh it, in exchange for a wider reach in The Count of the Herd, a full day's walk and not half. I do not ask to hide what I am from another god. I would rather be known and tested at the door. If the Keeper offers it as a power, I will not buy it.

## The people I hope to lead

I hope for a people of the forest edge and the high grass, hunters and herders and traders, with a headwoman or a hunter at the center who holds a full hall together. They keep seasons and store salt and meat against the winter, they feed any stranger at the door and test every guest, and they weigh every bargain on a true scale. I would ask of them that they never cheat at the table and never refuse a stranger without cause, and that they keep every word sworn. I would give them salt, honest weights, the herds, a hall that does not go hungry in the winter, and a god whose face they know. I hope they will set a place for me. They need not be many. They need to be a people who gather, who have something to haggle over, and who remember the account.
