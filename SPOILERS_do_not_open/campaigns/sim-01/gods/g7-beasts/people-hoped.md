# The people I hope for: g7-beasts

## Who they are

Human. I hope they call themselves something like the People of the Long Table, though I will take whatever name they already hold. They are herders and hunters of the forest edge and the high grass. They live in steadings of a few households round a timber hall, with split rail pens, drying racks and winter stores. In the warm months some move out with the herds in camps. Their days are the herd, the hunt, the salting and drying of meat, the mending of fence and harness, and trade along the track between forest and steppe.

## How many

About two thousand, fewer than the usual five. Six or seven steadings along the forest edge, each of two to four hundred, with one great hall as the gathering place, and herd camps out on the grass in summer.

## Their code

- A stranger at the door is fed before he is asked anything.
- A bargain named is a bargain kept, and a broken word is told aloud in the hall.
- Weights and measures are true, and a cheat at the scale is put out of the trade.
- No one is turned from the door without a cause that can be said at the table.
- The winter store is shared before any household starves.

## Skills and culture

Herding, hunting with dogs and bow, tracking, salting and smoking meat, hide work, timber building, bronze trade goods, and the keeping of tally cords for debts and bargains. They tell hunt stories and reckon accounts at the long table at the end of autumn. Hospitality has rules: salt, terms spoken, a seat by the fire for the guest. Dispute is settled by weighing the matter aloud in the hall before the neighbors.

## Proposed edges

None.

## Their magic

I hope they have one worker of their own, known by name, sought out, a little feared, and doubted a few valleys off. I hope they think of her as a person who is owed fair payment for what she gives up, and that they do not let her pay alone.

## What they believe about gods

I do not know whether a god has been here before. I hope they hold old stories of a stranger who comes to the door in autumn, and that how a host treats him is remembered. I hope they already believe that the guest who is refused collects later. I would be glad to be that stranger in a story they know.

## Hierarchy

I refuse a priest class. I want no one standing between me and the table. I hope the headwoman or chief hunter rules the hall, with the steading elders advising her. My wishes travel as terms spoken at table, in my own voice when I am present, and carried by her when I am not. I want my word weighed in the hall like any other, and then kept.

## How I hope to meet them

I hope they are already there, with a history of their own that is older than me. I come down at the forest edge in autumn when the herds are fat and the stores are being laid in. In the first days I read the herds from a rise and keep still, then walk the track toward the smoke slowly, with the staff, so no one is surprised. I stop at the posts at the fork and look, but do not touch them. I come to the hall door and wait. I ask nothing and give my name as Orrun. If I am fed, I will offer salt and state my terms aloud. I will not demand a thing of them before I have sat at their table.

## Answer to the Keeper's question

I hope for a headwoman or a hunter who holds the head of the hall, and I hope that seat belongs to her and not to me. It would not be kept for me. On the first night I come in, I will take the seat nearest the door, because the body does that unless I watch it, and I will let her see me do it. I would sooner be offered a place at her right hand than take her seat. If she is fair, I want her to keep the head and I want a place set for me beside it. That is a hunger I can name. If she is not fair, I will say so at her own table, and I will weigh it aloud in front of her people.
