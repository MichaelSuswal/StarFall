# The people I hope for: g1-stone

## Who they are

Human. Highlanders. I hope they call themselves something about the pass or the door, but that is theirs to say, not mine. They live in small steadings of stone-walled houses set hard against the rock, as the smoke below me shows. They terrace the sunny slopes, herd goats and sheep up the valley with the thaw, cut stone, tend stores, and watch the weather. They are hard-handed and tired of long winters. I hope they were already here, with a history I do not own.

## How many

Fewer than five thousand. I hope for about eight hundred, in six or seven steadings strung down the valley and up its side gullies. No town. Each steading is a few families, with a shared granary dug into the hill.

## Their code

- A stranger who comes cold to the door is fed and housed. Then he is watched, openly and without insult, until he is known.
- Salt is shared. A bargain sealed over salt is kept when it is easy to break.
- Stores are laid against winter before pleasure, always, and the granary is counted by more than one person.
- They do not abandon a steading to a bad winter. Those who can walk take in those who cannot.
- A bad order is not obeyed loudly. It is turned aside quietly. A good one needs no asking.

## Skills and culture

Dry-stone walling, terracing, goat and sheep herding, felt making, cheese, bronze working in small quantities, stone cutting, reading snow and slope for slides. They tell the winter-count by notches in a stone. Songs are short. Speech is slow and plain, and silence is not rude. Guests are given the seat farthest from the door and nearest the fire. Children learn the safe tracks and the slide gullies before they learn letters, if they have letters at all.

## Proposed edges

None.

## Their magic

I hope for one worker, as the land allows, a woman known by name, sought out and a little feared. I hope the people neither worship nor drive her out. They pay her in food and keep an eye on what the work costs her. I would not ask her for anything. She may be wary of me. That is fair.

## What they believe about gods

I do not know whether a god came before me. I hope they believe the mountain listens, and that old stones are owed courtesy. I hope they are slow to name any god and slower to kneel. If one is named, I hope it is as a neighbor who keeps a door, not a master. I would be named last, and only after I have earned it by staying.

## Hierarchy

I refuse a priesthood and I refuse a throne. I will not take orders and I do not want to give many. Each steading keeps its own elders and its own custom, and the elders settle what is theirs. A few steady people in each steading, chosen by the steading, are keepers of the door. They carry my word and bring theirs back, and I answer anyone who climbs to me. My wishes travel as asking, never as command, and I hold myself to anything I swear.

## How I hope to meet them

They are already here, with a history older than my landing, and with their own sorrows. I do not know what they wait for. In the first days I stand still on the rock and watch the smoke. I listen to the stone. I do not come down at once. I choose one steading, I go down slowly in daylight with my hands open, and I stand outside the wall until someone speaks. I ask nothing first. If there is need before the thaw ends, I open a shelter where they can see it. I look each one in the eye who goes in.

## Answer to the Keeper's question

Only winter in them. I want them to keep their own roofs and their own hearths the rest of the year. A shelter holds about twenty, it closes at the thaw, and a people cannot live in a room that closes. I hope they take refuge in them in the worst weather, in slides, in storms, and when a steading fails. If they chose to live in them instead, I would let them in and I would be troubled. A people who lives in my rock is a people that needs me every day, and that is not what I want. I would ask them, once, to keep their roofs. I would not command it.
