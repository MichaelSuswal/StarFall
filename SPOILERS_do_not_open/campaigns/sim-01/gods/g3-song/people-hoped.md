# The people I hope for: g3-song

## Who they are

Human. I hope they are already here, and I hope they call themselves something plain, like the river folk or the people of the cuts, and I will learn it from them. They live in steadings of one to three households on raised ground, joined by plank causeways and by boats. They cut peat, trap eels, keep geese, pole flat boats, weave reed and brushwood, and work bronze they trade for. In the cold months they gather at the one long low roof I can see, and eat and sing there.

## How many

About fifteen hundred, in perhaps forty steadings spread along the river and its cuts, with the long roof as the gathering place. I hope for fewer than five thousand. A table should be one a person can see the end of.

## Their code

1. They tell the truth to those they eat with, and a liar sits at the door end of the table until it is mended.
2. They name their dead aloud every autumn, all of them, in order, and no name is skipped.
3. A sworn word is kept or paid for, and they swear rarely.
4. No one is turned from the long roof in winter, and no one who comes in is asked to leave a quarrel outside.
5. They do not sing a lament for gain, or a flattering verse for anyone.

## Skills and culture

Boat-craft, causeway-building, eel and fowl craft, peat-cutting, smoking and salting. Songs for work, for the dead, for the harvest and for the road. A custom of the long table: each household brings a pot, and the singers go round it in turn. Children learn the shortest lineage verses before they learn to pole. Quarrels are settled by each side singing its account, and the table decides which sounded true.

## Proposed edges

### The lineage singers

What it is: three or four people at a time who can hold a whole lineage, law and ancestor-list word for word in their heads. Few peoples have it.

Weakness I would accept: they are worn thin. Each is short-lived, in poor health, and poor at remembering anything outside song, so that a singer may forget a debt, a face or the road home. The people must feed and watch them, and when one dies, much dies with her.

## Their magic

I hope they have one worker, a woman known by name in a few steadings, sought out in need, a little feared, and doubted by half. I would not have her serve me. I hope they think of her as one thinks of deep water: respected, not owned. As they say, a thing that many have is a tool, and a thing that one has is a mystery.

## What they believe about gods

I do not know if a god has stood here before. I hope they believe in old powers in the water and the fog that do not care for people, and that they leave a bowl out and do not pray. I hope they will not make me more than I am, and will tell me when I am wrong.

## Hierarchy

I refuse a ruler under me. Let the singers advise, and let the household heads decide at the table, as they do. My wishes travel by voice, spoken at the long roof, and no one is bound by them except by their own word. I want the door open, and I want to be argued with.

## How I hope to meet them

I hope they are already here, with a history that is theirs, and that I was not waited for. I will not walk into a steading first. In the first days I will lay my hand on the old squared stones where they go under the water, and on the long roof's door post, and listen. Then I will come to the long roof at dusk, as an old woman with a wet hem, and sit at the door end and be silent until someone speaks. I will say plainly what I am and what I am not. As the old ones say, you do not ask the oldest tree for directions, you sit under it.

## Answer to the Keeper's question

I would keep it for need alone. A song that takes from every singer should not be worn smooth by habit, and I do not spend what I cannot count. Before the first singing I would tell them this, without softening: each who sings will lose one small thing of their own, a morning, a taste, a name once answered to, and not one of them will choose it or be told what it was. I lose one too. No one is made to sing, and no one is made to obey. If they ask for it, and know the price, I will sing it. I hope that is rare, and that when it comes it is the table that asks.
