# The common past of the eight

Written on creation night 2, before any secret backstory or hidden power, under the deep lore's fifth truth. Every name here is a sealed name. If one is used again in the world on night 3, the use is logged as a deliberate likeness, and no god's home ground carries more of them than another's.

## What the eight are

The eight are the remainder of one far country, thrown clear on the night its star came down. None of them was ever a person. Each is the most-held pattern of something that country kept for a very long time: an office, a law, a rite, a river's habit of being prayed to, a story told to children. What each god dimly remembers is not its own life. It is a people's long memory of the thing it is made of, pressed together without order, so that a thousand keepers of one gate feel like one keeper, and the worst night of an office feels like a wound. This is why no god remembers a childhood, why each wound is real and belonged to no one in particular, and why each arrives expecting to be followed.

No god knows any of this. Nothing in this file is ever stated to a god or to the player. It can surface only as effects: a being, a mark, a word, a dream beside the warm stone that fell with them.

## The far country

Its people called it Anmere. It lay under its own star for longer than its records ran, and it was old and spending when the things below were kept: its seas shallowing, its speech flattening at the edges, its outer provinces gone plain.

- The river Issel ran the length of it, from cold springs under the mountain to the port. For most of the country's life every ford on it had a shrine where things were set down in the water for the river, and the river had a name that was said there.
- The mountain was called the Keld. Under it were cut rooms older than the country's last government, and the high pastures on its shoulder were called Auren, where the great herds summered.
- The port at the river's mouth was Maunt, a crowded harbor with a deep in it that the harbor people fed with whatever was to be kept and not seen again: wrecks, the dead of bad years, debts written on lead.
- The one road out of the heart of the country to the provinces passed a gate town called Hesp. The gate had two doors: the great door, where a lamp was lit at the evening hour and those who came were sorted, taken in or turned away, under a law of hospitality older than the gate; and a low postern beside it, kept by a warden under oath to the government and to no law of hospitality at all.
- The last government was the Varrach, a regency of stewards that ruled in the name of heirs who were always children. In its final century it tried to slow the country's spending by shutting it in. Its last regent was a woman named Ysolt Brenna.

## The events near the end

1. The closing of the borders, about ninety years before the end. The Varrach shut the provinces out. Hesp, which had been a gate of welcome, became the wall of a quarantine: those who came from the plain provinces, who could speak and could not say what their mothers had called them, were turned away at the great door by the same lamp that had once taken them in. The law of hospitality was never repealed. It was reread.
2. The striking of the shrines, about sixty years before the end. The Varrach forbade offerings to the Issel, holding that prayer was a spending. The ford shrines were stripped by the regency's quiet works in a single season, and the stone that carried the river's name was taken down to Maunt and sunk in the harbor deep with the lead debts. Within two generations no one on the river could say the name.
3. The hiding of the heirs, in the last decade. Two child heirs, twins, were put in a cut room under the Keld, behind a door that opened only inward, to be kept through whatever was coming. Before that, each autumn, one of them had ridden down from Auren on the shoulders of the herd warden at the head of the drive, as the small king of the year. One child came out of the room. The record does not say which, or by whose hand the other did not. The one who came out was not let stay in the country's heart.
4. The short count, in the last autumn. The droves came down from Auren early, ahead of weather no one had seen before, and were counted through the great door at Hesp as they were every year. The count was short. Some had been lost on the mountain; some had been sorted at the lamp and turned away as plain. The counters counted again and it was still short.
5. The open night, the last night. The warden of the postern at Hesp left it open and walked away from the gate. Whether the warden was released or ran, and what went out or came in by the low door, no record says, because that night the star came down on Anmere.

## What became of each pattern

- The player's god is the lamp and the law of the great door at Hesp: the office of the one who sorted, who was fair and not kind, and who in the last ninety years turned away as many as it took in. The lit windows it remembers are the houses of Hesp seen from the door at the evening hour. Its name is the old word for that hour.
- god-thresholds is the postern warden's oath: the standing, the weight, the door opened once and left.
- god-rivers is the Issel as it was prayed to at its fords, and then not.
- god-sea is the harbor deep at Maunt, which kept what sank, and which was given one thing it should never have been given.
- god-secrets is the hidden works of the Varrach: the makers of its vaults, inward doors and quiet removals.
- god-beasts is the herd warden of Auren and the written law of the drive, with the small king on its shoulders.
- god-storms is the count at the gate, and the weather-reading of the drovers who lived by it.
- god-stone is the child under the Keld: the one who came out.

## How any of this may surface, and how it may not

Only as effects, under rule 1 of the mystery standard. A dream beside the warm stone of a crowd kneeling at a ford and a river no dreamer has seen. A god that finds it cannot say a certain word and another god in whose mouth the word sits unnoticed. Two gods who meet face to face and each feel the other stood at the same door. A child's game with a rider on a tall one's shoulders. Nothing states that the gods are patterns, that the country was one country, or why any of it was thrown here. A god may learn that it is a remainder and never learn whose.
