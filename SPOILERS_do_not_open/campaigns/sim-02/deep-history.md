# Deep history: sim-02

Written on creation Night 3b to the depth the survey set (age of the human story 9 of 10: ruins under ruins, several fallen ages, the oldest unreadable). Dates are years before the arrival of the gods (BA). Every place carries its map id. Nothing here is told to anyone; a people knows only what its own record says, and most of this is known to no one living. It contradicts no truth of the deep lore and explains none.

## The ground before anyone counted

Under the oldest ruins of the North Ashring [r12], and bare in places on the Bare Floor [r14], lies a layer of blue-grey glass a hand thick that rings when struck. No people has a record of when it was laid. It was there before the first cut room.

The Bare Floor [r14] was a lake. The Sleeper [f27] lay in the middle of it as an island that no one took for anything else. The lake did not drain and did not dry: over about eleven hundred years it grew shallower everywhere at once, lost its fish kind by kind, lost its cold, and was a pan of pale silt by 4,300 BA. The Sleeper [f27] has grown in every age since. It is now about forty miles from nose to tail and stands some four miles high at the shoulder, lying on a rise of its own making. From the rim crest on a clear evening its flank can be seen to rise and fall, once in about an hour.

The great pale curves that stand out of hillsides here and there, of which the Rib [f29] is the largest above ground, are stone bones. The beasts they belonged to were dead before the first cut room. No one knows what they were.

## The people of the heights (from about 6,000 BA)

The pass folk are the oldest people on the sheet. Their cords count six thousand winters from the cutting of the first room, at what is now Kinhold [f17]. They held the foothills on both sides of the range until human kingdoms climbed, and were pressed above the trees between about 3,800 and 800 BA. Their cords have a knot for "came down" tied about 3,900 BA, when four lights fell on one night. About 1,900 BA, when the terrace lords of the dales sent an army up the old road to take the high pastures, the knotters of the heights did one great working in a deep room above the Underfell [r06] and brought the mountainside down across the High Way [f09] below the Knot Stair [f07]. Eleven knotters went into the room. The cords say four came out, that none of the four could afterward be taught their own kin-knot, and that the room was closed from outside. That room is at the Hush [f16]. Nothing particular has grown in its hollow since, and the people of the heights have halved in the last five hundred winters. On the night of the arrival they watched a warm stone fall into the Kettle [f08], and they have kept two watchers on it since.

## The first age: the lake towns (about 5,400 to 4,300 BA)

The first people to build in stone built on the lake's shore. Their chief town was Lakewall [f63], on the north-east shore, with a quay 600 paces long that now faces dry silt. They wrote in pressed clay, in a script no later people has read; some hundreds of their tablets lie in the lowest cistern of Old Orsa [f24], reused as packing. They fished the lake from sewn boats and did not land on the island. Cause of their end: the lake. As it went shallow and plain their towns followed the water inward, each new quay lower than the last, until there was nothing to follow. The last of them moved up to the rim crest by 4,300 BA. What remains: Lakewall [f63]; five lesser lines of stumps on the old shore; the tablets; the lowest courses under Old Orsa [f24].

## The second age: Orsand, the road-making kingdom (about 3,800 to 2,900 BA)

About 3,900 BA four gods came down on one night onto the rim and the country round it. They found the rim towns and led them. Within a century there was one kingdom, Orsand, with its seat at Old Orsa [f24], and for six hundred years it laid fitted stone roads from the rim to every shore: the Marchway [f11], the Fitted Way [f04] and the High Way [f09] west over the Knot Stair [f07] to the harbor of Westerhythe [f10]; the Salt Road [f23] north through the Sennway [r08] to a harbor that lies under Gullwick [f21]; the Hardway [f35] to a light on the Tern Shore [r18], whose tower still stands at Lastlight [f38]; the Rope Line [f36] east across the grass and off the sheet, with a road station at the Flat Banks [f37] and a caravan town at Stonemeet [f66]; the Stopped Road [f49] south to the Lowe [f03] and on, on a line the river has since taken, to a port whose streets are the Drowned Streets [f53] and whose outer harbor is the lower of the two under the Tide Stumps [f43]; and the Banked Way [f59] south-west through Emberholt [f65] to a provincial town that lies under Quillan [f55], with the barrows of its governors at the Bottom Mounds [f58].

Its people remembered the four as the First Crowns: the Road-Mother, the Harbor Lord, the Reaper King and the Measurer. Their faces are worn from every carving at Old Orsa [f24], though the robes and hands beside them are sharp. Three of the four grew great, and each in turn was seen to go light: the Road-Mother about 3,310 BA, the Harbor Lord about 3,180, the Reaper King about 3,050. Orsand's records call each going a crowning in the Lamp and kept its day as a feast. One relief at Old Orsa [f24] shows the Reaper King standing a hand's breadth above a threshing floor, with the threshers looking at his feet.

The Measurer did not grow. For three hundred years it gave away what it had: it set a keeping on certain springs, of which Wardwell [f48] is one that still runs true, and it had rooms cut and filled and sealed. There were nine of these rooms. Five are on the sheet and unopened: the Measure House [f26] under Old Orsa [f24]; the Stairfoot Door [f62]; the Lintel Door [f15]; the Underkeys [f57]; and the Springdoor [f47]. Two were broken into by 2,500 BA and stand empty, under Gullwick [f21] and under the Tide Stumps [f43]. Two lay on the Rope Line [f36] beyond the sheet. Each sealed room holds the same kinds of thing: rolls of names, of families, villages, fields, breeds and tools, cut in slate; jars of seed, each kind labelled; the kingdom's standard weights and measures in bronze; and a slate that names the other eight rooms. The Measure House [f26] holds besides these the school's star tables: nine hundred years of the Lamp's height, taken yearly through the Holed Stone [f25] and a second stone at Westerhythe [f10], which show no lowering at all until the last forty years of the count and then a very small one.

The school was the tally-wrights of Old Orsa [f24], some two hundred workers of the grip at its height. Its last master, Meren, held that the beast in the dry lake was drinking the land and the Lamp together. In 2,960 BA, against the Measurer's word, the school did one great working from the rim crest to stop the beast's breath. The beast did not wake. Most of the two hundred were, by the next spring, people who could be led by the hand and could not say their names. What had still grown on the floor stopped growing. Within thirty years the rim towns could not keep their own speech apart from their neighbors', the harvests of the heartland failed kind by kind, and the provinces stopped sending grain to a capital that could not say what it wanted. In those years the frightened heartland turned on the one god it had left and prayed to it without rest. The Measurer was last seen at Old Orsa [f24] in 2,948 BA. Meren sealed the Measure House [f26] in 2,951 BA; her handprint is in the plaster of the blocking. The kingdom is counted as ended in 2,900 BA, when the last road warden left the Sennway [r08].

What remains of Orsand: its roads; the nine rooms; the Holed Stone [f25] and the cliff-top stone at Westerhythe [f10]; the cased springs; the reused stone in half the walls on the sheet; the rim people.

## The third age: the broken provinces (2,900 to about 1,100 BA)

- Old Orsa [f24]. Never wholly empty. Its people became the rim people, tenants of each later power that claimed the rim. Families there kept names and pedigrees as late as 400 BA; the last named in a southern record is the house of Orsavel. Names for people went out of use hamlet by hamlet over the four centuries since, nearest the floor first.
- Westerhythe [f10]. Held by a few fisher families until about 2,700 BA, when slides closed the High Way [f09] for the first time. Remains: a mole, a stair cut in the cliff, the sighting stone on the cliff top.
- Emberholt [f65]. An Orsand provincial town of about 4,000 on the Banked Way [f59]. It outlived the kingdom by six hundred years as a free town. In 2,300 BA a spotted fever came up the road from the south; the survivors walked to the bend town. Remains: tree-grown walls, a paved square, a cistern.
- Stonemeet [f66]. A caravan town of the grass, perhaps 3,000, living on the Rope Line [f36]. Its wells did not fail; they went flat, tasting of nothing, and stock would not thrive on them. Abandoned by 1,900 BA. Remains: wall footings, nine wells with water in them.
- Tollmere [f64]. The market city of the toll kings of the Rivermeet [r15], about 7,000 at its height around 1,700 BA. Burned in 1,450 BA by grass peoples in the third of three dry years, after its king shut the fords to their herds. Remains: grassed mounds, a black layer a foot down, and a name the farm villages still use for the place.
- The gap lords of the Sennway [r08] took the first toll there from about 2,600 BA to 1,500 BA and built the first wall where Dunmarrow [f18] stands. The Bare Gate [f19] is older than they were: it is the gate of the Orsand road station, whose walls they took for stone. The gap then stood untolled for twelve hundred years.
- The Hallar, the terrace lords of the Folddales [r02], about 1,800 to 700 BA. Dale chiefs who took one another's valleys until one house held twelve. They built the hill walls and terraces, kept lamps at the old waystations as a duty of lordship, and at their height fed about 40,000 people, some 6,000 of them in the Hald Valley [r01], where their hall stood at the Highfold [f06]. It was their army the mountain fell on in 1,900 BA. They ended in the Blight Years, 720 to 690 BA: three wet summers in which the oats and barley of the dales carried a black spur in the ear. Those who ate the bread burned in the hands and feet, saw things, miscarried and died, and the dales blamed strangers on the roads and a store-pit door opened at the wrong time. Nine in ten of the dale people died or left. The last lord, Ossa Hallar, died in 694 BA at the Highfold [f06]. Those who stayed in the Hald Valley [r01] became the Haldren. Some 900 who left walked south along the mountain's foot and built under the old wall at Undercrag [f13]: the Hodd, who have counted their living and their dead aloud ever since.
- The bend town, under Quillan [f55], was never abandoned. Its own records were burned in a fire of 800 BA, which is why the town is older than its records. The keys of its gates and of the Underkeys [f57] have passed in one family since before the fire.

## The fourth age: the peoples now on the land (from about 1,100 BA)

- The Thessend river kingdom rose about 1,100 BA at Talcross [f52], then a river-mouth town, and moved its strength upriver as the coast drowned. At its height, in 598 BA, Queen Halla Thessa dammed a side valley of the Middle Reach [r26] to feed a canal and drowned the town of Sellnot [f46], whose headwoman, Brid Sarrow, had refused the crown's price three times. The canal silted within a century. The Bankwater [f45] remains. In the same reign the crown tried to drain the Reedsea [r27] and hunted the reed folk for nine summers; the marsh won. The kingdom split in three in 280 BA, when the heir, Corin Thessa, a boy of fourteen, vanished on the night before his crowning. He had gone up the river. He lived and died a ferryman at the upper ford above Kessary [f44], under the name Corin Ashe, and was buried there in 231 BA under a marker that carries a small crowned fish, the mark of his house, cut by his own hand. Tobin Ashe, keeper of that ford now, is his descendant in the eighth generation and does not know it.
- The reed folk were in the delta before the river kingdom, living over the Drowned Streets [f53], which had gone under water between 2,400 and 1,800 BA as the coast sank.
- The Tavrek came onto the Hornfoot Grass [r16] about 900 BA from the east, along the Hardway [f35]. Their first law-keeper, Skel Orrun, made a bargain with the wardens of the Greyspur [r10], a small lordship of shepherds that has since died out: the grass, against a yearly tithe of horn and a promise that the Rib [f29] would not be dug. It was written in the wardens' speech. Its text is the first lines of the Tavrek's oldest law skin, which no Tavrek now reads.
- The Brannow's present town grew to its size after 600 BA on river trade and fruit.
- The Corrant founded Gullwick [f21] about 500 BA on the old harbor, took the toll at Dunmarrow [f18] about 300 BA and garrisoned it. They lost it in the winter of 120 BA. On the open morning the north gate stood unbarred in fog and the hill people walked in. The bar was lifted by the garrison's own quartermaster, Tam Brandoch, a man of the gap, who was paid twice: by the hill towns, and by three ship-holders of Gullwick [f21] who wanted the garrison's cost off their levy. The ship-holders' letter to him, with their seals, lies in the council chest at Gullwick [f21], misfiled among harbor dues. Ilse Brandoch, toll-reeve this year, is his great-granddaughter and has been told he died defending the gate.
- The Ushkai came onto the Rope Ridges [r20] about 400 BA from the Thornhollows [r21], pushed north by the levies of the coast crown.
- The Pellim city of Saltstairs [f41] is the third harbor on its ground. The lowest is Orsand's. The second, the upper line of the Tide Stumps [f43], was a Thessend royal port drowned by 500 BA. The Dry Hull [f42] is a royal grain ship of that port, beached in a storm surge in 540 BA on what was then the strand. The present city owed the coast crown a harbor due from about 340 BA and stopped paying 40 years ago.

## Travelers from afar

About one in a generation reaches this land from under another star, and none in living memory has been believed. Three left traces. The other stars are many lifetimes' walk away, so each traveler was the last of a line.

- The Far Woman, 212 BA. She came ashore at the Pell Mouth [r23] alone, in a sewn boat of a build no one knew, the last of a ship-line out of the south (plane entry p03). She carried a sighting staff with two sliding pegs and said that in her people's oldest story their own lamp had come down. She taught that a lamp falls when a shore stops keeping its fires, which is not so. She lived eleven years in Saltstairs [f41] and was laughed at kindly. Her staff hangs in the weighing house on the long quay, called the Far Woman's rule, and no one remembers what it measures.
- The Dun Walkers, 95 BA. Six people who walked out of the east onto the Rope Ridges [r20], the end of a walking line from under a star that still hangs (plane entry p02). They could speak, and could not say their names or where they had set out from. They carried a bronze disc a forearm across, scored with rings and small marks. The Ushkai took them in as strays. Their blood is in the easternmost families. The disc is the lid of a milk vat in the largest camp.
- Jorun of the Lamp Hut [f61], 68 to 12 BA. He was brought ashore on the Far Capes [r30] as a child by his mother, the last two of a ship-line that had left its home port some five hundred years before (plane entry p01). She died within the year. He lived alone at the Lamp Hut [f61], kept a fire on the cape every night of his life, and traded dried fish with summer fishers, who thought him simple. On the lintel of his hut he carved what his line had kept: the word Maunt, for the port; the word Issel, for a river; and a gate with two doors, one tall with a lamp over it and one low. These two words are likenesses, logged as such in the keeper log: they are the same names the sealed common past holds, because the line came from that country. Under them he cut a short joke about a ferryman who charged the river for crossing itself.

## The non-human peoples

Two, both godless and both older on this ground than any human people now living on it: the pass folk of the Deep Scarrow [r04] and the High Scarrow [r03], and the reed folk of the Reedsea [r27]. Neither has a story of arriving. No file says where either came from, and no being on the sheet knows.

## Individual stories whose traces could be found

1. Meren, last master of the tally-wrights: her handprint in the blocking of the Measure House [f26]; her name on the rolls inside.
2. Urreth, eldest of the eleven knotters at the Hush [f16]: her cord hangs first on the east wall of Kinhold [f17], unfinished.
3. Ossa Hallar, last terrace lord: a cairn at the Highfold [f06], larger than the rest, with a bronze cloak pin under it.
4. Wat, the Hallar steward who walked the count: no grave. His tally sticks, 31 of them, are in the roof of the house of Hallin in Ostrey [f01], taken for old pegs.
5. The first Corve, the lame lamp-keeper: the lowest notch on the tally stick in the Corve rafters.
6. Brid Sarrow of Sellnot [f46]: her house is the one nearest the Springdoor [f47], under forty feet of water, with its door bar still in place.
7. Queen Halla Thessa: a boundary stone at the Bankwater [f45] with her name and the price offered.
8. Corin Thessa, the heir who became a ferryman: his marker at the upper ford above Kessary [f44].
9. Skel Orrun, first keeper of the Tavrek law: the first lines of the oldest skin.
10. Tam Brandoch, who lifted the bar: the ship-holders' letter at Gullwick [f21]; his name scratched inside the north gatehouse at Dunmarrow [f18].
11. The Far Woman: her staff at Saltstairs [f41].
12. Grandmother Dun, last of the six Dun Walkers, died 51 BA: the disc; a grave cairn on the easternmost ridge of the Rope Ridges [r20] with six stones on it.
13. Jorun of the Lamp Hut [f61]: the lintel; his fire platform; his grave, dug by fishers.
14. Corwin Vale-Marrow, keyholder of Quillan [f55], who in 58 BA pledged the second key of the Underkeys [f57] to the lending house of Tessel in Vessany [f50] against a loan for grain in a bad year, and died before redeeming it. The pledge is in Tessel's book. His grandson Edrin believes there was only ever one key.
15. Dann Corsey of Saltstairs [f41], lost with his boat on the Drowned Shelf [r35] in 29 BA. He had left his share with his sister Hanne, to be sent to a woman in Talcross [f52], Leah Marrin, and their son. It was not sent. The son, Perrin Marrin, is a clerk of the coast crown.
16. The house of Orsavel, last named family of the rim: a pedigree in the archive at Ardmoor [f51], ending in 400 BA with the words "and their children, who are not written".

## Checked at writing

Against the deep lore: the star is condensed and leaks; nothing here says what it is. The glass is shown and not explained. The lake's end, the dead wells, the plain people and the flat speech are shown as what happened, with no cause given beyond what each people believed. Mortal magic is shown by its cost. Gods are shown to have come before, to have grown, and three of four to have gone light, which the lore's sixth and eighth truths require of any god that passes the ceiling; the Measurer is shown shedding and then gone, and where it went is not written because no one saw. The star's results stand: one hoarder, visibly a beast; no carriers and no thinners, and nothing invented in their place; the falling phase, with the lowering first measurable about 3,000 BA and now plain to an eye that has an old mark to go by.
Against the records: every people's dates agree with this file (the Tavrek about 900 years on the grass; the Hodd about 700 at the mountain's foot; the Ushkai about 400 on the ridges; the Corrant founded about 500 BA, the toll taken about 300 and lost about 120; the Thessend risen about 1,100 BA, at their height about 600, split about 280; the pass folk's first rooms about 6,000 BA; the rim names lost over four centuries). Every meeting is "already there", and each people was there: none arrived with its god and none was waiting for one.
