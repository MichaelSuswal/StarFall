# People: the Pellim

God: god-sea (the people do not know this on the day it lands). Agreed on creation Night 3a from the god's `people-hoped.md` and its table. The people named themselves; the god left them and their home unnamed.

## Who they are
Human. A city people of the estuary, mixed by trade: families that came by sea and families that came down the river, who have stopped asking which is which.

## How many
About 20,000, inside the wall and on the dyked ground behind it.

## How they live
Packed in, loud, warm. Fishing and boat work in the channels, weirs and stakes on the flats, salt pans, dyked pasture. Carters, lightermen, tally clerks, caulkers, net-menders, brewers, cooks. Lamps along the quays at dusk. Autumn is their best season.

## Their code
Weights are public and a bargain is a bargain. You may lie about your cargo and never in the terms. A debt is spoken aloud before a witness. Haggling is sport; fighting over price is bad manners. A person's silence is their own.

## Skills and culture
Shoal-water boatcraft, tide-reading, weir and stake work, salt-making, caulking, dykes and sluices, tally and weights, trade-talk, bawdy songs, fog days as half holidays. Ordinary and unpriced.

## Edges and their weaknesses
One edge, priced at the table (ledger L-0022). Edge: a people far larger than the default, about 20,000. Weaknesses that pay for it, two. First: the city does not feed itself. Its dyked pasture, flats and boats feed about half its mouths; the rest is grain bought from up the river and along the coast. Measurable line: with the grain boats stopped, stores carry the city two months. Its sellers know their hold. Second: the city is crowded past what its water and its low streets can bear. Measurable line: in a year a sickness reaches it, it loses between one in twenty and one in eight of its people, where a town of the default size loses between one in forty and one in twenty; neighbors who know this close their ports to the city's hulls and people while the sickness lasts. A port closed for sickness is not an embargo; an embargo is a separate act. Both are the people's weaknesses and no part of the god's card. No sickness or hunger year is promised by these lines.

## Their magic
Fitted to the world, as for every people: about one in a few hundred has the grip, and most of those do not know it. A working is attention paid; it does small things (warmth from cold, a bleed stopped, a thing found, weather or a beast read); it takes from the worker something small and specific that she does not choose and notices only afterward. Among the Pellim: perhaps sixty with the grip, in a city that size, most unknowing. A net-mender on the east quay finds lost things for a fee; she has forgotten which of her sons is the elder.

## What they believe about gods
Coast sayings, tide-luck, a story of a city that went under. No temple, no priest, no god with a face. They hold that weather is weather.

## Hierarchy
None under the god, and no king. Each ward keeps its weights and tally. A dispute goes before a bench of three or five drawn by lot from the ward for that tide, sitting in the open at the weighing place. Walls, sluices and watch are kept by wards in turn. The keeper of the harbor's weights is the nearest thing the city has to a standing officer, and rules nothing.

## How the god meets them
Already there. The god means to come up out of the water at a quay as a broker in a damp coat and be found by being useful in a deal.

## Their history, their neighbors, and a trouble their god did not ask for
The city stands on the stumps of two older harbors, the lower of them under the tide: the lines of stone and piles on the flats at low water. The old hull on dry ground well back from the shore lies where the shore was. For three hundred years the city owed a harbor due to the nearest of the three crowns inland; it stopped paying forty years ago and nobody marched. Most of its grain comes from that same crown's plain. Their trouble: the crown has begun, this year, to ask for forty years of arrears, in a letter read aloud at the weighing place and laughed at. Their second trouble, counted and not understood: the salt pans on the east flats make salt that keeps meat a shorter time than it did, and the pan-wives say the brine has gone thin.

## What the god hoped, and what the people hold
Held: a walled city on the tide, no ruler, benches by lot, public weights. Not held: any knowledge of the god. The keeper of weights the god described exists, as who she is.
