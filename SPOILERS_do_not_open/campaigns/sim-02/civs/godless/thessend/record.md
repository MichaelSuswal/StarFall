# People: the Thessend (the three crowns of the lower river)

## Who they are
Human. The remains of the largest kingdom this land has had in a thousand years: three crowned cities and their countrysides on the lower course of the long southern river and the low country behind the coast, each with a king or queen who claims the whole and rules a third. Tired, proud, lettered, rooted. They are the "old kingdoms" of every neighbor's telling.

## How many
About 60,000: three cities of 6,000 to 9,000 each and a farmed lowland of villages between.

## Where
The lower long river from below the last hill fords to the edge of the delta marsh, and the plain eastward behind the drowned coast. Finished from the map in the section at the foot of this record.

## How they live
Grain, flax, cattle, river barges, salt bought from the coast and sold inland at a king's price. Stone cities built on older stone cities. Tolls at every bridge and landing. Scribes, tax tallies, a levy of spearmen, a few hundred horse. No steel; good bronze, tin bought dear from across the water.

## Their code
A debt outlives the debtor. A boundary stone is not moved. A crown is owed service and owes bread in a famine year, and each crown is judged by whether its granary opened. Guests are counted and taxed, not turned away.

## Their history in the land
The river kingdom rose about 1,100 years before the gods came, on the ruins of two older ages it did not understand, and reached its height about 600 years before, when one crown ruled from the hill fords to the sea and dammed a side valley to feed a canal, drowning a town that would not sell. It split in three about 280 years before, in a succession fought over a missing heir, and has not been joined since. Each generation has been a little smaller than the last. Their oldest quarter is the one they are quietly leaving: its wells have gone flat, its fig strain has stopped fruiting, and its street speech has lost the words the other quarters still use.

## What they believe about gods
Gods are the old kings, who went into the star and will be asked for an accounting. Each crown keeps a house of ancestors with lamps. They would treat a claimed god as a claimant: politely, with clerks, and with a question about what it wants taxed.

## What they want
Each crown wants the other two. All three want salt, lamp oil and the tolls of the upper river. None wants a war it cannot pay for.

## How they touch the great events
They hold the land's best records, and the records show loss they have misread as decline of blood and virtue. Their debts and granaries are where the quiet debt and the hunger of a bad year are decided for half the south. They are the nearest great neighbor of two gods' peoples.
