# People: the Ushkai

God: god-storms (the people do not know this on the day it lands). Agreed on creation Night 3a from the god's `people-hoped.md` and its table. The people named themselves; the god left them and their home unnamed.

## Who they are
Human. A herding people of the grass and the long ridges of the east, wary of the sky and used to it.

## How many
About 1,900, in groups that part and rejoin.

## How they live
They move with their herds and the seasons: new grass in late spring, ridge pasture in summer, down the hollows in autumn, the sheltered hollows among the thorn in winter. Tents of hide and felt. They burn old grass to bring on the green. They build no fixed roof.

## Their code
Count the herd at the gate every night, aloud. A guest at the fire is fed once without a question. Ground is used and not owned; no one's mark is cut in anything. A quarrel is argued by the fire until morning and may be reopened. Taking from a neighbor's herd ends a welcome.

## Skills and culture
Herding, tracking, horse-handling, felt and hide, cord and notch counting, weather sayings, long fire songs, sour milk. Ordinary and unpriced.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. The god proposed none and the table closed in round 1. Smaller than the default, no seat, no hierarchy under the god: the god's choices, which bought nothing.

## Their magic
Fitted to the world, as for every people: about one in a few hundred has the grip, and most of those do not know it. A working is attention paid; it does small things (warmth from cold, a bleed stopped, a thing found, weather or a beast read); it takes from the worker something small and specific that she does not choose and notices only afterward. Among the Ushkai: perhaps six with the grip. A girl of the north camp can quiet a horse with a hand; she has stopped answering to her milk name and no one has noticed why.

## What they believe about gods
Nothing of this god. They say the sky counts its own. They have a saying that a stray taken in before a storm brings the herd through it, and they take in strays. No priest, no altar, no offering.

## Hierarchy
None under the god. A council of elders and headmen argues at the fire. Between peoples, the headmen go and talk.

## How the god meets them
Already there. The god means to drift in as a stray grey gelding between two storms, and to come as the old herder only later. It comes to itself in late spring as the storms break.

## Their history, their neighbors, and a trouble their god did not ask for
They came onto the ridges about four hundred years ago from the south, behind their herds, and have fought the grass's other herd people for winter hollows in dry years. The long straight lines in the grass and the worn banks in the hollow were there before them; their saying is that the lines are where the sky dragged its rope. Good iron they have never had; bronze they trade for. Their trouble: two dry summers running have thinned the winter hollows' water, and the council is split on moving the winter camp north onto ground the other herd people use. A second trouble, taken for a breeding fault: on the easternmost pasture the calves and foals are coming all one dun, the brindles and the blazes gone, and the families that summer farthest east come back quieter each year.

## What the god hoped, and what the people hold
Held: a moving people with a council, the nightly count, no roofs, a saying about a stray. Not held: any saying about an old herder who is good with weather; that would have to be earned. The counter the god described exists, as who he is.

## What they know of the land
Finished from the traced map (step 3a.6), in the section at the foot of this record.

## Named mortals
- Oren Tash, headman of the largest camp, about fifty, who can hold a herd, a household and a quarrel in his head, is right too often to be loved, and drinks a little on a good night.
- Kessa Tash, his daughter, about twenty-two, who is good with people, which he is not.

## The land, from the traced map

Written on creation Night 3a after the tracing, from `map.json`, never from the plan.

Home: the Rope Ridges [r20], E2, and in winter the Thornhollows [r21] south of them. The long straight lines in the grass are the Rope Line [f36]. The worn banks in the hollow are the Flat Banks [f37]. The river between the ridge lines is the Ropewater [f34]. Their god comes to itself on the spring pasture beside the Flat Banks [f37].

### What they know of the land

What a herding people know: their grass, their trading edge, and what riders tell. Distances are straight lines measured on the traced map. Days are on foot along that line, each stretch at the pace table's rate for the region it crosses (rough ground 14 miles a day for valley, hill, plain, grass and shore; forest 10; mountain 8; marsh 6), with the kinds of ground named in order of their share; a line that follows a used road is given at the road rate and says so. Grades: seen (they have been there), reported (told by one who has), rumored (a name and a rough place).

- The Rope Ridges [r20] (seen): their own ridges. The spring pasture lies in it.
- The Thornhollows [r21] (seen): their winter ground. From the spring pasture, the middle of it lies about 480 miles south, 34 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (hill country by share of the way).
- The Ropewater [f34] (seen): the ridge river. From the spring pasture, its nearest reach lies about 140 miles south, 10 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (hill country by share of the way).
- The Rope Line [f36] (seen): where the sky dragged its rope; ridden along, never to its end. At the spring pasture.
- The Flat Banks [f37] (seen): banks enclosing nothing; a camp ground. At the spring pasture.
- The Pellwater [f40] (seen): the river on the grass's southern edge, where horses and felt go for bronze, salt and grain. From the spring pasture, its nearest reach lies about 590 miles south, 42 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (hill country, then open grass by share of the way).
- The Noman Grass [r19] (seen): the middle grass, into its edge. From the spring pasture, the middle of it lies about 380 miles west, 27 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (open grass, then hill country by share of the way).
- The Dunwater [f33] (reported): the meeting water with the other herd people. From the spring pasture, its nearest reach lies about 190 miles west, 14 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (hill country, then open grass by share of the way).
- The Hornfoot Grass [r16] (reported): where the other herd people gather, north-west. From the spring pasture, the middle of it lies about 630 miles north-west, 45 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (open grass, then hill country by share of the way).
- The Tern Shore [r18] (reported): a stony shore north, with fishers. From the spring pasture, the middle of it lies about 310 miles north-east, 22 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (hill country, then shore country by share of the way).
- Saltstairs [f41] (reported): the great city on the tide; headmen have been to its edge. From the spring pasture, lies about 750 miles south, 54 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (hill country, then shore country, then open grass by share of the way).

- The Lowe [f03] (rumored): a great river west of the grass. From the spring pasture, its nearest reach lies about 500 miles south-west, 36 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (hill country, then open grass by share of the way).
- The North Ashring [r12] (rumored): brown hills beyond that river. From the spring pasture, the middle of it lies about 970 miles west, 69 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (open grass, then hill country by share of the way).
- The Sleeper [f27] (rumored): something asleep past the brown hills. From the spring pasture, lies about 1,030 miles south-west, 74 days on foot, at the pace table rate for each stretch of ground crossed (open grass, then open plain, then hill country by share of the way).
