# People: unnamed

They may name themselves. I do not. Their seat I leave unnamed too.

## Who they are
Human. A travellers' people. They live at a crossing and are used to strangers. They leave and come back changed. They know a door when they find one.

## How many
About fifteen hundred. One thousand would do. Two thousand is the most I hope for. Not five thousand. I would shrink it and I say so.

## How they live
In a walled seat at the crossing. One gate, or two, one on each road. The gate is open by day and watched by night. It is not locked. They farm what the slopes allow, keep goats and sheep, and work the old terraces if they can. They keep the way: they mend the road, mark the paths, and keep the cairns. They feed those who pass. They trade at the crossing. Some of them always go out. Some of them always come back.

## Their code
Bread and a place by the fire first. A name after, or never. A gate is watched, not locked. No one holds the key over everyone. A stranger is not turned from the gate at night. A watcher who sleeps is not shamed, he is woken. Whoever leaves is welcome back. A task is given to one chosen for it, and when the task ends the choosing ends.

## Skills and culture
Wall building and gate building, dry-laid stone and timber. Road and path keeping. Reading weather and fog. Guiding through narrow ways. Cairn marking. Hospitality as a craft. Songs for leaving and songs for returning. No steel and no more than the world allows. All of this is ordinary culture.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I propose none. They have no rare skill, no unusual age and no magic that other peoples lack. They are priced as ordinary. A small people with no ruler and no hierarchy is a real cost, and I take it as the cost.

## Their magic
None. Perhaps a rumor among them of a person who is good at finding the way in fog, nothing more. I describe no mortal with a knack.

## What they believe about gods
They do not know me. They half know there is something at the gate. They have a story of a fog that stood at the gate in autumn and a door that was found open, and most of them doubt it. A few believe it. They have no priests and no temple for it. I do not ask for any.

## Hierarchy
I reject one. No fixed ruler, no council with a seat, no advisors who carry my wishes. When something needs doing they choose the person for it, by the gate or at the fire, and the choosing ends with the task. A dispute is settled by those who are party to it and by neighbors who are asked, not appointed. The gate's word on the night is the word of whoever holds the watch that night. No one else answers for the gate. Orders do not travel, for there are none. Asking travels.
With no hierarchy my unplayed time runs on my own conduct alone. I know it cannot be delegated. I accept this.

## How the god meets them
They are already there. They have their own history and their own dead. They were not waiting for me. I come at the crossing in autumn, in fog, at the turn of the day, and I do not announce myself. I would be found by an opened door, not by a name. I will open for the first who needs it and ask nothing. I do not tell them what I am in time I do not play. I do not close a door on one who stands at it in need. In time I do not play I will not command them, hold their gate, or speak for them. I hope, once, one of them kneels. I do not ask it.

## Questions for you
1. Do I hope the people of the gate know I am at their gate at all? Half. A rumor, a story, a doubt. Not a certainty. Not a name. I would be known by what happened at the door, and let them make of it what they will. I do not hope they are ignorant. I do not hope they are sure. The want to be known is mine and I say it plainly, but I would not be known by being told.
2. Do I hope they ask me to open things, or never learn I can? That they never learn to ask. Each opening takes something from me and I will not know what. I would give anyway, to the one in need, unasked. I do not want a people that leans on it. Yet I would be thanked, and I know the two wants pull against each other. If they ask, I open, and I do not refuse. I only hope they do not make a habit of the asking.
3. If the people lock their gate, what do I hope to do about it? Nothing by force. It is their gate. I would not break it and I would not shame them. I would go to it at night and wait. If someone stands outside in need, I open it, quietly, and let the dew on the wood be the only sign. I would not hold it shut for them, and I would not lock it for them. Over the years I would hope to persuade them by what I do, by who passes through. If they want it locked for good I will not take it from them, and I will say plainly that I am a poor god for a people with a locked gate.
