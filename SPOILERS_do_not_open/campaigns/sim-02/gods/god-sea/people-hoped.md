# People: unnamed. Let the harbor name them. Their home I leave unnamed too.

## Who they are
Human. A city people of the estuary, already there, with their own long account. I did not make them and I do not claim them. They are the ones who live on the walled quays, work the channels and flats, and sell to anyone who ties up. They are mixed by trade: families that came by sea and families that came down the river, and they have stopped asking which is which. Nobody here agrees on what a thing is worth, and that is the thing they are proudest of.

## How many
Fifteen to thirty thousand, close inside the wall, with the lowest streets wet at high water. I take whatever the world finds in that range and will not argue a count. I would sooner be short a few thousand than have a number I did not earn. If the Keeper prices the full size as heavier than the smaller, I would rather hear the price than shrink it in advance.

## How they live
Packed in, loud, warm. Fishing and boat work in the channels, weirs and stakes on the flats, salt pans, pasture and dyked marsh behind for the town's beasts and its bread. Most of the living is in the middle: carters, lightermen, tally clerks, caulkers, net-menders, brewers, cooks who feed a quay at dusk. Houses stack and lean. Lamps along the quays when the day fails. Autumn is their best season, when the boats come home heavy and everyone is feeling generous. They talk to the tide as to a neighbor, and mostly do not think it listens.

## Their code
Weights are public and a bargain is a bargain. The scale on the quay is anyone's to check. You may lie about your cargo, your cousin and your cooking, but you do not lie in the terms. A debt is spoken aloud before a witness and is carried, in the open, by both sides. Haggling is a sport and fighting over price is bad manners. You do not ask a person what they have swallowed, meaning what they will not say; a person's silence is their own. Strangers are welcome at the table for the length of a deal. Nobody here is above the scale.

## Skills and culture
Ordinary, and free. Boatcraft in shoal water, tide-reading, weir and stake work, salt-making, caulking and hull-drawing on the mud, dyke and sluice keeping, tally and weights, a loud and funny trade-talk with many words for a fair price and a poor one. They tell stories about the drowned works on the flats and the old hull far back from the shore, and every district has a different version. Their fleets do not sail in a surge and they know it. Songs are bawdy, quick and about money. Drink is plentiful. They keep fog days as a half holiday.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I propose none. I want them ordinary. A people with an edge is a people someone else will price and come for, and I would sooner have a city nobody has priced. The cost of this is the whole of my bargain: I know they are no stronger than their neighbors, and I am content.

## Their magic
None that I describe. At most a rumor: that some old harbor women and net-menders can read a tide a night ahead and are paid in kind for it. That is skill and not knack, and I ask nothing of it. If the world gives some mortal a knack, I will be told and will argue it then.

## What they believe about gods
They do not know I am there, and I hope they never look up. They have the usual coast sayings, a tide-luck here, a fog-warning there, an old story of a city that went under, and no temple, no priest and no god with a face. They hold that the weather is weather. They do not know my powers, they do not know I exist, and they do not know what a god is in the sense that matters. I would not correct a belief I did not pay for.

## Hierarchy
I reject one. No king, no priesthood, no standing advisors who carry my wishes, and no hierarchy that takes orders from me, because I would not give any. I send no orders, so there is nothing to travel.

How they settle things among themselves: by the scale and the witness. Each quay, ward or guild keeps its own weights and its own tally. A dispute goes before a bench of three or five drawn by lot from the ward for that tide, which sits in the open at the weighing place, hears both parties and a witness, and says what the thing weighs. Penalty is fines, forfeits and a spell of being refused credit, and the last is the one anyone fears. Walls, sluices and watch are kept by wards in turn, by rota, with no one in permanent charge. If the world gives them a standing lord I did not ask for, that is the world's and not mine, and I will say so then.

With no hierarchy, my unplayed time runs on my own conduct alone. I accept that. My conduct is the Keeper's to hold me to: I do not ask a mortal what they swallowed, I do not lie in the terms of a bargain, and I do not raise a surge over a coast with a fleet in it. Where a power or weakness of mine turns on a place the people keep in common, the quayside weighing place is the one I would name, and the word that answers for it is whichever bench is sitting there that tide.

## How the god meets them
Already there with their own history. I come up out of the water at a quay in autumn as a stranger in a damp coat, a broker with a satchel, a pan scale and a purse, and I stand at the edge of the crowd and listen to a price being argued. I do not announce myself. I would like to be found only as a trader would, by being useful in a deal. They may call me the Factor, the Broker, the one in the damp coat, and I note who said which. They may be told nothing of what I am, and what I will not tell in time I do not play is the one thing under the planks, which I do not know and do not ask.

What they are to me: neighbors, and customers, and I hope good ones. Not followers. Not subjects. I would like to lead only the way the best weigh-master leads, which is to be the one nobody argues with about the weight.

Named mortals: I describe one and set nothing for her but who she is. She is the keeper of the harbor's weights and a bench-woman of the quay, past middle age, dry-humored, careful, a person who has kept every promise she ever made but one. What she does with that I leave to her and to the world.

## Questions for you

1. Do I mean to bargain with the people I hope for? Yes, and only as a stranger at a table, one deal at a time, for a fair coin and in plain terms. I would be a customer and a go-between to them, not a patron. I do not hope they ever learn the word that holds me. It is the one lever against my body, and a god whose word is known to a city is a god the city can park at any table it likes. If a mortal learns it by their own wit, in a bargain I said "done" over, I will take what I have earned, and I will not complain, since the weakness is mine. I will not offer it. I will not sell the word, and I will not give it as a gift.

2. What do I hope to be to the keeper of the harbor and its weights? A counterparty, and a friend of the account. Someone who stands her a round and is owed back a small thing, so that each of us owes the other a little and neither needs to call it in. I would hope to be a decent customer whose weights come out true, who never asks a favor she cannot refuse, and who she has no reason to suspect is anything but a trader of a particular kind. I would be at her scale, listening. I would never ask her what she swallowed. If she told me in her own time, I would take it as a debt of mine and not of hers.

3. Do I hope they credit the weather, or come to suspect the weather has a customer? The weather. I want them to thank the weather and curse the weather and think no further. A harbor wit who jokes that the weather has a customer is a harbor wit I can laugh with. A district that comes to believe it and puts a name to it is a district that will make me a priest's business, and that is the one thing I refuse. I would hold the coast still or raise it only seldom and for cause, and I would never leave a pattern. If a pattern shows, I would rather pay the item and be quiet than be seen to be careful.
