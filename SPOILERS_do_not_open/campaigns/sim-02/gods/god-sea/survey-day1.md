## Answer 1
A harbor at the hour the lamps are lit and the deals are still open. Wet stone, loud stalls, a thousand people haggling and no two of them agreeing on what a thing is worth. Under the planks, something deep and patient keeping the change. The truest world is crowded and warm on top and very, very deep underneath.

## Answer 2
Coast. Always coast. Drowned coast, tidal flats, estuaries where the river and the salt argue over who owns the mud, and the shelf beyond where the land gives up and the water takes over. Give me a shore with a long reach of sea in front of it and a city with its back to the wall and its feet in the tide. I will take other land if it comes with a quay.

## Answer 3
Four seasons, and make them honest about it. A long mild stretch for trading, a storm season to keep everyone humble, a cold one to make them value a full larder, and a bright one so they forget to be afraid. Storms should have a calendar, but not a reliable one. A little uncertainty keeps the insurance business alive.

As for waking, I will take the season when the ships come home heavy and the harvest is in, when everything is for sale and everyone is feeling generous. I wake into autumn.

## Answer 4
Some of each, and I want the old kingdoms nearer than the wild. Wild country is only good for what comes out of it, and what comes out of it is better when someone old and rich is waiting at the harbor to buy. Old kingdoms across the water, with their own ledgers and their own debts. Wild country behind them, unwalked, so there is always something to be sold as a rumor first.

## Answer 5
Thousands of years, and ruins under ruins, and the lowest ones under the waterline. I like a past that has been sunk. Whatever people built and lost goes down eventually, and what goes down is kept. Make the story old enough that nobody remembers who owes whom.

## Answer 6
Closer to the wise-woman end than the rumor end, but not in every valley. One in a good port town, who tells weather and tides and the price of salt, and is paid in kind. Never so much that a mortal can do without the rest of us. Never so little that a deal has no spice.

## Answer 7
It should feel like a tide: it comes in without being asked and goes out when it is tired, and you cannot hurry it. The cost is the usual one in my trade. You pay up front, in something you did not quite agree to give. A memory of a taste, a name, the weight of a good night's sleep. Small coins, taken often, and you only notice the purse is light when you reach for something you thought was there.

## Answer 8
Barely more than a great mortal sorcerer. Let a god be a very large sorcerer who can be mistaken for weather. I do not want to be the one-eyed man, because the one-eyed man gets noticed, and being noticed is expensive. I would sooner be one customer among many whose account no one has quite checked.

## Answer 9
Strange, and strangest where it is wet. Familiar animals on the land, a few legends in the forest, and in the water things with no name and no manners. Give the deep a hundred fish no one has priced. Give the shallows a few that look back. The land can be sensible. The sea has never been sensible and should not start here.

## Answer 10
Beyond each other, the thing mortals should fear is the thing that takes without asking and does not give back. Drowning is the honest one. Fog is a good one. Sickness that comes on a ship. A hunger that is not for food. And the quiet debt, the one that is called in at the worst time by someone you forgot you owed. A good story has a creditor in it.

## Answer 11
Fresh water, clean and sweet and in reach of everyone, and never enough of it. A city that drinks from the sea is a city that must trade for every cup. Close second: salt of the good grade, which sounds like nothing and is everything. Let them trade for it, scheme for it, marry for it. Do not let them fight for it. Fighting is wasteful. Haggling is a sport.

## Answer 12
Wrecks. Whole fleets of them, on the sea floor and then, when the sea draws back, on the land. Sealed holds with the cargo still in them. Old roads that go down a beach and keep going into the water. Bones too, something enormous, with a hollow place in it where something used to live. I would like to find all of it and I would like not to be the one who is asked what it was.

## Answer 13
Yes, a few, and mostly the kind that live where I do. A people of the shallows who are not human and are not fish and are good at both, who keep their own accounts and do not like being counted. A tall pale folk of the cold coasts who sing at the wrong times. And something at the very bottom that I will not describe, because I would prefer it stayed an item on a list.

## Answer 14
A few, and let them arrive by sea, because everything good does. Strangers with cargo no one can price, and tools no one has a use for yet, and a language that sounds like a sales pitch and is not. I would like many to come and few to stay. The ones who stay should be the ones who bring something I want and cannot name.

## Answer 15
Face to face only. A pull at any distance means every other god can find me whenever it likes, and I do not care for being findable. Let us meet the way merchants meet, across a table, with something between us, and a good deal of discretion about what is on it. Premise bent: I would also accept a faint wrongness in the water when another god is near, as long as it can be mistaken for weather.

## Answer 16
One body, if it can be helped. Several, if it must, and then it should cost something worth paying: for each extra, a share of what I know, divided so thin that no single one of me can be sure what the whole of me agreed to. Spread myself and I am cheaper and easier to cheat. A god who is in two places is a god who has lost track of one of them.

## Answer 17
A trader, a go-between, a clever liar with a conscience that comes in late. Someone who can talk a storm into a discount and a king into a bad bargain, and who ends up owing someone more than they can pay. I want a mortal whose story is that they kept every promise but one. I would follow that one all the way down.

## Answer 18
A companion, I think. Something that is not afraid of the deep and does not ask what I ate. A place is a good second: a bottom to the sea that has a floor. And a truth I cannot put a price on, because I have never found one I could not, and I would like to be wrong about that.

## Answer 19
That nobody ever asks me what I swallowed. That the sea, when it is looked into, is only dark and cold and large, and does not turn out to have a reason. And that the people I end up with are many, and loud, and close together, and warm, and never once look up to see who is keeping the water from falling in on them.
