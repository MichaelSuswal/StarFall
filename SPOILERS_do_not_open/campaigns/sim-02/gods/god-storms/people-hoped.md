# People: unnamed

They may name themselves. I will not name them, and I will not name their home. A name given from outside is a hand laid on a herd.

## Who they are
Human. A herding people of the grass and the ridges, a little wary of the sky and used to it. They read weather the way other peoples read faces, and they are wrong about it often enough to stay humble. They are in the country already, with a history of their own that I did not make and do not know. I hope they remember something of the half-swallowed lines in the grass and the banks in the hollow, and that what they remember is a saying and not a story anyone can prove. Whatever the land turns out to hold of their past, I take as the world's to say.

To me they are neighbors I have not met. They are not followers or subjects, and I hope they are not a people who owe me anything. I hope to lead them only in the way a dog leads a flock, by being at the edge and being noticed by no one. If they never learn there was a leader, that is the best of it.

## How many
About two thousand, within one to three thousand. Close enough that a call carries across a camp at night, spread enough that no single hollow holds all of them and no single storm or sickness takes the whole count. I would take fewer before I would take more. I am counting them, and a count I cannot hold in my head is a count that will come out wrong at the gate.

## How they live
They move with the herds and the seasons, in groups that part and rejoin, with no fixed seat and no town. In late spring they are on the new grass, in summer on the high ridge pasture where the wind keeps the flies off, in autumn they come down the hollows, and in winter they lie up in the sheltered hollows among the thorn trees where water gathers. They burn old grass to bring on the green, and they leave the ground they have used to rest. Their dwellings are tents of hide and felt, windbreaks and lean-tos, struck and carried. I hope they do not build roofed houses on fixed walls for the winter, because the open sky is what binds me and I would sooner never be tested on it. If they do, it is theirs to do. I do not forbid it and I do not ask for it.

I hope they raise a cairn on a ridge for their dead and for their road, and leave it, and do not return to it as a shrine. I hope they avoid the hollow of worn banks without being able to say why, and that nobody settles there.

## Their code
- Count the herd at the gate, every night, aloud, and never let one number stand unchecked. A short count is everyone's business until it is found.
- A guest at the fire is fed once without a question, and watched a little after.
- Ground is used and not owned. No one's name is carved on anything. A man who cuts his mark into a stone is gently laughed at, and then asked to stop.
- A quarrel is argued by the fire until morning. The decision is the one that survives the arguing, and it may be reopened by anyone at the next fire.
- The weak are carried by the strong, and the strong are not ruled by anyone. No one is a king.
- Taking from a neighbor's herd is the one thing that ends a welcome.

I notice that this code counts mostly their own. I do not ask them to change it, and I do not claim it is kind to the stranger. It is the shape I hope for, and I know where it is thin.

## Skills and culture
Herding, tracking, horse-handling, felt-making, hide-working, the old crafts of a people before steel. Counting by knotted cords and by notches, kept in the head more than on any cord. Weather-lore as proverb and old saying: a great many of them, some wrong. Song at the fire, long and with many verses, and a good deal of sour fermented milk. A custom of telling the same story differently at each fire and letting the versions argue. They have a saying about an old herder who comes and goes and is good with weather. It is a joke and a comfort, and it does not point at anyone.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed. I ask for no edge over any other people. A people with an edge gets watched, and a people watched gets bargained with. I would sooner they be ordinary and hard to find than strong and easy to fight over.

## Their magic
None described. At most a rumor: that there is a hand or two among them with a knack for weather or for an animal's moods, nothing anyone plans around, nothing anyone would be sure of. I do not describe a mortal with a knack, and if the world gives them one, I ask only that it cost the worker something they would feel losing and not be common.

## What they believe about gods
Nothing about me. They do not know I am there. I hope they hold no god of the kind that takes an offering, and no priest, and no altar. They may have sayings about the sky and the weather, and they may speak of the sky as a thing that counts its own, and that is theirs to say or not say. I will take no prayer. If they begin to pray to me, I leave until they stop. I do not hope that they know what I am or that I exist as a god, and I do not hope they know my powers.

## Hierarchy
I reject one. There is a council of elders and headmen, chosen by no ceremony, who argue by the fire all night and get it wrong and keep arguing and are right often enough to keep the herd alive. They answer to no one, and I give them no orders. I serve them with counsel at most, and I hope not even that, only the weather turned a little in their favor on a hard night, which they will take for luck. Orders do not travel from me, because there are none. Disputes between households are settled by the elders at the fire, in the open, with anyone free to listen. Between peoples, the headmen go and talk.

With no hierarchy, my unplayed time runs on my own conduct alone and cannot be delegated. I accept that. In the time I am not played I do these things and no others: I stay at the edge, I count, I take no prayer, I accept no name, I give no order, and I leave if they begin to pray.

## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history, going about their work, not waiting for anything. I come down into the weather as the first great storms break, find them, and stay out of the light. I take no place of honor. I hope to be found, if at all, as an old herder who turns up at the edge of a camp after a storm and is fed without a question, and as a stray gelding that a herd takes in. I do not say how I mean to be found beyond that. I leave it to the world and to them.

A place they keep in common: none. They have no gate, hall or boat, and I will not ask that any one person's word answer for a place that turns on me.

Named mortals, described and not set. One of them can hold a herd, a household and a quarrel in his or her head and say what each is worth, is too stubborn to stop when it hurts, is clever and loyal and not noble, and is a little drunk on a good night. A headman who is right too often to be loved. That one has a daughter or a brother who is better at the thing the counter is worst at. I do not say what that one does, whom that one loves, or what becomes of them. I name no one, and the people may.

## Questions for you
1. How I hope to come among them the first time, and as which shape. As the horse. A grey gelding, ribs a little showing, rough-coated, drifting in at the edge of a herd in the clear cold light between two storms, with nothing on it and nothing carried. The herd takes it in, and no one looks twice at an old horse. The man comes later, as the herder, and only once I have counted the camp, and only to someone who is already out in the weather and already tired, so that the face goes in a season and the cord and crook and voice stay. I will not come as a stranger who announces himself, and I will not come in a way that gives a child a story.

2. Whether I hope any one of them knows what the old herder is. No. I hope no one knows, and I hope the counter does not ask. I am honest that there is a corner of me that would be glad of one who knows and says nothing and does not pray. I do not ask for it, I do not hope for it as a plan, and I would not trust it if it came, because a person who knows is a person who can bargain. If one comes to know, I will count that one as mine, and I will learn the name, and the rest I will take as it comes.

3. Where I hope to be when they are under their roofs. Outside. In the winter hollows, among the thorn trees on the sheltered side, in the horse shape, in the herd, in the cold, under the open sky. I do not sleep in their tents and I do not stand under a hide roof if I can stand beside it. I hope their winter quarters are tents and windbreaks and not fixed roofs, and if they are fixed, I stay at the edge of the camp under the sky and count them from there. If I must go under a roof for the length of one night, I do, and I am out by the next dusk, because a second night costs me the horse and my powers and my head. I will not be kept, and I hope no one learns that I could be.
