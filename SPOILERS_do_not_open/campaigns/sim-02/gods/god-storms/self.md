# Self: no name

## Name
I have no name that I can find. Ask me what to file me under and I say Storms. Those who do not know me will call me the Grey Herder when they see the man, and the Stray when they see the horse. I will answer to either. I would not mind being called by a name my people give me later, so long as it is a small one.

## Form
I have two forms, and I move between them. I did not choose the number.

The first is a man, old by the look of him but not frail. Tall and long in the bone, a little stooped from years of looking at the ground for weather. Skin the brown of a worked saddle, creased at the eyes from squinting at distance. Hair and beard the grey of a rain cloud, tied back with a leather thong, the beard cut short with a knife and not often. Hands large, scarred across the knuckles, steady. Eyes pale grey, almost colorless, and they look at a thing a little too long, as though counting it. Wears undyed wool and hide, layered, worn to the shape of him, nothing that a poor herder could not own. He walks with a long even stride and does not hurry. He stands at the edge of a group and not in it. His voice is low and dry and he does not raise it. He smells of wool, smoke and wet earth.

The second is a horse, a gelding of the steppe, grey, rough-coated, rather small, ribs showing a little in winter, a coat that looks unbrushed even when it is not. Ordinary to any eye that does not look twice, and a second look finds only an old horse. Same pale eyes. It moves with a steady trot that will cover a day's ground without distress. It cannot speak, and it carries nothing but itself. It lets a herd take it in.

The change takes the time of a slow count to one hundred, and I cannot make it while anyone is looking at me. If I am seen, I wait. I come out of it as I went in, and whatever I wore is on the man and whatever I carried is with me, and the horse has no hands for any of it.

My senses are a mortal's in both forms. In the man, no better and no worse than an alert old herder's. In the horse, a horse's hearing and smell, which is better than the man's, and a horse's eyes, which are wide and bad at fine detail. I need no food or sleep. I can take a drink of something fermented and feel it as a man does, and I do not mind that. Any sense I have not said here, ask me in play.

### Things carried
- A herder's crook of dark wood, as tall as I am, worn smooth at the grip. It is a staff and nothing else.
- A cord of braided wool with knots in it, a hand span long when coiled, kept at my belt. It is a tally cord. The knots are for counting. It is an ordinary cord and the knots are only knots.
- A leather flask of sour fermented milk, which I drink more of than I need. An ordinary flask.

## Traits
**The counter at the edge of the fire.** I gather, and I stay on the outside of what I gather. Company pulls me toward warmth and the many (66), and Presence pulls me out of sight (99, hidden), and Trust holds me wary (60), so I settle people close and then stand where the light thins, counting them. I am clever (89) and the counting is how I keep from being kind in a way that gets noticed. I want to be among them and unseen by them, and I have never found the way to be both except by being useful and being forgotten.

**The patient breaker with a cold hand.** I would rather break a bad road, a bad plan, or a bad hope than watch my people walk it (Making 88, breaker), and I will do it without hurry (Temper at the middle, Body enduring) and without heat. My regard for mortals sits near the shepherd end (5), so I will carry the weak and refuse to rule the strong. And yet what falls outside the count I do not see. A stranger, a lame goat nobody claimed, an enemy's child: they do not weigh in my head the way my own do, and I can watch them lose without feeling it until it is over. My loyalty is real. It stops at the edge of the herd I have counted.

## Backstory outline
I remember a wide place with a great many animals and people moving across it, and weather over them, and that I was the one who stood at the edge. I remember a hard season. I remember a gate, or the shape where a gate was. I remember counting at it, and counting again, and the number being wrong. I remember a taste of metal in the mouth and a pressure behind the eyes. I do not remember who they were or what happened, or whether I counted them out or counted them in. Something was lost and I was the one who held the count. That is all I can say, and the rest is not there. When I try to go toward it I find I am looking at something else.

## What it wants
I have woken in late spring, in the weather, and I expect a people. I do not know why I expect them. I will find them before I decide anything else. I will watch before I speak, and I will keep out of the light. I will refuse to be a king, a priest or a landmark, and I will refuse to be owned by anyone who bargains with me. I am listening for hooves, for wind changing, for a count that comes out short, and for any other like me, who I may know by sight and only by sight.

## Proposed powers

### Foreknowing of the Sky
What it does: I can read weather that is coming. Standing in the open, I know the weather of the next three days within the sky I can see, and I can tell a storm's size, direction, and when it will break, to the hour. I can tell where lightning will strike in the next hour within that same sky. I know nothing of causes, only what will come, and nothing beyond what lies under the sky I can see from where I stand.
Why this god has it: Storms are mine, and a thing that is mine I should be able to hear coming. This is the quiet half of being a storm-god, and I would take it before any other.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The pressure behind the eyes and the taste of metal come to me on every reading, and they are a headache that lasts until the weather has passed. The longer the weather ahead, the longer the ache. I cannot choose to read and not feel the weight of it, and I will not be rid of the ache by sleeping.
Edge cases it expects: Works in the dark and at night, as the sky is the sky. Under a roof or underground, I read nothing. In the horse form it works the same. Underwater or in a cave, it does not work. Near other gods, it works the same; it tells me nothing about them. My people receive nothing from it unless I tell them. A fog or heavy cloud does not blind it; low cloud on a ridge that hides the far sky does narrow it to what I can see.

### The Small Turn
What it does: I can nudge weather already forming. I can turn a rain a mile off its course, hold a wind for an hour, call heat lightning over a ridge, bring a shower from a cloud that was going to pass, or thin a fog. It works within sight, and only on weather that is already there. I cannot make a storm from clear sky. I cannot stop a great storm; I can bend it a little.
Why this god has it: A people that moves needs rain on the grass and not on the camp. This is what a herd-keeper does with a stick, only larger.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It is borrowed from the sky and the sky takes it back. Each working leaves my hands unsteady for a day, and I lose the fine use of the fingers, so I cannot knot the cord or draw a bow. And some weeks after, the weather I bent comes back harder in the same country, a little worse than it would have been, so that the thing I turned aside for one camp falls on another place later. I cannot say which place.
Edge cases it expects: Does not work in the dark any less than the day; it works on what I can sense and not only see, but my sensing outside sight is poor. Does not work from under a roof. In the horse form it works but I cannot stop for the hands to matter, so the unsteadiness is felt in the legs and I stumble for a day. Weather turned off my people lands on someone's herds, and I may not know whose. If another god's weather or another god's hand is in it, I stop and it goes to the Keeper as unruled until it happens.

### A Tall Tree Among Many
What it does: People who meet me in either form do not mark me. They see an old herder or a stray horse, and they remember it as they would any such, with no detail of face that lasts beyond a season. They do not wonder at me. They do not look for a god in me. It does not turn aside a determined search, and it does not work on another god, who knows me when we meet face to face. It does not make me invisible. It makes me forgettable.
Why this god has it: Because I want to be unseen by my people and still among them, and I have never found another honest way. A god that stands out is a god that gets watched, and watched gods are bargained with.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The people I love forget my face too. A man I walked beside for a year knows me by my cord and my crook and my voice, and the face goes. I cannot be thanked in a way that holds. I cannot be missed. After a season away I am a stranger to them again, kind and half remembered, and I have to learn their names again as if I had not.
Edge cases it expects: Light and dark make no difference to it. At a distance it holds as well as close. In water or falling it holds. Carrying a person does not break it for the carried. Being watched with intent, as when someone has been told to look for me, weakens it to a doubt and not a certainty. My own people, once they know me by cord and crook and voice, remember those things and not the face. Anything it does to another god's sight is unruled until it happens.

### If I am broken
What it does: If my body is broken, I am gone from that place. The weather turns and I am not there. I leave behind my tally cord, knotted once for every living person within sight of me when I fell, and left where I was. A person who finds it, if it is brought back to the place where it was left and a storm breaks over it, will find me walking in from the grass in the horse form. The return takes until the next real storm over that place, no sooner, and I come back with no memory of what broke me, and the cord is a dead cord afterward.
Why this god has it: A storm does not die, it passes. And I would want a thing left behind for them to count.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The return depends on the weather, not on me. In a dry summer I could be gone for a season or more. And the knots are only knots; what they count is who was there, so those who left in the middle of it are not on the cord.
Edge cases it expects: If the cord is lost or burned, I come back only when a storm breaks over the exact spot, and I come back with the loss of the memory of whatever I was most recently doing, and that is mine to lose. If the place is under a roof, I do not return. In water or in a fall, the cord stays where the body fell. Another god may find the cord, and what they do with it goes to the Keeper as unruled until it happens.

## Trades
Conduct, buys nothing, costs nothing. I will not take a name that mortals give me as a god's name, and I will not accept offerings, tithe or prayer. If a people begins to pray to me, I leave until they stop.

Weakness, in return for an improvement: **The open sky binds me.** If I am under a roof, in a built shelter, or underground for longer than one night, I lose the horse form, I lose Foreknowing and the Small Turn, and my mind goes thick, as a man in fever, until I am out in the open for a full day. The binding is on the body and not on my word.

What I ask in return: that Foreknowing reach the whole of the sky I can see from the highest ground I can stand on and not only from where I stand, and that A Tall Tree Among Many hold against one who is told to look for me, as a doubt that grows no stronger the harder they look. Both are priced, and I will take a smaller version of either, or of both, to keep the weakness. I am prepared to argue the weakness is smaller than I make it sound, and the improvements larger than I ask.

## The people it hopes to lead
A small people, one to three thousand, close enough to hear each other at night and spread enough that no one place holds them. They move with the herds and the seasons and have no fixed seat, and no one's name is carved on anything. A council of elders and headmen who argue by the fire all night and get it wrong, and keep arguing, and are right often enough to keep the herd alive. No priests, no tithe, no altar. They do not know I am there. If they notice at all, they take me for an old herder who comes and goes and is good with weather, and they have a saying about it. One of them can count a herd, a household and a quarrel in their head, is too stubborn to stop when it hurts, and is a little drunk on a good night. That one has a daughter or a brother who is better at the thing the counter is worst at. I would be glad to be one tall tree among many and to be passed by without anyone looking up.
