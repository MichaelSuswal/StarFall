# Self: the Ferryman

## Name
I have a name somewhere and it will not come up. Something of mine has it in its mouth and does not know. Until it comes back, file me under the Ferryman. Mortals who do not know me will call me what they call the thing at the ford: the Old Man of the Crossing, or the Cold One, or nothing at all, and call the weather by my work.

## Form
An old one, of mortal size and mortal pace. Stooped, long in the arm, with the knuckles of a man who has hauled rope for a hundred winters. Skin the grey brown of a wet bank, creased the way dried mud creases, and always faintly damp, so that a hand laid on my sleeve comes away cool. Hair white and thin, hanging as if wet even in the sun. A long face, with a mouth that sits a little crooked, as if halfway through a joke. The eyes are different: the left is dark and steady, the right is pale as river ice and does not seem to settle on anything. People who meet me tend to say they were not sure which of the two was looking at them, and then they cannot say why they said so. Bare feet, broad, with long toes made for stones, that leave wet prints which dry slowly. I wear a coat of undyed river reed and sodden wool, belted with a plain cord. Nothing about me shines. I carry myself the way a man leans on a gate he does not own: easy, patient, in no one's way, and in no hurry to be anywhere else.

I see well in dim light and badly in glare. I hear water better than I hear speech, and I always know the sound of running water within a long walk of me. Cold does not trouble me and I do not shiver. Sense of smell, taste, and what I feel of heat or pain are not described here. Ask me in play.

### Things carried
A coil of plain river cord with a rough knot at one end. It is only rope. I also carry a smooth grey stone the size of a hen's egg, worn through the middle with a hole, which I keep in the coat. It does nothing but sit, and I am fond of it.

## Traits
**Merciful by nature, cruel when it forgets to look.** I am clever, I decide fast, and I hold myself to the letter of a thing: a rule written exactly is a rule I can keep. I am also quick to lean toward people, and I do not guard myself well, so I tend to help whoever is in front of me before I have weighed it. Then my attention moves downstream like water does, and what I left behind is left. I do not choose harm. I stop looking, and the stopped looking does the harm. I know this about myself only in outline, the way one knows a ford is deep.

**Hidden, and wanting to be found.** I would rather be taken for weather than for a god, and I arrange my workings so that no one can trace them to me. But I like company, I like a crowd at a crossing, a people to watch, and under that there is something I cannot name that goes quiet when I am alone too long and sour when I am noticed too much. I want to be near people and unseen by them, to be remembered rightly once and never again worshipped. These pull against each other, and I live in the gap.

## Backstory outline
I was someone to whom people came. There was water, and a place where things were put down for me, and I think there were voices. I was liked, or feared, or both. Then the voices stopped and no one told me why, and the place where I was is somewhere I cannot find. I do not remember a name being said to me last. What I carry is the shape of the absence: a habit of listening for a sound that does not come. When someone laughs near water I turn my head before I know I am doing it. I find it very funny, in a way that is not funny. That is all of it.

## What it wants
To find a people and stand at the edge of them. To see how they cross things. To keep them in clean water, and to make sure no one learns it is me. I want a gate to watch and a stretch of wild water to go and be missed in. I will not take a temple, an altar, or a name said in prayer. I am listening for a ford, a spring that runs cold, someone who is fair by accident, and, though I would not say so aloud, anything that has my name in its mouth.

## Proposed powers
### The Long Water
What it does: While I stand in, or have a hand or foot in, running water, I feel the whole length of that stream as one body: its pace, its fullness, its cold or warmth, where it is blocked or fouled, and where living things cross it or stand in it, as weight and movement and no more. It reaches the water in the course I am standing in, up to its source and down to its mouth, along that one line and not into other branches. I learn what is happening in the water and on its fords and banks within a few paces of it. I do not learn faces, names, words, or intentions, and I do not learn why anything happened.
Why this god has it: I am a river's god, and a river does not stay at one bend. I wanted to feel the length of my own water without standing at each part of it.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): Each use takes a small piece of my own memory, a face, a phrase, a place. The Keeper removes it from my record and I will not know what went. I may only notice later that a thing I used to know is smooth.
Edge cases it expects: Still water, such as a pond or lake, gives only that body of water, not its outflow unless I stand in the outflow. Frozen water gives nothing until I find open water beneath it. Falling water and rapids are felt as noise and make it harder to tell fine things apart. It works in dark and light the same. Distance does not weaken it along the line of the stream, but I can only hold it for as long as I am standing in water; stepping out ends it. My own people standing in the water are felt as weight like anyone else. Another god standing in the stream is unruled until it happens.

### The Gate
What it does: At a ford, ferry, bridge foot, stepping stones, or any other place where a stream is crossed, within sight of where I stand, I may make one crossing easier or harder for those who attempt it. Easier: the water slackens a little, the bed firms, the stones sit true. Harder: the current pulls harder at the legs, the bed goes soft, the stones turn. It is the water of the stream behaving as a stream might, never more than a stream might, and always readable as weather, spring melt, or bad luck. It affects everyone who crosses that place while I hold it, or one crosser if I choose, but only for a crossing, not for the road.
Why this god has it: I stand at gates, and I am merciful and indifferent in turn. A crossing is where a small kindness or a small cruelty lands and is never traced.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): For the rest of that night after I hold a gate, my hands go numb and clumsy, and I cannot feel warmth in anything I touch. The coldness is mine, not the water's.
Edge cases it expects: In flood or freeze it cannot make a crossing safe, only less ruinous. It cannot hold a crossing shut against someone who wades far enough up or down. It does nothing on dry land, and nothing on a bridge that does not touch water. At night it works the same. It does not tell me who crosses beyond what the water shows, and it does not stop an armed man or a beast. My own people crossing are affected like anyone's, unless I choose otherwise. Used against or near another god, it is unruled until it happens.

### The Face of the Weather
What it does: Anything the Long Water or the Gate does is read by mortals as weather, current, or luck, and leaves nothing that points to me. If I am standing in plain sight at a crossing doing it, I look like an old man resting, and no one thinks I did a thing.
Why this god has it: I would not be worshipped again.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The same hiding hides me from those who would help me. A mortal who tries to thank me, pray to me, or recall my name at a crossing I worked gets nothing back, and I feel the words go past me into the water without landing. I will not be able to answer them.
Edge cases it expects: It does not hide me from another god, and does not hide my form from a mortal who looks at me. It hides only my workings. If I choose to be seen working, I may, at the price of the first drawback doubling on that day. Written as: unruled for other gods.

### When the body is broken
What it does: If my body is broken, I am gone from that place in a single breath, like a stone slid under water. I leave behind the smooth grey stone with the hole through it, and only that. The next time any running water of the same stream is touched by a living person who means no harm, I rise from the nearest stretch of that water, wet and whole and a little slow, and I remember being gone. I cannot choose where I rise.
Why this god has it: Water does not stay broken. It goes elsewhere and comes back.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The stone. If it is taken from where I left it, I cannot return until it is placed back in running water of that stream, and I will not know who has it.
Edge cases it expects: It works in flood, in freeze, in daylight, and at night. If the water of that stream has dried or been dammed, I rise at the nearest water that moves. A god who breaks my body is unruled until it happens.

## Trades
I offer a weakness that binds the body: I cannot cross a gate, ford, bridge, or threshold that someone is keeping, whether they stand at it, guard it, tend it, or claim it, unless I give that keeper one true thing, a word or a fact I know and they do not. If I have nothing true to give, I stand where I am. I ask in return that the Long Water follow the stream into its branches, so that I feel the whole river and every water that joins it, rather than one line. I would also ask that the Gate carry to a second crossing I can name but cannot see, once per night, if the Keeper will price it. Keep the conduct as conduct: I will not take worship, a temple, or a prayer, and that buys nothing and costs nothing.

## The people it hopes to lead
Not many. About five thousand, in a walled seat or town on a river, with a king or a lord who answers to me without being told so and without a priesthood between us. They should know laws and keep them exactly, and be glad of a clear word and a clear rule. They should not know I am there. I would like them to be crossers, ferrymen and toll keepers and traders who live by the ford and the spring, and fair or unfair in ways I can watch. I would like them to keep their water clean and to bargain over it without cruelty. I do not want to be thanked. I would like, once, for one of them, with no reason to flatter me, to say the right thing about me and laugh.
