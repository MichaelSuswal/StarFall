# People: unnamed

They have no name that I set for themselves or for their home. Let them name both. I would only hope the name has water in it.

## Who they are
Human. A river people who live by crossing: ferrymen, toll keepers, boatwrights, weir and fish trap tenders, traders who move goods over the fords, and the farmers of the terraces who feed them. They are fair and unfair in the ordinary mixed way of people, and I want them that way, because I want to watch what they do and not be handed a flattering people.

## How many
About five thousand. I take the usual number. I take a walled seat on the river with a landing below it, such as the land shows, and the rest in hamlets at fords and along the terraces. I do not shrink them. A crossing people needs many crossings, and many crossings need many hands.

## How they live
By the water and by its crossing. Boats drawn up above the flood, weirs, traps, tilled terraces above the flood line, water meadows grazed in summer and left to the river in spring. They know the river's moods and keep a reckoning of its height each thaw. They treat clean water as the thing they cannot do without, and bargain over it, hard but not cruelly.

## Their code
Law, kept exactly. They hold that a rule is a rule only when it is written plainly and said the same way every time. They post their laws, as they post their fords, on markers and boards at the places where the law applies. They are glad of a clear word. A bargain struck in the exact words is kept even when it hurts the one who struck it. A keeper of a crossing who takes more than the posted toll, or who lets in a lie, is shamed in the open under the posted law, not privately. They keep their water clean as a matter of law and of habit: no fouling a spring, no fouling a stream above a landing, and a fine that is written down.

## Skills and culture
Rope, knots, boats, ferries, fords and the reading of water. Weirs and fish traps. Measuring: poles marked for the river's height, weights and measures for tolls and trade, tallies cut in wood. Bargaining, and the keeping of what is bargained. A habit of telling the exact truth at a crossing, partly because the posted law asks it and partly because a keeper who is caught lying loses the crossing. A fondness for jokes told at the landing. Songs about the river. The people tell time by the river and the thaw.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I propose none. They have no rare skill, no unusual longevity and no magic that other peoples lack. Their strength is only that they are many at a river, and the world gives that to anyone.

## Their magic
None that I describe. If the world wants a rumor, let it be only the rumor that the old man at certain fords seems to know the river, and that the water is kind or hard to people in ways that look like luck. A mortal with a knack I do not ask for.

## What they believe about gods
Before I arrive they honor the river as a thing, as people do, with small habits at the water: a handful of grain, a word at the ford. I would rather they had little more than habit. They do not know I am there, and they do not know what my powers are. They may tell stories of an Old Man of the Crossing or a Cold One, as one tells of weather. I ask for no temple, no altar, no priesthood and no prayer to me. If they already keep such things for another being, that is theirs to keep and I take no part in it.

## Hierarchy
I want one, light, and I do not want it to know why it answers me.

A lord, or a warden of the crossings, who holds the walled seat and the landing. He or she answers to me without being told so, because the lord's own law and the lord's own sense of fair dealing happen to run where I would want them. Under the lord, the keepers of the fords and ferries, each answering for the crossing they keep. Disputes are settled at the landing by the lord and three keepers under the written law, and the judgment is posted. I give no orders and have no messengers among them. Orders travel the way the lord's orders always have, from the lord to the keepers by word and by the posted board.

I reject a priesthood and any advisor whose office is to speak for me. I reject any hierarchy that carries my wishes as my wishes.

I know the cost. With no one to carry my word, my unplayed time runs on my own conduct alone and cannot be delegated. I take that cost.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history and their own law. They are not waiting for me, and I did not arrive with them. I come to them as an old man at a ford, resting, who talks too much and laughs at the wrong moments. I mean to be found only as that. I will not take a temple, an altar, a name said in prayer or thanks, and I will not tell them what I am. In time I do not play I will not tell them anything beyond what I owe at a crossing by my own weakness, and I will not set my hand on the lord. If I am ever to be remembered rightly, let it be once, by one of them with no reason to flatter me, and let that one laugh.

## Questions for you

1. How do I hope to reach such a lord, when I will not be seen working and cannot be thanked? I do not reach the lord. I hope to reach the fords. I stand at a crossing, the old man resting, and give a keeper one true thing when the toll asks it, and let the keepers carry it to the lord as what they heard at the ford. I trust the lord to be a person who weighs what keepers tell them, and I watch whether the lord is. I do not speak to the lord as a god and I do not shape the lord. If no such lord arises, then I do not have one, and I watch the people as they are. I cannot be thanked, and I accept that as the cost of the Face of the Weather.

2. Do I hope the keepers know why the old man at the ford tells them things? I hope they do not. I hope they take him for a garrulous old man whose small facts turn out to be true, and I hope they come to trust the old man's word at the crossing for no better reason than that it has proved right. I know the toll can be used against me if a keeper learns it, and that a people with keepers on every ford could learn it. I take that risk. I would rather be an old man who says true things than a god whom keepers fear or flatter.

3. Whose laws do I hope they keep? Theirs. The ones they have. I do not give them laws unseen. A law I gave them in secret would make me a ruler in the dark, and a law only I knew was the origin of would be a law I could not keep exactly. I hope the laws they have are plain and posted and exact, and I want to learn them, not write them. If they ask me, as the old man at the ford, whether a posted law is fair, I would answer as a man would, and I would not answer as a god.
