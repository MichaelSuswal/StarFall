# People: unnamed

We leave the people and their home unnamed. Let them name themselves, and let the world name the rest. We would not be the one who gave them a word.

## Who they are

Human. A stubborn, plain-spoken, unhurried people of the high country, already there, with their own history and their own account of the mountain. We hope they live in the walled seat at the foot of the valley, behind the well-laid stone, and that they have been in the mountains a long time, long enough to have buried many people and to have lost some roofs and learned from it. We do not hope for conquerors, seekers or a chosen people. We hope for people who stay.

We hope they are neighbors to us, and not followers or subjects. If it must be one word, neighbors. We hope to look after them. We do not hope to lead them in the sense of being asked, and we say plainly that we are not sure we are fit for it.

## How many

Around five thousand, in the walled seat and its nearest terraces. We take the usual size. We would take fewer before we took more, since a roof is held one at a time, but we will not argue a number, and we do not want it made small for our sake.

## How they live

In the lower valley, in houses of laid stone with deep roofs, through a long hard winter, a late spring and a short summer. They keep terraces and flocks and put up stores for the cold. They go to the high places for stone, timber and pasture, and some of them know the old cut openings and the old terraces under the snow, and leave them alone. They do not leave the valley when it is hard. They keep a room in every long winter-house for whoever comes in from the cold, and the walled seat does not shut its gate on a stranger in deep snow.

## Their code

- They keep honest count of the living and the dead. Every birth and every death is spoken aloud to the household and set down, and a count that does not come right is not let alone until it does.
- They say what is so, even when no one wants to hear it, and they do not make a person say otherwise.
- They look after the small, the lost and the hurt before they ask whose they are.
- They do not abandon a roof, a cellar or a shaft with someone under it.
- They do not worship to a face, and they do not ask a being to be their ruler.

## Skills and culture

Ordinary and unpriced. They are good with stone: laying it dry, setting lintels, reading a slope, knowing which ground is sound and which is not, and they talk about it the way other peoples talk about weather. They build roofs well and re-lay them every few years. They are slow to trust and quick to feed a guest. They teach children the sounds of the mountain, the groan of timber, the settling of a wall, the low rumble before a slide, and teach them to go quietly and tell someone. Their songs are low and slow. They keep a little at the lowest hearth stone of each house and say nothing of it. They keep a room for a guest who does not come, and nobody explains it to a child except that it is kept.

## Edges and their weaknesses

We propose none. We ask for no edge over other peoples, no rare skill, no long life and no magic. Their skill with stone is the ordinary skill of a people who have lived a long time under heavy mountains, and we ask the Keeper to count it as culture. We would rather they be a people no one has reason to take from, and whose strength is that they hold.

We will say one thing for the Keeper's pricing and not as an ask: the Held Place already carries a roof for a people, and that is priced on us, on the card, with the Winter Stillness. We ask that nothing about this people be priced again for it.

## Their magic

None that we describe. At most a rumor, the sort every high valley has: an old woman who is said to know when a slope will go, a man whose hands are said to find water. Mostly doubted, sometimes proved, and never a power anyone could buy. If the world gives them a mortal with a knack, we would have it be small and cost the body, and we would want to be told. We describe none.

## What they believe about gods

They half know. They have a custom and no creed. Most of them believe there is something old and quiet in the stone that does not like to be thanked, and some say it is only a custom. They have no name for it, no image of it, and no story that says it is a child. They do not know what any god's powers are, ours least of all. They know that some roofs have not fallen when they should have, and they have not agreed on why. We hope the doubt stays, and that it is never total.

If a god comes to them in the open, we hope they treat it as they would any stranger at the gate in deep snow.

## Hierarchy

We reject one. There is no king, no priest, no chief, no advisor who carries our wishes, and no one who rules under us. Orders do not travel from us, because we give none.

We say how they settle things among themselves. Each household speaks for itself. Matters that touch more than one household, such as a boundary, a shared wall, a flock on the wrong pasture or a debt, go to a gathering of the heads of households at the gate in the dry cold of the year, where the one with the grievance speaks first and everyone is heard before anyone is answered. Those who keep the count of the living and the dead are trusted to say what is so about who is alive, who is owed and who owes, and they are not trusted with anything else and rule nothing. Anyone may speak. A matter that will not settle is left standing, and nothing is taken by force while it stands.

We know the cost. With no hierarchy, our unplayed time runs on our own conduct alone and cannot be delegated, and a people with no one to carry a word will not carry ours. We take that cost. We would take the heavier cost before the written fence.

Whose word answers for a place the people keep in common: the lowest hearth stone belongs to the household that keeps it, and that household's word answers for it. The walled gate, and the room kept for the guest, belong to the gathering. We name no single person.

## How the god meets them

They are already there, with their own history. We arrive into deep winter, awake, and we come down unseen from the heights toward the path that climbs from the walled seat. We mean to be still first and listen before we move. We mean to learn where their ground is sound before we are seen by anyone.

We hope to be found, if at all, as a child at the gate or on the path in the snow, with an undyed cloak that is too long, and we hope to be taken in as any child would be. We will not be worshipped to our face. We will not be made a ruler. If we are asked what we are, we will say what is so, and we will choose when, and we will not lie to them.

What we will not do, in time we do not play: we will not set a priest, a tithe or a law with our name on it. We will not take the offering. We will not use the people to hide behind. We will not lie to them to be kind. In the winters we are still, we ask nothing of them and they owe us nothing.

## Questions for you

1. Every winter after your first you lie still while the ground is frozen, and anyone may find you. Do you hope any of the people know where the child sleeps, and if so, who?

We hope one household knows, and no more than one: the household that keeps the room for the guest who does not come. We hope it is the room where the child is laid, and that it has been laid there before and left alone. We would not have a guard and we would not have a watch. We would have a family that goes in each morning to count the breathing and finds it counted, and says nothing of it outside the door. We hope no one carries the child, binds it or walls it in. We know that anyone who learns it has us every winter, and that this is the weakness we took. We would rather trust one house than hide behind none, and we say that this is a wager, and that we may be wrong. We hope that if the household fails, it fails by neglect and not by use. If the world gives the people no such household, then we hope no one knows, and we will choose where to lie down and choose a place that is not seen, as we said on Night 2.

2. A roof you hold for a whole season stays held until the end of the next winter. You have one body. Which roofs do you hope to stand under first, and do you hope the people know why those roofs do not fall?

First the roof where the most people sleep through the cold and cannot get out quickly: the long winter-house, the cellar under it, and whatever shaft or cut they go down into for stone or stores. After those, the roof over the gate and the gathering place, then the roofs of the poor, the very old and the very young, which are the worst laid and the most likely to groan. We would choose by who is under it and how badly it is laid, and not by whose it is. We cannot hold in winter, so these are spring, summer and autumn roofs, and we say that most of the people will go under unheld roofs in the winter we are still, and that will weigh on us.

We hope they do not know why. We hope they know only that those roofs have held, and that they say so to each other, and are not sure. That is the hope we have. We say again that if anyone asks us, we will say what is so, and we will not lie to hide it. We hope no one asks, and we know that hope is not a rule.

3. You hope they leave a little at the lowest hearth stone and keep a room for a guest who does not come. Do you hope ever to be the guest?

Yes. We hope to be, once, and not in the way a god is a guest. We hope to come in cold from the snow and find the room kept, and the hearth stone with a little left on it, and that no one stops what they are doing to look at us. We hope to sit and not be asked what we are, and to be told we may stay. It is the one place we want to be allowed to stay. We would not take what is left on the stone, because it is not ours, and we say we do not know yet whether we would be able to say who we are when the room is ours. If we are asked, we will say.
