# Self: the Still Child

## Name
We have no name that we can find. When we reach for one there is only the cold of a floor. Those who do not know us will call us the quiet child, or the stone child, and we will answer to either. If we must be filed, file us under Stone. We say "we" of ourselves, and we would not be corrected on it.

## Form
A child of perhaps seven winters, small and narrow, a little under four feet tall, thin through the shoulders and wrists. The skin is pale grey brown, the colour of wet slate drying, cool to the touch in any weather and never flushed by cold or effort. The hair is dark, coarse and cropped unevenly, as if cut with a flint edge by someone in a hurry. The face is round and plain and very still. The eyes are wide, set a little far apart, a flat grey with no bright in them, and they move slowly and rest long on a thing before leaving it. We blink rarely. The mouth is small and does not smile easily. The hands are broad for the size of the child, with short thick fingers and nails like chipped shale.

We stand more than we sit, and when we stand we do not shift our weight. We walk at a child's pace, without hurry and without skipping, and we place each foot as if testing the ground. We do not run. We are heavy for our size; we lift a thing the way a stone is lifted, all at once or not at all. Cold does not trouble us, and we do not shiver. We do not tire in the body, and we need no food. Sleep is ours to choose, and we choose it seldom and lightly.

Senses we describe: sight is ordinary and good in dim light, poor in glare, which we squint against and avoid. Hearing is keen for low sounds, the groan of timber, a stone settling, a heavy step, and we lose small high sounds in crowds. Touch is exact through the soles of the feet and the palms. The voice is high and quiet, and carries in still, enclosed places. Smell, taste and any other sense we leave unsettled, to be asked in play.

### Things carried
An undyed wool cloak, too long for the child, belted with a plain twisted cord. Nothing else. We carry no tool, no token and no stone.

## Traits
### Stays under the weight
Reconciles Enduring (Body 95), Hidden (Presence 77) and the flaw of fear. We do not flee what is heavy, and we do not stand where we can be looked at. We hold, quietly, out of sight, and the fear is why we hold: we cannot bear for a thing to fall, so we put ourselves beneath it and let no one see us do it. We would sooner be underestimated than looked at. We will bear a long cost to avoid a short, loud one.

### Says what is so, and keeps what is ours
Reconciles Sovereign (Regard 78), Freedom (Order 78) and the virtue of honesty. We believe the people under our hand are ours to keep, and we do not want a throne, a priest or a law with our name on it. We will not lie to them or to anyone, and we will not be made to say otherwise, but we will choose carefully what we say and when, and silence is not a lie. We keep without ruling. Where those two pull, we take the heavier cost before the written fence.

## Backstory outline
We were in a hollow, in the dark, and it was dry and cool and there was a slow sound far above. We were not alone. There was a second small breathing beside ours, close, and then there was the quiet after it, and a weight that was ours and that we did not set down. We do not look at that quiet. We know only that we were not allowed to stay in that place, and that we have been waiting a long time for someone to say we may stay somewhere. Before the hollow, nothing. After it, the landing, and the cold, and the sense that others came down when we did.

## What it wants
To wake in the deep of winter, when nothing is asked of us, and to be still and listen before we move. To find the way in to the high valley, if there is one, and to learn where the ground is sound and where it is not. To find the people we expect and look after them without being seen to. We will refuse to be worshipped to our face, refuse to be made a ruler, and refuse to lie, even to be kind. We are listening for the groan of a slope, a roof, a bad floor, and for anyone small and lost who is near. We would keep one door open and one place where we are allowed to stay.

## Proposed powers
### The Held Place
What it does: We can lay both hands on stone, earth packed hard as stone, or a stone wall or roof, and hold it. While we hold, the stone does not shift, crack, slide or fall within a body length of our hands, and a roof or slope of ordinary size above that reach is carried with it, steadied by our hold. Load that would break it does not. We do not move the stone and we do not make it stronger; we only keep it from letting go. It is meant for roofs, cuts, shafts, cellars and slopes, so that a family can be under them and not listen.
Why this god has it: We are the one who stays beneath the weight. It is the thing we most want to give, a roof that will not fall.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While we hold, we cannot take our hands from the stone or move our feet, and we feel the whole load as pressure in the body, rising the longer we hold, until we are in pain and cannot speak above a whisper. If we are made to let go, all that was held goes at once, in a rush, on whoever is under it.
Edge cases it expects: In light or dark it is the same, as it is by touch. At a distance it does not work; we must touch. Falling, if we are falling, we cannot hold. Carrying, we cannot hold while we walk. Water, it holds the stone but not the water. Watched, anyone may see us standing very still with our hands on a wall, and that tells them little. Other gods, a god in our reach does not stop it, and the effect on a god or on another god's power is unruled until it happens. Our own people, they are safest under it and we ask nothing of them for it.

### The Listening Stone
What it does: With a bare hand or bare foot on living rock, we hear through the stone for about a mile around: footfalls, water moving, a slope working loose, a hollow, hammering, the weight and number of those walking. We hear effects only, what moves and how heavy, never who, never why, never words.
Why this god has it: We want nothing to arrive quickly, and we do not like to be surprised.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While we listen, we are deaf to the air and can hear nothing spoken near us, and the deep slow sounds of the mountain come large and near, so that the fear in us is loud. We cannot stop at once; it takes a slow count of breaths to come back.
Edge cases it expects: Dark and light change nothing. Distance past a mile is nothing. Falling or carrying, we need a hand or foot on rock and a still body. Water and loose earth or snow stop it. Being watched, a child kneeling with its palm to a rock is seen as a child. Other gods, a god who keeps still in sight of us we hear as weight and nothing more; any perception of a god by this power is unruled until it happens. Our people, we may hear their steps and count them, nothing of their words.

### The Unmarked Way
What it does: In stone places, in caves, cuts, tunnels and on bare rock, we leave no trace of our passing: no track, no scuff, no echo of our step, no smell on the stone. Those who come after find the stone as it was. It does nothing to what is seen of us while we are in sight.
Why this god has it: We would rather be a rumor than a pull. We want the high places to keep their own account and not to have our mark.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While we are on marked stone we also cannot leave a sign for anyone we mean to find us, and those we would want to find us cannot find us by any track or mark until we leave the rock. We may be hidden from our own.
Edge cases it expects: Light and dark are as they are, and we may be seen in either. Distance does not matter. Falling, the mark of our fall is not erased. Carrying, what we carry leaves its own mark. Water washes anyway. Watched, it hides the trace, not the child. Other gods, a god tracking us by trace finds none; a god that sees us is not hindered. Our own people may not find our trail.

### The Break (what happens when our body is broken)
What it does: If our body is broken past mending, we are gone from that place, and one small stone of the place, the size of a child's fist, is left behind where we were, warm and plainly out of its kind. We return from the nearest deep stone to that place, after a winter has passed, as a child, whole, in the cold, and unmarked. If the left stone is carried away, we return from the stone nearest to it instead, and it takes longer. If it is broken, our return is delayed by another winter.
Why this god has it: We cannot die, and we do not want to be set to lie where we fell in sight of whoever did it. We would rather leave something to be found than be looked at.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): We do not remember the last season before the break, and what we learned in it is lost, and those who knew us before may not be found as we left them. The loss is felt as a cold place in the mind.
Edge cases it expects: Water, if we are broken in water, the stone is left at the bank nearest. Being watched, those who broke us see us go and see the stone. Other gods, a god that takes or holds the stone has made a choice that is unruled until it happens. Our people, they may find the stone and keep it, and we would have them keep it.

## Trades
We offer a weakness that binds the body, the Winter Stillness: in the deep of every winter, for as long as the ground is frozen shut, we fall into a cold stillness in the stone, unable to act, speak or be easily woken, and anyone who finds us finds a child sleeping in the cold. It is meant to be real. We ask in return that the Held Place last. Once we take our hands away from a roof or slope we have held for a full season, it should stay as steadied as it was held for a year after, and not go at once. We would rather carry a heavier drawback than be fenced by a written rule, and we will bear a heavier weakness for a longer hold.

We keep one rule as conduct, and it buys nothing and costs nothing: we will not lie, to any mortal or to any god.

## The people it hopes to lead
A people in a walled seat or small town at the foot of the mountain, around five thousand, who know us only as a rumor and a custom. They have no king and no priest. They leave a little at the lowest hearth stone and say nothing of it, and there is a household that keeps a room for a guest who does not come. They are stubborn, they build their roofs well, they keep honest count of the living and the dead, and they do not leave the valley when it is hard. We would like them not to know we are here. We would like to hold the roof over them and be, to them, only the reason it does not fall.
