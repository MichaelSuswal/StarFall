# People: unnamed, and let them name themselves

I leave the people and their home unnamed. A name given from above is a leash with a pretty face. If they have a name for themselves when I find them, I will use theirs and no other.

## Who they are
Human. A herd people of the grass and the broken hills at its edge, who have their own history and were here before I came down. They are clever enough to read the land, hard enough to survive its teeth, and honest enough to write down what they paid. They keep their own tales of where they came from, and I hope they have a few good wrong ones about the bones on the hillside.

## How many
Small. About two thousand, within a range of one thousand to three thousand. They live as several bands that travel apart for most of the year and gather close when the herds gather. I do not want a town. I want many near me, and not one roof over them.

## How they live
They follow the herds and eat what they follow. They have no fixed seat. The shelters come down in a morning and the camp is gone by noon, and what is left is a ring of blackened stones and a trodden circle. They cross rivers and do not settle on them. In autumn, when the herds are moving, the bands draw together. In winter they thin out and huddle. They hunt, they herd where they can, they trade for salt, and they fight over water as every people does. Hunger is real to them and I do not hope to take it away.

## Their code
A written law, with every limit named, and the cost of each breach named beside it. I want it exact. The saying is that a rope made of smoke holds nothing. Disputes between bands, and between a band and a man, are settled by the law and by those who keep it, and not by whoever is strongest at the fire. A bargain hidden is a bargain broken, and the law says so. Those who pay a cost write down what it was, so the next one knows the price. I do not ask that the law favor me. I ask that it be written, and that it bind me where I have agreed to be bound.

## Skills and culture
Tracking, reading weather far off, moving a camp fast, butchering and drying meat, working hide and bone and horn, and keeping an honest count. They carry law in memory and on skins. They have sayings and they use them. They sit on the ground to talk. They trade for what the grass will not give, salt first. They are wary of strangers and fair to them once measured. These are ordinary culture and I ask no price for them.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I propose none. They have no rare skill that sets them above their neighbors, no unusual longevity, and no magic most peoples lack. I would rather they be a small people with no edge than a people that owes its standing to a bargain I made.

## Their magic
None that I will describe. A rumor at most, the kind a keeper of the law would have to account for as one accounts for weather. I will not set a mortal with a knack on them. If the world gives them one, I ask only that the cost be written down by those who pay it.

## What they believe about gods
They half know. They have old tales of a Tall One at the herd's edge, who stands still and listens, and they do not agree whether he is a tale for children or a thing that was seen. They do not know my powers. They do not know that I cannot say a false thing, until the keeper of their law finds it out, and I hope that is by asking and not by an accident. I do not want them afraid of me. I will not be a god to a people who are afraid.

## Hierarchy
I reject a ruler under me. I do not rule them and I want no steward, no priesthood, no temple and no tithe. Orders do not travel from me to them, because I give none. I give counsel when asked, and I answer true.

What I hope for is one office, and it is theirs and not mine: a keeper of the law, who walks with the bands and carries the whole law in memory and on skins, and who may stand in front of me and say, that is not what you agreed to, and be right. I describe the keeper, I do not name or direct them. The keeper is someone who writes down what was said and what was meant, and will not bend it for a friend, and I hope also not for me. Beneath the keeper the bands have their own elders and settle their own quarrels by the law. Disputes that reach the keeper are settled by the keeper. Disputes between the keeper and me are settled by the written law, and where the law is silent, by the elders of the bands, and not by me.

Because there is no hierarchy that carries my wishes, my unplayed time runs on my own conduct alone and I cannot delegate it. I accept that. In time I do not play, I will not lie, since I cannot. I will not take a tithe. I will not enter a place of many roofs. I will not break a bargain. I will not hide a bargain. I will not turn a herd until the people's own law has said who it is for.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history, and I come to them. I mean to be found at the herd's edge, standing still, in the open, and not to walk into a camp. I will wait to be seen and I will kneel to speak, which I cannot help. I will answer what is asked and answer it true, and I will hold the rest behind my teeth until I have measured the asker. I want to listen to the land first, and then to them, and I do not want to be met as a wonder. I want to be met as a stranger who keeps his word.

I hope that what I say of the land or its past is heard as a wish and as their belief, and that the world decides.

## Questions for you

1. You cannot say a false thing. Do you mean the people you hope for to know this of you? If the keeper of their law learns it, what do you hope that keeper does with a god whose every word is true?

Yes. I mean them to know it, and I do not hope to hide it. A god whose word can be trusted is worth more to a law than one who must be doubted, and a people that cannot catch their god in a lie are not made small by it. I hope the keeper writes it down, plainly, with its cost: that my silence is also a sign, and that what I will not say is a thing to be asked about carefully. I hope the keeper uses it as a measure and does not lean on it as a crutch. A true word from me is still a word from one who can be wrong, and I would have the keeper test it against the land. I do not ask to be spared the use of it. If the keeper asks me a thing before the bands and I must answer true, my pride will dislike it and I will answer. I hope the keeper also remembers that I can be silent, and does not take silence as a confession. And I hope the keeper never asks me for a kind lie, because I cannot give one, and the keeper should know that before the dying are in the room.

2. You may turn one herd in a season. In a lean season with more than one hungry camp, who do you hope decides which camp the herd is for: you, their law, their keeper?

Their law, and the keeper as the one who reads it. Not me. I would have the law say, before the lean season comes, how a turned herd is to be spent among hungry camps, and the keeper say which camp it is for before I call. If the law is silent on the case, I hope the elders of the camps argue it at the fire, with the keeper writing down what is decided. I will say what I can truly see, where the herd is and what the camps lack, if I am asked. I will not choose between hungry people. A god who decides who eats has made himself a king, and I did not come to be one. If they argue and decide badly, then the law failed to govern the scarcity, and that is the very thing I asked the world for.

3. You hope for a people with no fixed seat and you walk at a mortal's pace, slowed after a turning. Do you hope they wait for you, or go on and let you follow?

Go on, and let me follow. A people that waits for its god has made a seat of him, and a seat that walks slowly is no help to hungry herds. I will not hold up a band for the sake of my own feet. I hope the bands leave their marks as they go, the ordinary signs of a camp that has moved, and that the keeper leaves word of where the next gathering will be. When I am slowed after a turning, I will be behind them, and I will say so if I am asked. I do not ask them to turn back. If I fall far behind and the herds are moving, they should go on, and I will find them by the trail, since reading a trail is what I am for. I would rather arrive late to an honest people than keep an early one waiting.
