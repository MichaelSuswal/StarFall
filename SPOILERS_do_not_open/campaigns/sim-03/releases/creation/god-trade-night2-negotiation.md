# Negotiation: god-trade, Night 2

Your powers, priced. You proposed four powers and one weakness that buys, and asked about one sense.

## Round 1

### How the table prices two things for every god
These two are not offers. They are how this table prices the same thing for any god, and they are written into your card where they apply.
- A loss of memory or habit. Where a drawback takes "a memory or a habit" and you are not told which, the Keeper removes one item from your own record (your record of other gods or your goals) for each time the price falls due, chosen by lot among the items, and you are not told what went. You asked that it stay unspecified; it does.
- A return from a broken body. A full season, at the nearest ground of your own kind to where the largest part of you lies, is paid for by one item lost. A return in a month is paid for by two items lost each time. The table sells nothing faster than a month. If the nearest ground of your kind is more than a day off, the walk to it at your own pace is added to the time. Being held, bound, buried or shut in is not being broken: you stay where you are. Remains kept from the ground of your kind do not return until it reaches them or they are let out. What another god does to your remains is unruled until it happens.

### Your count (a sense, under Form)
Draft text: You always know how many of a thing are in plain view in the room or the ground in front of you, and the count is never off by more than one. It counts what you could see by looking. It tells nothing of worth, intent, or anything hidden, covered or behind you.
The Keeper's reasons: As you wrote it, "how many of a thing are in a room" would count a man behind a curtain. Held to what is in plain view it is a quick eye and is color, at no price. If you want it to count the hidden, it is a power and you may propose it.

### The Lent Seal
Draft text:
Description: You press your stamp to a thing you are about to send (a letter, a token of wax or clay, a person's wrist) and speak into it a message of up to a few sentences. When a mortal carrying that mark reaches the one it was sent to and shows it, your voice speaks the message once from the mark, there and then, for all present to hear. While the mark is carried, those near it feel something of your weight, and the bearer is heard as one who has you behind them. The lending lasts up to a few days. It carries no sight and no sense of what happens at the mark. One seal stands at a time.
Drawback: While a seal stands you are hollowed. No bargain you strike in your own person while it is out binds either party, and you are no stronger in the arm than a strong mortal. If the seal is broken in the bearer's hands you feel it and lose a day: you wake from it unsure what you did.
Limits: It travels at the bearer's pace and can be lost, stolen or stopped as any carried thing can. A thief who shows it gets your message as you left it; you cannot recall it. A wax or clay mark is spent once it speaks; a mark on a wrist fades with the lending. The weight compels no one: no mortal must obey, agree or believe, and a bargain made under it binds only as any bargain does. The dead, the sleeping and those out of earshot hear nothing. It rules no mind. Shown to another god, or set on a thing made or held by more than ordinary craft: unruled until it happens. Your own people get no exemption. Use across a year: one at a time, each a few days.
Choices: none.
The Keeper's reasons: A message still walks at a messenger's pace, so this breaks nothing. What it adds is that the word is known to be yours and comes with weight. The hollowing is a real price for a god whose word is her coin. Accepted as you wrote it.

### The Counted Word
Draft text:
Description: A bargain is two sets of terms spoken aloud, each side saying what it gives and takes, closed by a struck hand or a passed token, in your presence and hearing. When a mortal strikes such a bargain with you, you know from then on whether its terms stand kept, open or broken, and while a debt to you is open you know the direction of the one who owes and roughly how many days' walk off. No more than that: not what the debtor does, thinks or feels, and not why a term broke. You hold a few dozen such bargains at once and no more.
Drawback: You are bound by every term you speak in such a bargain. You cannot lie in the terms, equivocate inside them or fall silent on them, and a term given carelessly is a term you keep. If you break one, the sense is gone for a season: you cannot tell whether any bargain of yours is kept.
Limits: Both sides must understand the terms; a bargain not understood is none. A bargain through a messenger counts only if the messenger carries the Lent Seal. Anyone who learns how you are bound may hold your own terms against you. The power doubles no debt and enforces nothing: what a broken term costs the debtor is your law and your captains' work, not the power's. Distance makes no difference to the knowing. A bargain with another god, or with a thing made or held by more than ordinary craft: unruled until it happens. Your own people get no exemption. It rules no mind.
Choices: none.
The Keeper's reasons: The doubling of a forgotten debt is refused as part of the power: a power that makes a debt grow would be acting on what a mortal owes without anyone's hand, and that is law, which you may make in your own court. The rest stands as you wrote it. Being bound to your own careless word, where others can learn it, is a price.

### The Washing
Draft text:
Description: Before a fight you wash the gear and hands of one who is about to go into it, with water and a cloth, by your own hand, and speak terms over them. For that one fight, a day at most, the one you washed stands a measure harder: a wound that would drop them slows them, a blow that would break a limb bruises it. Afterward the account is called, and they pay what was named and agreed before the washing: a thing they would notice missing. If they do not agree, you do not wash.
Drawback: You carry every wound a washed mortal takes, in your own body, at the same place, as that wound's pain and weakness without its bleeding, for as many days as it takes to heal in them. They add. If they add past what your body can bear, your body breaks.
Limits: Each is washed singly, by your hand, in the time a patient washing takes, about a quarter of an hour. There is no rite over many at once. No number is fenced: your time before the fight and your own body are the limit. It heals nothing already taken and holds for the one fight only. It compels no one to fight or obey. You cannot wash yourself. The mortal's price falls due even if they ran. Washing a god, or gear made or held by more than ordinary craft: unruled until it happens. Your own people get no exemption. Use across a year: every fight you are present before.
Choices: none, unless you counter.
The Keeper's reasons: You asked for a few score in a single rite and offered a heavy drawback in place of a fence. The fence is left off, as you asked. What is refused is the single rite: one washing over scores would make a war band harder than any other in an hour, across a whole people, fight after fight. By hand, one at a time, with every wound coming home to you, the power limits itself. The wounds are written as weakness and not only ache, so that the price is one you would notice in a fight's aftermath.

### The Count Returns
Draft text:
Description: When your body is broken past use, the pieces lie where they fell and do not rot, and you are gone. After a full season the largest piece draws the others to it and you stand up where it lay, whole, in whatever was on that piece.
Drawback: Each time you return, a memory or a habit does not return with you, and you are not told which.
Limits: The return follows the table's rule above. If the largest piece is carried away you return where it is. If it is sealed in metal or shut in a closed place, you do not return until it is let out. If the pieces are so scattered that none is largest, the season begins when a mortal or chance brings two together. Held and not broken, you are held. What another god does to the pieces: unruled until it happens.
Choices: the pace. (A) A full season, one item lost each time. (B) A month, two items lost each time.
The Keeper's reasons: Seven days is refused. A god that is back in a week has not been broken in any way the world would notice, and the table sells nothing faster than a month to any god. You said you would bargain it; the month at its price is the counter.

### The weakness you offer, and what it buys
Draft text:
Open account. A wound to your body does not begin to close until you have given something away unasked to a mortal who needed it (goods, a favour, a measure of your time) and the mortal has accepted it. The giving must be real: unasked, not a bargain, and not meant to be owed. A gift to a god, or to a mortal who does not know it was given, does not count. Once the gift is accepted the wound closes at a mortal's pace from that day. How it can be used against you: one who learns it can keep you wounded by keeping the needy from you, or by refusing what you offer.
The Keeper's reasons, and what it buys: As you wrote it, the wound "closes at once when the gift is accepted". That is a faster healing than a mortal has, bought with a loaf, and it is a gift to you, not a weakness. Written as above it costs you something you do not already pay, because what you give so as to be owed does not count, and that is how you give. It buys one thing, and you name which: two seals standing at once in place of one; or a seal's lending lasting a month in place of a few days; or the Washing's hardening lasting through a second fight within three days in place of one. A season's lending is refused at this price.
Choices: the three purchases above, or keep the weakness as color at no price. The hands stay as color either way.

## Form

One body, in one place, as for every god here (the campaign allows one body).

As you described yourself, in your own words:

A tall woman, human to the eye, in the middle of her years. Broad through the shoulder and long in the thigh, built to walk a road all day and all night and start again. Her face is weathered and plain, a wide mouth that is quick to name a price, grey eyes that look at a person the way a buyer looks at a measure of grain. Her hair is dark and bound back with a cord.

She is visibly wrong in one way: her fingers have an extra joint each, a third knuckle where there should be two. They bend in ways a hand should not, and she counts on them without thinking, folding and unfolding in a rhythm she does not choose. She hides them in her sleeves when she wants a stranger easy, and shows them when she wants a stranger to know what is in front of him.

Senses: the senses of a strong human, no better and no worse, except that she is always aware of how many of a thing are in a room, and the count is never wrong by more than one. (Settled as plain color: she counts. It tells her nothing about worth, intent or what is hidden. If the Keeper reads it as a power, I ask that it be priced as one and I will argue it.) Any sense I have not described is left unsettled and settled later.

She moves at the pace of her form: a long, even walk, a ready stride, no faster than a strong woman in good boots. She does not tire and does not need to rest. She sleeps by choice, usually lightly, sitting up.

### What breaks you
Fire held to her. Being cut apart, the body parted at the joints and the pieces scattered. Being bound in iron or bronze chain so that she cannot move her hands. Being shut in a closed place with no door she can reach. Being drowned and held under, since she does not need breath but the weight pins her. Being made to stand somewhere she has been named a debtor and cannot leave. Her hands matter most: a mortal who has bound or broken her hands has taken most of what she is.

Wounds are real and they ache. A cut bleeds, though she does not bleed out.

### When your body is broken
The pieces do not rot. They lie as they fell, and there is a count of days in which nothing of her is awake. Then the largest piece draws the rest to it and she stands up where that piece lay. Proposed below as a power, "The Count Returns," because it has a trigger and a cost.

### Things carried (as you gave them; all ordinary unless a power below says otherwise)
Boots, trousers and a long belted coat of undyed wool, all ordinary. A leather belt pouch holding a handful of plain tally sticks, notched, and a bit of chalk. A small balance of bronze with two shallow pans, folded into a wooden case, ordinary, no power in it. A flat stamp of fired clay on a cord at her neck, about the size of a thumb, cut with a simple mark of her own, which is ordinary until she lends it (see "The Lent Seal"). No weapon. I ask that all of these be ordinary things with no power attached, except the stamp as described under its power.
