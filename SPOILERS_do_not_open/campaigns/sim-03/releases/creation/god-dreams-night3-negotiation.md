# Negotiation: god-dreams, Night 3

Your people, priced. You proposed one edge with its weakness, and one ask to be priced on you. Everything else you wrote is ordinary life and costs nothing: the road and the ford, the ring, the code, the sticks and cords and the Book, the tending of sleepers, the Court of Terms, the Reckoners, the Keepers of the Lamp, the offices, the split stick that proves a carried word. The names you gave as a wish, the Lowlamp and the Ford, stand as your people's own unless the world gives them an older one; you will see. What you left to the world (their magic, what they believe about gods, what they know on the first morning, whose the wall and the trough are) is fitted tonight. Your right to refuse a helper you judge a thief is yours already, as it is any god's; if you use it, it is recorded with its date and reasons, and the office stays empty until you fill it.

## Round 1

### The Night Watch, paid for by the Day Sleepers
Draft text, the edge: Between dusk and dawn a camp or town of the Lowlamp cannot be taken by surprise by any mortal force: no factor for surprise is counted against it in those hours, and it stands to arms as quickly as another people does at noon. It is a habit of half the people and no more than that: it sees nothing a wakeful sentry would not see, and gives nothing against stealth by more than ordinary craft, which is unruled until it happens.
Draft text, the weakness: From mid-morning to mid-afternoon a camp or town of the Lowlamp is half abed. In those hours the factor for surprise counts double against it, and it counts even against a force seen coming at a bowshot. Outdoor work done in those hours goes at four fifths of the rate. Breaking camp takes the people one day longer than it would take another people of their size. It holds in every season. It ends only if the people give up the night watches, and then the edge ends with it.
Choices: none.
The Keeper's reasons: The two are the same habit seen from both ends, and they are near enough in weight that the one pays for the other as you offered. Each is given a number so that it can be applied. One thing is said so that you price it yourself: your own eyes are dazzled by day and your own sleep is owed by day, so your people's worst hours are also yours.

### What the Chief Keeper of the Lamp may leave you
Draft text: See Choices.
Choices:
(A) An ordinary sign. A copper coin laid on a flat stone, meaning "come", is a thing any mortal can do and any passer-by can see. It is no power. You know of it when your eyes or a messenger find it, and no sooner. It costs nothing and nothing goes on your card.
(B) A power of yours, added tonight. The Coin on the Stone. Status: added Night 3. Description: Once a season, the Chief Keeper of the Lamp, who bears your mark, may lay a coin once taken from your hand on a flat stone at the lamp fire and sleep beside it. If your body is within a day and a half's walk, you taste copper, wherever you are and awake or asleep, and know it for that office's call. It says "come" and nothing else: no word, no image, no place but the lamp fire. Drawback: You owe a day of your own sleep for it, as for a dream entered, within the same five days, and the debt adds to the others. The Keeper pays as mortal workers here pay: the night's sleep is spent with nothing got from it, and the Keeper is cold and slow through the day after. Limits: always the holder's choice; that holder's alone, priced again for another; it ends with the office, the life or the mark. Beyond a day and a half's walk nothing reaches you and the Keeper pays all the same. It makes no mark, enters no dream and carries no bargain. Other gods, and more than ordinary craft: unruled until it happens.
The Keeper's reasons: As you wrote it, a coin on a stone does nothing only a god can do, so there is nothing to price, and (A) is yours for nothing. If what you want is to know at a distance that the coin was laid, that is a mortal reaching you by a power of yours, and it is priced on you. It is held inside what your card already agreed: your mark by your own hand, your reach, and sleep owed for sleep. Your Night Tally is yours to send and not a mortal's to call, so the call is its own power with its own debt.
