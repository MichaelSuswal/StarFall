# Negotiation: god-war, Night 2

Your powers, priced. You proposed four powers and one weakness that buys.

## Round 1

### How the table prices two things for every god
These two are not offers. They are how this table prices the same thing for any god, and they are written into your card where they apply.
- A loss of memory or habit. Where a drawback takes "a memory or a habit" and you are not told which, the Keeper removes one item from your own record (your record of other gods or your goals) for each time the price falls due, chosen by lot among the items, and you are not told what went. You asked that it stay unspecified; it does.
- A return from a broken body. A full season, at the nearest ground of your own kind to where the largest part of you lies, is paid for by one item lost. A return in a month is paid for by two items lost each time. The table sells nothing faster than a month. If the nearest ground of your kind is more than a day off, the walk to it at your own pace is added to the time. Being held, bound, buried or shut in is not being broken: you stay where you are. Remains kept from the ground of your kind do not return until it reaches them or they are let out. What another god does to your remains is unruled until it happens.

### Your own strength (under Form)
Draft text: Your body has about the strength and wind of three strong men, and a cut on you closes in about a day. This is the stock that Loaned Strength lends from. Burns, broken bones and deep wounds mend at a mortal's pace.
The Keeper's reasons: You wrote that a cut closes in a day, and that you lend "a share" of your strength without saying how much there is. A body that mends faster than a mortal's is more than a form, so it is written down and counted in the price of Loaned Strength, whose drawback is paid out of it.

### Loaned Strength
Draft text:
Description: A mortal who takes your hand and says yes, freely and aloud, knowing what is lent, is lent a share of your strength: about the strength of two men, hurt that closes in a day, and a steadier nerve. It lasts until you end it, the borrower asks to end it, the borrower passes out of your sight, or a night and a day have passed, whichever is first. Up to three borrowers stand at once.
Drawback: For each borrower, a third of your own strength and quickness is gone while the loan stands, and your own cuts close no faster than a mortal's. With three borrowers you are as weak as a mortal man.
Limits: Only a willing mortal who understands. Not beasts. The borrower cannot lend it on, and it does not stack on one borrower. A borrower behind a wall is out of your sight and loses it. A borrower's death returns the share at once. It makes the borrower stronger and steadier, not wiser, and compels nothing: it rules no mind. Light and dark: you must be able to see the borrower. On another god, or on one armed or armored by more than ordinary craft: unruled until it happens. Your own people get no exemption. Use across a year: three at a time, as often as you choose to be weak.
Choices: none.
The Keeper's reasons: You asked for three expecting a trim. It is not trimmed: the price rises with every borrower and ends with you as a mortal man in plain sight, so the power prices itself.

### The Counted Ground
Draft text:
Description: With your heel or the butt of a staff you score a ring in the earth up to a hundred paces across and walk the whole of it to close it. Within the ring, while you stand inside it or on its line, no mortal can bring a weapon or a fist down in anger to wound or kill another mortal: the hand slows, the grip loosens, the weapon is set down. It lasts until sunrise, or until you say aloud that it is ended, or until the line is scuffed through anywhere.
Drawback: While the ring stands you cannot step across its line and cannot raise a hand in anger yourself. You are held as the mortals are, and you are not shielded from what comes from outside.
Limits: The ring is scored and walked in plain sight; it cannot be made in secret or at a run. It stays the hand and changes nothing in any mind: anger, hatred and intent stand as they were, and anyone may walk out of the ring or scuff the line. It does not stop words, shoving without intent to wound, accidents, beasts, or an arrow or spear loosed from outside. A blow already falling when the line closes lands. Earth only: it cannot be scored on bare rock, paving, a floor, ice or water. On a god, or on a weapon made or held by more than ordinary craft: unruled until it happens. Your own people get no exemption. Use across a year: one ring at a time.
Choices: none.
The Keeper's reasons: This is the one power you asked for that reaches into what a mortal can do, so it is tested hard. It passes because it stays a hand and not a will, because anyone can end it with a foot, and because you are held inside it with no shield. "Earth only" is added so the limit is exact.

### The Bell Nobody Rings
Draft text:
Description: When you strike your own breastbone three times with the open palm, a deep bell tone, the same each time, sounds at every shrine within a morning's walk (about eight miles) that is kept in your name. A shrine is kept in your name when a light is lit before it at least once each night by whoever keeps it. It carries no words. Everyone near a shrine hears it.
Drawback: For a night and the day after, you hear no voice, only the bell. Speech reaches you as weather at a distance, and you cannot be asked a question.
Limits: It cannot be aimed at one shrine, hushed once struck, or made to carry a word or a second tone. Friend and foe hear it alike. A shrine dark for a full season stops sounding. You need not see the shrines. Whether another god hears it as more than a bell: unruled until it happens. Use across a year: as often as you will go deaf to voices.
Choices: none.
The Keeper's reasons: A signal with no words, short in reach, heard by everyone, and paid for with a day of your own hearing. Accepted as you wrote it.

### The Set Place
Draft text:
Description: When your body is broken (cut apart, burned to ash, or crushed so that it cannot move), it falls still and pale, keeps its shape for one night, and is gone. After a full season you stand, at dusk, at the place nearest to where you fell where a seat has been laid for a guest and left empty.
Drawback: Each time you return, a memory or a habit does not come back with you, and you are not told which.
Limits: The return follows the table's rule above; if the nearest laid seat is more than a day off, the walk is added. If no seat is laid anywhere you could walk to in a season, you return where you fell. You do not return into a place a mortal has barred against you; you come to the next nearest. Being held, bound or shut in is not being broken. You return in what you wore, with nothing you carried.
Choices: the pace. (A) A full season, one item lost each time. (B) A month, two items lost each time.
The Keeper's reasons: Back at the next dusk is refused: a god that is home by tomorrow evening has not been broken in any way the world would notice, and the table sells no god a return faster than a month. The seat itself stands as you wrote it, without the thirty-mile bound, which the table's rule replaces. You left the clothes and gear unruled; it is ruled the plain way, clothes and no gear.

### The weakness you offer, and what it buys
Draft text:
The Refused Door. Any mortal to whom you have given a gift (bread, a place at your table, a loan of your strength) may later refuse you their door by saying aloud, awake, in your hearing or the hearing of two witnesses, "I refuse you my door." From then on you cannot cross the threshold of that mortal's own dwelling: a house, a hut or a hearth room with a door, refused by the one who dwells there, knowing whom they refuse and sound of mind. Not a tent, a temple, a public hall, a field, or a rented house. It lasts until that mortal dies or opens the door to you by their own words. You cannot argue, trick or break it, and no other mortal can lift it. How it can be used against you: anyone you have fed can shut you out, and can be seen doing it.
What it buys: the Bell reaches a day's walk (about twenty miles) in place of a morning's.
The Keeper's reasons: Your form does not pay this already, and the ones who can use it are exactly the ones you make by giving. It is accepted as offered and buys what you asked.

Accept, refuse or counter each item, and give your reasons. Accepting everything is a choice you have, not the expected one. You may counter with a drawback of your own in place of the Keeper's.
