# The common past

Under the deep lore's Truth 5. The eight who came down here are the remainder of one far country, thrown clear when its star fell. None of them was a person. Each is a pattern that country held hardest: something its people did, kept or leaned on for so long that it outlived them. What each remembers is that people's memory of it, not its own. Nothing here is ever released. Each god's past reaches it only through play.

## The far country

Veshkarn: a river country under a low, old star, far across the plane, further than any people here has walked. One great river, the Ondre, ran from the mountain Corrow in its north to a gated mouth at Ondremouth in its south. Between them lay the Marrowood, a forest that was also the country's burial ground, and on a rise above the river's middle reach the hall of Tal Sedder, where its quarrels were settled at one long table. At Cennac, a ford in the wood, the dead were walked across the water before they were laid under the trees. At Illun, a long low house of clay rolls, everything said under oath was kept.

Veshkarn was very late. Its sea had gone to salt flat, its dialects had flattened, and its people knew in the way late peoples know that something was running out. They did what such peoples do: they held harder to the few things that still held.

## The events near its end

In order, over about three generations. Named in lower case; none of these is a name anyone here uses.

1. The leaving-off of the rite at Cennac. The walking of the dead across the ford had been the country's oldest observance. As the villages thinned it was kept by fewer, then by one house, then by no one. The threshold stone stayed; the hearth beside it went cold.
2. The oath of the scale. The river towns and the hill clans, close to war over salt and seed, swore a peace at the long table of Tal Sedder: fair weight, open fords, no arming of the passes. Its terms were written on one roll and kept at Illun.
3. The breaking of the oath of the scale. Within a generation one party armed the passes. Which party is not known to anyone; the roll that said who swore what is gone.
4. The felling. For palisades, pyres and the timber of a last war, the Marrowood was cut on the flanks of Corrow and along the river. The tree-graves were opened to the sky.
5. The slide at Corrow. In the thaw after the felling, the south flank of Corrow came down on the hill clans' winter town. The mountain had been trusted to hold for longer than the country had writing.
6. The swallowing of the last roll. The keeper-pattern of Illun, which knew how every kept story ended, came to the roll that held the oath's terms and the list of the country's names that a walking, singing people had come to ask for. It did not hand it over. The roll was not burned or hidden; it was taken in. Nothing of Veshkarn was carried out by anyone.
7. The night of the two closings. An order of bell-wardens, who believed the country could be kept from ending if nothing more left it, sealed the hall at Tal Sedder with its table laid and shut the river gate at Ondremouth. They rang until morning. The herds were on the wrong side of the gate.
8. The fall. Not long after, in the scale of such things. What was densest was thrown clear.

## What was thrown here

- The player's god: the under-weave of the Marrowood, the habit of the wood and its dead of being joined, grove to grove and grave to grave. The quiet part of something large.
- god-beasts: the walker of the dead at Cennac, half of the wood and half of the house, honored and then left off.
- god-stone: the shadow of Corrow. The country held its mountain as two, the bright face that was looked at and the dark weight behind that held. What came here is the one behind. The bright face was not thrown; it was under the fall.
- god-trade: the struck hand: the oath of the scale itself, with the washing of the dead's gear that the river towns did before a fight.
- god-dreams: the herd-feast of the autumn crossing, the counting in and counting out, held as a child because the country's herd-god was always shown as one.
- god-war: the host of Tal Sedder: the long table, the laid seat, the peace of the hall, sealed in with its lights burning.
- god-secrets: the keeper of Illun, which held every ending but its own, and swallowed the last roll.
- god-rivers: the two-faced keeper of the gate at Ondremouth, shut in its own gatehouse on the night of the two closings.

A warm blue-grey stone of the same throw came down the same night (the Remainder; placed by roll on Night 3a). It dreams the kneeling crowd at Tal Sedder and the Ondre, a river no one here has seen.
