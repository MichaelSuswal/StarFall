# People: unnamed

I give them no name. A name given too early is a leash put on a thing that has not yet shown its gait. Let them name themselves, and I will learn it from their mouths. The same for their home: I call it the Crossing, as the land does, and nothing more.

## Who they are
Ford folk. People of the river country who live by the one good crossing: boatmen and pole men, reed cutters and willow weavers, hay makers of the flood meadows, barley growers on the high bank, fishers at the weirs, and the families who keep the toll and the stakes. They are tolerably hard and tolerably dry of humor, and slow to be cheated. They know the water as a neighbor with a temper. They already stand at the ford and have their own past there. I do not bring them. I come to them.

A wish, recorded as a wish and not as fact: that they have long kept the crossing without a crown, under a council of ferry houses and a reeve of the toll, and that they are used to tolls, oaths and the keeping of a gate. The world decides.

## How many
About five thousand, gathered close in one place: a town on the high bank above the flood line, on the road side of the ford, with its barley and hay fields at its back and its boat yards at the water. Larger would cost more grain than a river valley gives in a bad year. This is already the number the world sets for a people of this kind, and I ask for no more.

## How they live
By the water and by the calendar of the water. In spring, when the ice goes out and the meadows go under, the boats work and the ford is shut by the flood. In summer the meadows are cut for hay and the herds go to the hill pasture. In autumn the grain goes into the granary and the salt and meat are laid by. In winter the weirs are mended, the boats repaired, and the town sits on what it laid by. The thaw tells you who prepared.

They keep sheep and goats, a few cattle, geese and hens. They build dugout boats and light skin boats, and a flat broad ferry that is poled and roped. They smoke fish and meat. They weave baskets and fish traps from willow and rope from reed and bark. Their houses are timber and wattle on raised footings, with the floors above the flood. They work bronze in small amounts from traded metal. They have no steel.

They trade at the ford: salt, hides, grain, bronze, amber and cloth from the road, and the goods of the river folk who keep to the reeds, if such folk are there, and the hill people of the high rock, if such are there. I say nothing about them, and leave it to the world.

## Their code
Few words, and hard ones.
- The toll is owed by everyone who crosses, and a toll paid is a bargain kept. A toll dodged is a theft from every house.
- Whoever is given a gate keeps it, and shuts it at night. A gate left open is the worst of the small crimes.
- An oath at the water is kept at cost. A man who does not mean to keep an oath does not swear one, and no one thinks the less of him for staying silent.
- No one is bound in rope, chain or stocks among them. A crime is answered with a fine in salt or grain, with labor at the weirs, or with being put out past the stakes. Death is answered only for killing, and the verdict is said aloud before three witnesses at the water. I want no cage in my town. I do not give the reason, and I do not look at where the wish comes from.
- A guest is weighed at the gate before being fed, and fed after. A guest is not harmed once fed.

## Skills and culture
Boatcraft and river reading: the pole, the rope ferry, the landing in a current, the sound of a rising river and the look of ice that is about to go. Basketry, weirs, smoking, hay making. Small bronze work. The keeping of a ledger of tolls in tally marks on split sticks, each house keeping half.

They speak in sayings, and I am at home among them. "The thaw tells you who prepared." "A shut door tells you nothing about who shut it." "The well that serves everyone is the first poisoned." Their songs are work songs for the pole and the rope. They bury their dead in the high ground, and put a bowl of river water by the grave for a season.

Their pride is the crossing: they say no stranger has ever been lost at their ford who followed their stakes. I leave this as their craft and their pride and ask the world to say what it is worth beside other peoples'.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed. I would not be owed to by the world for a thing I did not need, and a debt unnamed is a debt someone has not yet told me the price of.

## Their magic
Left to the world. A wish, recorded as a wish: that it be uneven, a wise woman or a knack in a few houses and none in many, and that it cost the worker, as the world says. I do not name any mortal with a knack. I would learn where it runs before I trust it.

## What they believe about gods
Left to the world, with a wish recorded: that they hold the river to be a neighbor with a share in everything, that a bowl of water is set at the stakes at the spring thaw for whoever keeps the crossing, and that they have a saying that the keeper of a ford is owed first and is owed in kind. I do not wish them to hold that gods walk among them. I would like to arrive as a stranger and be weighed.

## Rule and worship
Within my leans, and I ask for no departure.

Rule: a god-king in the manner of a warlord. I hold the crown myself, with a court and officers. My word is law, and it is carried by the court and not by me. I rule by force through a war band and its captains. The band is a body of about two hundred, sworn at the water, tested at the gate before they are trusted. The band keeps the roads to the ford, the stakes, and the town's walls of timber and earth.

Worship: local shrines only, kept by whoever keeps them, with no order of priests. A wet stone set at each household door, wetted from the river each dawn. A bowl of river water set out beside it. A village shrine at each bend of the road, a stone and a bowl in a low shelter. Anyone may keep one. No one is paid to.

## Hierarchy
- Advises me: the Speaker, and the court as a whole when I sit.
- Rules under me: the Reeve of the Ford (tolls, the town, the houses), the Marshal (the war band and the roads), the Keeper of Salt and Grain (stores, the granary, the winter), and the Stake Warden (the stakes, the ford marks, the flood watch).
- Settles disputes: a bench of three at the stakes, the Reeve, one head of a house drawn by lot, and one captain. They settle quarrels between families, thefts and boundaries. A matter of death, or a bench that cannot agree, comes to me at the water. Anyone may appeal to me on any matter once, and I judge it in the Crown shape, in public, with the court around me.

I hold that no office may be kept by one hand. Each of the four is held by a keeper and a second who watches the keeper. They are not friends by design. A gate has two faces.

## Helpers
Offices, not named persons, and I leave the holders for the world to say. Each is a mortal with their own wants, and none is a puppet.
- The Speaker: one. Hears me, repeats my word before the court, and carries the court's word back. The Speaker is the only office I speak to alone.
- The Reeve of the Ford, with a second.
- The Marshal of the Band, with a second, and the captains of the band under them.
- The Keeper of Salt and Grain, with a second.
- The Stake Warden, with a second.
- The heads of the ferry houses, who sit as a council and carry the court's word into the households.

How orders travel: I speak to the Speaker. The Speaker repeats it in the hearing of two officers who are not of the same office, so that no one can bend it. The Reeve, Marshal, Keeper and Warden carry it into the town, the band, the stores and the water. The captains carry it to the band, and the heads of houses to the households.

My standing conduct while I am away: the tolls are fixed at what I have set and are never changed in my absence. No one is bound. The ford is never closed by anyone's word but mine. The gates are shut at night. No one is put to death without three witnesses at the water. The war band keeps the roads and does not cross the stakes to make war on anyone unless I have said it. When I have said nothing, they hold.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own past, at the crossing in the spring flood, and I do not bring them. I mean to arrive as the Ford shape, at dusk, wet from the river, in an ordinary cloak with an ash pole, and to take a place among the boats like any ferryman come late to the work. I will look at what they carry before I listen to what they say. I will learn the stakes, the toll, the names of the houses and the sound of the ford in the flood. I will wade, ask little and answer less.

When I have weighed them, over days and not hours, I will stand at the stakes at dusk with the council gathered, and take the Crown shape before them, plainly, so that they know what crossed the water. I do not ask what they will do. I record only that I hope they will hear a word and weigh it, as they weigh a guest.

What they know of me on the first morning, and what my helpers know, is the world's to say.

## Questions for you

1. After you listen to the river you are deaf on dry land for a day at the least. Who stands at your back on those days, and do they know why?

A pair of the band, sworn, stand at my back on the bank, facing outward. I draw them by lot from the old sworn of two different captains, so that each pair watches the other, and neither knows beforehand which night is theirs. They are told that the Warden listens to the river and cannot hear on the bank for a day after, and that a hand on the shoulder is how to speak to me, one tap for a matter that cannot wait. They are told it is a toll the river takes. They are not told that I am helpless in the water while I listen, though a clever one will see it. I will not listen on a night I do not trust the road, and I will not listen with no one at my back. The saying is that a man who leaves his door unwatched is not brave but only hasty. I know this saying fits me as closely as it fits anyone.

2. Six days out of sight and sound of running water and you can do nothing but go toward it. Who among your war band knows, if anyone?

No one. Not the Marshal, not the Speaker, not the band. I have not told them, and I do not mean to tell them yet. A thing known by many is a thing sold by one. What I do instead is a custom of my court that looks like courtesy and not like need: the Warden does not go where the water does not. It is said that the god keeps to the river, and the band, when I go far from it, is under standing orders to keep within sight of the stream and to send a patrol along the water if I am gone beyond two days. They carry the order and not the reason. I see the gap in this. A man who is held, and whom no one knows to carry to the water, is a man held for good. I will tell one, the Stake Warden, when I have weighed that one for a full turn of the seasons, and not before. A shut door tells you nothing about who shut it, and I do not mean to make it easier to learn.
