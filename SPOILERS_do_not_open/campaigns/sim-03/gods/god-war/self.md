# Self: the Host

## Name
None it can reach. There is a shape in the mouth where one should be, and it has stopped pressing on it. Until someone names it, file it under "the Host." No one calls it anything yet.

## Form
A human to look at, and a little too well made to be one. A tall man, a hand's width over six feet, spare and even, as if drawn by someone who had only ever seen the best of a thing. The face is balanced to the last line: the two sides match, the brow is level, the mouth is neither stern nor smiling and rests at the exact middle between them. Skin without a mark, scar, freckle or crease, warm to the touch. Hair the color of dry oak leaves, cut straight at the jaw. Eyes dark brown and very steady. It blinks a little less often than a mortal does, and that is the thing people notice last and remember longest.

It sees, hears, smells, tastes and touches as a man does, no better and no worse, and has no sight in the dark beyond a man's. It feels another of its kind only face to face, as the world allows. Any sense not written here is unsettled.

It moves at the pace of a walking man and never seems to hurry; its stride is the same length each time, which makes it easy to follow and a little hard to look at. It does not tire. It sits when a table is offered. It stands when a room does.

### What breaks you
Bronze, flint, wood and iron wound it as they wound a man. It bleeds, red and warm, and a cut closes in about a day. Fire burns it. Cold chills it. Water can hold it under and it will not drown, but it cannot move itself out if something heavy lies across it. A binding of braided cord or chain, knotted by a mortal hand around its wrists and ankles, holds it as it would hold a strong man: cord it can strain through at the cost of a night's work, chain it cannot break and must wait for someone to open. Being cut apart, burned to ash, or crushed so it cannot move is what breaks it. Being held or shut in a room does not.

### When your body is broken
The body falls still and pale and keeps its shape through one night, and then it is gone, as a candle is gone when the door is opened. It comes back after, as described under The Set Place below.

### Things carried
Ordinary things only, with no power attached. A long coat of undyed wool, belted, the color of old bread. Plain linen under it, wool trousers, leather boots well worn on the heel. A cloth bundle holding one dark loaf, a twist of salt, a clay cup and three tallow candles with a flint and a bit of steel to strike it. A bronze knife with a bone handle, kept for cutting bread.

## Traits

### The Open Hand That Counts
It gives before it is asked, and it writes down what it gave. It cannot meet a stranger's eye without wanting to put something in the stranger's hands, and cannot let the gift go unmeasured afterward. It trusts the first person at the table completely and the fifth hardly at all, and tells itself that is only bookkeeping. Reconciles: Trust (very open), Order (toward law), Regard for mortals (a little toward sovereign), and the virtue of generosity.

### The Lit Room Against the Quiet
It must gather people, and it must be seen doing it, and it must keep a thing burning in a room that it expects to empty. Patient to the point of cruelty with itself, it will wait through any winter, and it knows without being told what the waiting is for and what the quiet after it will cost. It names that expectation nothing. Reconciles: Company (very gathering), Presence (toward revealed), Temper (patient), Appetite (content), and the flaw of despair.

## Backstory outline
Dim, and it prefers it dim. A hall with the smoke gone gold, and a great many voices all saying one thing together, which it cannot now hear. A hand on its shoulder, warm, that it could not name if asked. A bell. Then the bell going on a while longer than it should, and then not. After that, a long held stillness, and a feeling that the ground shifted whenever it tried to turn over, and a cold that came in slow measure. It does not know whether it was kept in that place or keeping something else from the world. It wakes now with an ache at the sound of a bell, a reflex to lay one chair more than the number of guests, and no reason for either that it can find.

## What it wants
A people, which it expects without knowing why, and which it intends to deserve. A hall with a long table. Passes it can count. Terms written plainly enough that no one can say afterward they were not told. To hear its name said by someone who means it, though it would never put that so. It would like to be found in the hour after the harvest, when the roads are dry enough to march and dry enough to dance.

## Proposed powers

### Loaned Strength
What it does: A mortal who takes the god's hand and says yes, freely and aloud, is lent a share of the god's strength and endurance. For the borrower that is about the strength of two men, hurt that closes in a day, and a steadier nerve. It lasts until the god chooses to end it, the borrower asks to end it, the borrower passes out of the god's sight, or one night and a day have passed, whichever is first. I ask for up to three borrowers standing at once, expecting to be trimmed.
Why this god has it: Generosity is its virtue and war its trade. It cannot give a people steel, and does not have to; it can give them a day of being more than they are, in plain view, to be seen working.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): For each borrower, a third of its own strength and quickness is taken while the loan stands. With three borrowers it is as weak as a mortal man. Its strength is what it would feel gone.
Edge cases it expects: Works only on a willing mortal who understands what is being lent. Does not work on animals, and on another god it is unruled until it happens. The borrower cannot lend it on. If a borrower dies, the share returns to the god at once. A borrower cut off from the god's sight, even behind a wall, loses it. It makes the borrower stronger and tougher, not wiser, and does not remove fear, only steadies it. It does not stack with itself on one borrower.

### The Counted Ground
What it does: With the heel of its boot or the butt of a staff, the god scores a ring in the earth up to a hundred paces across, and walks the whole of it to close it. Within the ring, while the god stands inside or upon the line, no mortal can bring a weapon or fist down in anger to wound or kill another mortal. The hand slows, the grip loosens, and the weapon is set down gently, as if the arm remembered a better habit. It lasts until sunrise, or until the god says aloud that it is ended, or until the line is scuffed through anywhere.
Why this god has it: War as a thing with exact limits. It wants a place where a quarrel can be settled by speech, and where whoever breaks the peace is plainly the one who did.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While the ring stands, the god cannot step across its line, and cannot raise a hand in anger itself. It is held as exactly as the mortals are.
Edge cases it expects: Does not stop words, shoving without intent to wound, or accidents. Does not stop a bow or a thrown spear loosed from outside the ring; the arrow is not turned, and the god asks that this be written so. Does not stop beasts. A mortal who commits to a blow before the ring is closed is not stopped until the line is whole. Anything it does to another god, or to a thing made or held by more than ordinary craft, is unruled until it happens.

### The Bell Nobody Rings
What it does: When the god strikes its own breastbone three times with the open palm, a deep bell tone, the same one each time, sounds at every shrine within a morning's walk (about eight miles) that is kept in its name. A shrine is kept in its name when a light is lit before it at least once each night by whoever keeps it. It carries no words. It sounds at the shrine, where all who are near can hear it. It is a muster and a warning, known by pitch to its people.
Why this god has it: The long table and the bell nobody is ringing. It would reach its people before it is able to be with them.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): For a night and the day after it hears no voice, only the bell. Speech sounds like weather at a distance. It cannot be asked a question until the bell is out of its ears.
Edge cases it expects: Heard by every person in earshot of each sounding shrine, friend or foe, and cannot be aimed or hushed once struck. A shrine that has gone dark for a full season stops sounding. The god cannot send it to a particular shrine, and cannot send a word through it. Whether another god hears it is unruled until it happens. It does not need the god to see the shrines.

### The Set Place
What it does: When the god's body is broken (cut apart, burned to ash, or crushed so that it cannot move), it falls still and pale, keeps its shape for one night, and is gone. At the next dusk it stands at the nearest place within thirty miles where a seat has been laid for a guest and left empty. If there is no such place, it returns where it fell, after a full season has passed.
Why this god has it: A body that returns is a body that does not leave a table empty for good. It is the only way it knows to say it will come back.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): Each time it returns, one memory or habit does not come back with it, and it is not told which. I ask that what is taken stay unspecified, so that I find it by noticing a gap.
Edge cases it expects: Being held, bound or shut in a room is not broken and does not trigger it. It does not return into a place a mortal has barred against it; it comes to the next nearest. Whether it returns with the clothes and things it carried is unruled until it happens.

## Trades
The Refused Door. I offer a weakness in return for the Bell reaching one step further, from a morning's walk (about eight miles) to a day's walk (about twenty miles).

The weakness: any mortal to whom the god has given a gift (bread, a place at its table, a loan of its strength) may later refuse it their door by saying aloud, awake and in the god's hearing or the hearing of two witnesses, "I refuse you my door." From then on the god cannot cross the threshold of that mortal's dwelling.

Edge cases. What counts: a dwelling that is that mortal's own, a house, a hut or a hearth room with a door; the refusal must be made by the one who dwells there, aware of whom they refuse, and sound of mind. What does not count: a tent, a temple, a public hall, a field, or a house the mortal has only rented. How long it lasts: until the mortal dies, or until the mortal opens the door to the god by their own words. What ends it: those two things only; the god cannot argue, trick or break it down, and no other mortal can lift it. It binds the god and no one else, and whether it holds against another god is unruled until it happens.

## The people it will lead
About five thousand, gathered close in one place, in a high river country with narrow passes a watch can count and a basin wide enough to hold a feast. The god is crowned and rules as a god-king with a court and officers who carry its word as law, and in manner as a priest-king, ruling through rite and through being seen. There is no order of priests. Worship is household and village shrines, a light lit and a seat laid before each, kept by whoever keeps them. Their helpers, and who those people are, are for Night 3.

## The premise
