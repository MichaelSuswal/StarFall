# People: unnamed

I leave their name, and the name of their home, to them. A people that is handed a name before it has used one for a winter will wear it like a borrowed coat. If they name themselves, and I hope they will, I would like to hear it from a mouth that means it. Until then, call them the people of the smoke.

## Who they are
Folk of the basin floor and the terraces above it, the ones whose smoke gathers in one part of the valley. I hope they are a settled people and an old one in this place, with a harvest behind them and a winter ahead, and with the habit of looking up at the passes before they look at their own doors. I did not make them. I only arrive among them, as one arrives at a table that was set before one knew there would be a guest.

## How many
About five thousand, gathered close in the one town on the floor of the basin by the river, and in the farmsteads and terraced hamlets within a day's walk of it. I ask for no larger. A larger people eats more, burns more and moves slower, and I would rather count my people by their faces than by their numbers, which I will be unable to do in any case.

## How they live
Barley and oats on the lower terraces, with beans, turnips and flax. Sheep and goats on the high pasture through the summer, brought down at first snow, which is now. Fish from the river, wood from the slopes and stone above the wood. Bronze, and a little wrought iron worked by hand in small quantities, no steel. Houses of stone and timber close together in the town, so that a winter fire in one is a warmth in six. Autumn is the season of accounts: stores counted, flocks counted, debts counted, and I would like to be found in that hour, when the roads are dry enough to march and dry enough to dance.

## Their code
Plain, and written in the only way I have any hope of it being kept: spoken aloud before witnesses, and then cut as notches on a tally stick, one half kept by each party. A debt turns honestly and on time. A guest is fed and sheltered, and not struck while a guest, and not asked their business before they have eaten. An oath on salt is kept past the day it stops being useful. A blade that has been yielded is not used again that day. Terms of a quarrel are said in full, so that no one can say afterward they were not told. These are what I hope for. How hard they are kept is for the people to show me.

## Skills and culture
Counting. They count what comes through the passes, what goes into the granary and what leaves it, and who is owed what, and they are proud of the counting more than of any one thing counted. I leave it as their craft and their pride, and ask for no worth set beside other peoples'. Tally sticks and knotted cords, bread baked in stamped rounds, long tables set for feasts at the end of harvest, songs that name the dead in order, a bell or struck iron in the high pass to say who is coming up it. Ordinary culture, and costing nothing.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed. I would rather bring them nothing that is a sure edge over their neighbors than bring them something that has to be paid for out of a weakness I have not yet seen them have.

## Their magic
Left to the world. I keep a wish on record, and only a wish: that it be small, in the manner of borrowing, a warmth in the hands, a taste of iron, a coat taken from a peg that is not one's own, and that it cost what borrowing costs, a thing returned with interest, a memory or a year or a sleep, written plainly so that none can say they were not told. That it be found in perhaps every third valley, and that the other two quarrel about whether the third is lying. I name no mortal with a knack.

## What they believe about gods
Left to the world, with a wish kept on record. I wish that they have a custom already, from before me and not because of me, of laying one seat too many at the harvest table and leaving it empty, and that they cannot say why. I wish that the old stories be partly true and that no one be sure which part. I do not say what was here before them or how long they have been here. I would like to be told.

## Rule and worship
Within the leans I was given. I hold the crown myself as a god-king, with a court and officers, and my word is law, carried by them. In manner I rule as a priest-king, through the rite of the table and through being seen at it. There is no order of priests, and the court holds no priesthood. The worship is household and village shrines, kept by whoever keeps them: a light lit before a shelf, a seat laid and left empty, bread and salt set out. The one thing I ask is the light, once every night, since the Bell sounds only at shrines where it is kept. Any house may keep a shrine or let it go dark. I will not send anyone to count them.

## Hierarchy
The offices, not the persons. A Council of the Table: the heads of houses, who sit at the long table with me and advise, and whose advice binds no one. A Steward of the Table, who rules under me. A Keeper of the Terms, who settles quarrels between families, thefts and boundaries, by hearing the terms said aloud before witnesses and cutting the settlement into the tally stick; appeals go to the Steward, and from the Steward to me, when I am in the hall to be asked. A Captain for each pass, who holds the watch there. Reeves for the quarters of the town and the hamlets. Wardens of the granary.

## Helpers
The offices that run things when I am away, with what each one does.
- The Steward of the Table. One. Hears me at dusk in the hall, keeps the ledger of what I gave, to whom, and on what terms, and carries my last decisions on to the others. Holds the loaf stamp, an ordinary stamped board pressed into bread and into clay, which marks an order as mine, since no one will keep my face to know me by. A mortal with a house and wants of their own, and I would want them to have both.
- The Captains of the Passes. One for each pass, as many as the land allows. Hold the watch, count who comes up the road, and strike the iron.
- The Keeper of the Terms. One. Settles quarrels as above. Acts for me only within my standing code and my last decisions.
- The Reeves and the Wardens of the granary. Quarter by quarter and store by store. Count, and report what they count to the Steward.
- How orders travel. I speak them to the Steward. The Steward cuts them on a stick or cord, stamps them with the loaf stamp, and sends them by runner to the Captains, Keeper and Reeves, who carry them on. Where there is need of a muster, I have another way, and I would not say it here.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own harvest, and I come to them as a guest comes. I mean to walk down the track from the pass in the hour after the harvest is in, carrying bread and salt and a clay cup, and to knock at the nearest door and ask for a seat. I do not announce what I am. They will know me, if they come to know me, by the long table, by what I give, and by what I write down. First they will know a tall guest, and some mornings that is all they will know. What the household that takes me in knows on the first morning is that a stranger came in out of the cold and left them better fed than they had been. I hope the first helper is the one who laid the seat, and that I do not have to pick them.

## Questions for you
1. I still feed everyone who comes. A table that asks what a guest might do with a refusal is no longer a table, it is a gate. As for the court, yes, the Steward knows what a refusal is, and has it in plain words, because I want every limit written exactly and I would rather they hear it from me than learn it from a closed door. A refusal is a thing a person may do, and I am to be told of it, and kept from that threshold. The household that refuses is not to be pressed, shamed or punished by any officer of mine, and its name goes in the ledger with its reasons, if it will give them. I would sooner be shut out by a house that had cause than let in by a house that had none.

2. By the light, the seat and the loaf. A shrine in my name has a flame lit before it once each night, a seat laid and left empty, and bread and salt set out, and those are how it is known and not by a likeness, for no one who has seen me will be able to describe what they saw. They will call it the guest's, or the empty seat, or the tall guest's, until someone gives it a name, and I ask only that the name they give be one they mean. I do not ask for a likeness to be made. If someone makes one anyway, I would want to see it, and I will say what I think of it.
