# People: the Column (what they call themselves; their home is unnamed)

We give them the name they would give themselves, and no name to the home. The Column is a people who go. A home they do not name is a home they do not hold.

## Who they are
Pass people. Herders, carriers and stone workers of the high broken country, who have lived by moving between the high summer grass and the sheltered winter ground for longer than any of them can count. They are lean, weathered, and quiet on a slope. They carry loads on their backs and on pack animals, they wear grey and brown wool and felt, and they keep their hair bound against the wind. They are not a gentle people and not a cruel one. They are a people who have learned that the mountain does not argue. Every one of them has seen a slope go, and every one of them walks as if it will.

We hold, as a belief of theirs and a wish of ours, not as fact, that the wide trodden way over the saddle, and the cairns beside it, and the old fire rings, are theirs. The world decides, and we will see.

## How many
About five thousand, in about a thousand households. We know a larger people costs what the world makes it cost: more food and fuel, slower growth, harder to move. A moving people of five thousand is heavy on a pass, and we know it. We take that cost, and we ask for no help against it except the edge below.

## How they live
They go round a circuit. In the thaw the whole Column climbs out of the winter ground by the passes as they open, with every household, every beast and the summer stores, and goes up to the high grass. They graze and quarry and mend in the long light. When the first hard cold comes they go back down the passes, before the weather closes them, and winter in a ring of rock holds on the lee side of the range: terraced rock shelters, cave mouths walled in dry stone, and cellars cut into the living rock. They stop in winter and not otherwise. They have no single seat. They have a ring of holds, and they come to each in its turn over the years, so that no hold is worn out and no valley is eaten bare.

A household is a family with its beasts: goats and sheep, and whatever sturdy pack animal the world gives mountain people. A household lives in felt tents in the open season and in a walled bay of the hold in winter. Work divides by season. In the high months: grazing, cheese, felt, rope, quarrying stone for the holds, mending the cairn ways. In the cold months: cellar work, weaving, tool making, and the telling of the Code.

The Column marches in a set order: a front guard, a body in three ranks with the pack beasts in the middle, a rear guard, and flank bands on the upslope and downslope sides. It never makes camp under a steep slope, and it never makes camp in the bottom of a gully in the thaw. Those are older than any of us.

## Their code
The Code is spoken and not written. It is recited at every evening halt and is exact: every limit stated, every debt stated, every penalty stated, so that no one can say it was not told to them. Some of it, in the shape we wish it:
- The Column goes at the pace of the slowest beast that is worth keeping, and no faster.
- A household carries its own sick and its own old for one day. After that the Column's litter bearers carry them, for as long as the way allows. If the way does not allow, the fallen are laid under a cairn with their name cut in a stone, and the Column goes on. This is not called cruel. It is called what the mountain asks.
- A debt is paid exactly, in the thing owed, or in labor of the same weight. A theft is paid back with the thing and the weight of the thing again. A killing of one of the Column by another is paid by the killer's household in beasts and in carrying, set by the captain of the band, and the killer walks at the rear of the Column for a year and a day and speaks to no one.
- The word of the god is law, and the word of the god comes down through the captains. No one disputes it at the halt. A person who thinks the word wrong says so to the head of their house, who says so to the captain, who says so to the Marshal, at the next halt and no earlier.
- The Column goes round standing water. This is old law. It is taught to the children as a rule for fever, for foot rot and for mired beasts, and those reasons are real.
- No one lies about the count. At each halt the households are counted, and the count is said aloud.

## Skills and culture
Their craft is stone. They cut cellars and shelters into living rock with wedges, fire and water, and they lay dry stone walls without mortar that last longer than the people who laid them. This is the pride of the Column, and we leave it as their craft and their pride, with its worth beside other peoples' left to the world. They make hemp and goat hair rope, felt, hard cheese, smoked and dried meat, flint tools and stone hammers, and a little bronze work by trade. They have a stacked stone sign language for cairns, which marks a way, a danger, a water place, a halt, a name and a count; every child learns it, and a cairn left on a pass is read by anyone of the Column who comes by. They signal by horn. They bury their dead under cairns, with a name cut in a stone. Their songs are marching songs, counted to the pace.

War is a duty of every household and a craft of the bands. The war bands are militia of herders led by captains: slings, spears, clubs, stone hammers, hide shields, and whatever bronze they have. There are about seven hundred who bear arms in a season, in several bands. They are good in broken ground and poor on a flat field.

## Edges and their weaknesses
**One edge proposed: Pass Craft.** In high broken country, in the thaw and in the freeze, the Column moves its whole host, with its beasts and its stores, over pass, scree and rotten snow at a pace and with a loss that no other people of this level can match. They do it by the cairn signs, by the rope and the roped line, by the Pass Wardens' reading of the snow and the ice and the stones, and by a march order that every child has walked since birth. It is an edge in movement over this kind of country, and only there: it does not make them stronger in a fight, nor better on low ground, nor on a plain, nor in a forest.

The ask, which we know is large: that the Column crosses a pass in the thaw twice as fast as any other host of its size and kind, and loses a tenth of the beasts and a tenth of the people that such a host would lose. We will take it trimmed, and we will take it said plainly.

**The weakness offered: Few Holds.** All of the Column's winter grain and its winter shelter are in a small number of known rock holds, about three or four around the circuit, which are fixed, known to anyone who has followed the Column once, and cannot be moved. Grain carried on the march spoils, or is eaten, or is cut off by a closed pass, so it cannot be kept anywhere else. The loss, siege, flooding or burning of one hold takes about a quarter to a third of the winter's grain and a quarter to a third of the winter's shelter with it, and a hold lost in autumn may be a winter lost. A people that moves but cannot hide its own cellars is a people that can be starved by anyone who finds and holds them. The Column knows this and guards its holds in rotation with a band at each, which costs the Column a fifth of its fighters in the cold months.

## Their magic
Left to the world. We record a wish: that it is rare and costly, as we said in the survey, and that it takes something from the body that the worker would notice, warmth, years or a name. Not a wise woman in every valley. A mountain hag, a stone reader, a few who can be sought out and may refuse. We hope for one such among them, and we do not ask for one. We do not name her, and we do not want her in the court. If the Keeper fits one to the world, we want to be told only that she exists, and we want to find her.

## What they believe about gods
Left to the world, with a wish recorded: that they have not held a god over them before, that they have no tale of one who ruled them and no rival in their memory, and that they hold stone, and the dead laid in stone, as something that remembers. We want them to meet a god in the first morning as a surprise, and to decide what to make of it, and we want them to have no one in their stories who would say we are wrong.

## Rule and worship
Within our leans. We hold the crown ourselves: a god-king in a warlord's manner. We rule by the word, through the Marshal, the court and the captains, and the war bands are how the word is enforced. There is no order of priests, and we ask for none. The worship is local shrines: a household stone at every hearth, kept by whoever keeps the hearth; a cairn at every halt and every pass, kept by whoever builds it; a stone at the well or the spring of each winter hold, kept by whoever keeps the spring. Each shrine is the household's own. The Column does not pray to us as such. It leaves a stone for the god, as it leaves one for the dead, and we are one more that the stones remember. We ask for nothing grander and we would not want it.

## Hierarchy
Under us, the Marshal of the Column commands the war bands, the march order and the halts, and carries our word to the captains. The Reckoner keeps the count of people, beasts and grain, and says it aloud at every halt; the Reckoner also keeps the Code and recites it. The Warden of the Holds keeps the cellars, the grain and the winter shelter, and the households who stay at the holds in the open season. The captains lead the bands: front guard, rear guard, the flank bands and the hold bands. The Pass Wardens read the way ahead and lay the cairn signs. The heads of the households speak for their houses at the evening halt.

Disputes are settled at the evening halt under the Code. A quarrel between two households is heard by the captain of the band they march with, with the two heads of house present, and judged under the Code. A theft or a boundary of grazing is heard by the captain, and appealed to the Marshal. A matter the Code does not cover is carried by the Marshal to us. We do not judge ordinary quarrels, and we would not be asked to.

## Helpers
Offices, not named people. The court that runs things when we are absent is the Marshal, the Reckoner and the Warden of the Holds, with the captains under the Marshal and the Pass Wardens under the Reckoner. Whoever holds an office is a mortal with their own wants, and none is a puppet. In our absence they act inside our standing conduct and our last decisions: keep the circuit, keep the count, keep the Code, go round standing water, camp under no slope, and ask us at the next sight of us for what the Code does not cover.

Orders travel in a line. We speak face to face to the Marshal and the Reckoner, and to no one else if we can help it. The Marshal tells the captains at the evening halt. The captains tell the heads of the households. The heads of the households tell the house. On the march the word goes by horn and by cairn sign: a short list of orders the whole Column knows. A runner carries anything else, a child or a young man of a band, always to a named office.

## How the god meets them
We wish to find them already there. The Column is at a saddle camp on the trodden way, waiting for the pass to open, with the thaw going loud in every gully. We come down out of the scree above the camp, a child in a grey coat with a coil of rope, a hammer and a pouch of stones, in the thaw. We go to the cairn at the head of the camp and stand there, still, and let the first of them find us. We do not say what we are. We say we are come to lead them, and we say it to whoever comes first, and we tell them to fetch the Marshal and the Reckoner.

We record, as our wish and not as fact, that what they know of us on the first morning is that a child came down out of the scree where the stones had moved in the night, and that it speaks in a voice that does not match its size. What the court knows is left to the world. We want to be believed for what we order, not for what we look like.

Our conduct, as a standing record. We stay in the middle of the column and behind the captains, and we keep our face for the Marshal and the Reckoner. We go ahead of the column alone at dawn, hands down on the rock, to hear the way before the Column walks it. We keep the hammer in the coat. We do not stand in the front where we can be looked at, and we do not give an order in person that a captain can carry. If a person of the Column falls on the way we do not stop, and the Code says why.

## Questions for you

1. Where is the grain kept by a people that does not stop?
We do not keep a single seat, but we give them a ring of winter holds, three or four, and the grain is in cellars cut into living rock at each hold. The Warden of the Holds keeps them, with a few households who stay behind in the open season to keep the cellars dry and the mice out, and a band of the war host sits at each hold in rotation through the winter. During the open season each household carries a tenth of its own year in sealed skin bags, and the Column carries a march store on the pack beasts, enough for the crossing of the passes and no more. We said we wanted a year of grain in a cellar. It is the cellar that we want to keep, and keeping it is why we have captains. The weakness we offered, Few Holds, is this answer seen from the other side. We hope it is a fair trade.

2. Who, if anyone, among your captains knows why the column goes round?
No one. The Column goes round standing water by old law, and the reasons the children are given are the ones that stand up: fever, foot rot, beasts mired to the hock. The Marshal and the Reckoner know it as our word and no more. The Pass Wardens lay the route by it and do not ask. We do not tell any of them the other reason, because a person who knows it can hold a ditch against us. We know that a keen captain who watches us pull up short at a wet place may guess, and we know that we cannot prevent that. We can only be careful where we stand beside water, and keep our face hidden. If one of them guesses, we will deal with it then, and we will not decide now what that will be.
