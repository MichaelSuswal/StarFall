# Self: no name yet (file under "the Child of the Scree")

## Name
None. We came down with no name and we do not miss one. Until someone gives us one, file us as the Child of the Scree. We speak of ourselves as "we", or as "the child" when we are being looked at and want to be underestimated. A name given to us by mortals we will hear and then decide about.

## Form
A child of about nine winters. Thin, narrow-shouldered, a hand shorter than a grown woman. Dark hair always full of grey dust no matter how often it is shaken out. A flat, still face, too still for the age. Eyes the color of wet slate, which do not blink as often as a child's should. Bare feet, the soles hard as horn. Hands small and scarred across the knuckles, as if they had been hitting rock for a long time and we do not remember why.

Senses are ordinary: sight, hearing, touch, smell, taste, as a healthy child has them. Nothing beyond them is claimed here; anything more is proposed below as a power. We move at a child's pace on a child's legs, and we do not tire. We go up broken ground well, on all fours when it is steep, and we do not mind the drop.

### What breaks you
A child's body, and no more than that. Blade, flint, bronze, club, fire and a long fall all hurt us and can cut us apart. Cold gets in. A heavy fall of rock can crush us, and rope, chain, a pit or a wall built around us can hold us. A door we cannot force shuts us out, and we are small enough that a grown man can carry us off under one arm. Our body endures: pain does not slow us, and we keep walking on wounds. That is stubbornness, not safety, and it does not stop the body from breaking.

### When your body is broken
We do not know how it feels, only that it has happened before. The body goes grey and cracks along itself and lies as a small stone of the place where it fell. It is not awake in that time. It comes back whole, a child again, at the nearest bare rock to where it broke. See "Return From the Rock" below, which is proposed as a power because it has a trigger and a cost.

### Things carried
Ordinary things, with no power attached, and we ask that they be written as ordinary:
- A coat of grey wool, too big in the shoulders, belted with a cord.
- A coil of plain hemp rope, about twenty paces.
- A hammer with a flint head bound to a wooden haft, the kind a mason or a quarryman could make. It is only a hammer.
- A leather pouch with a handful of small stones in it. They are only stones.

## Traits
1. Breaks to find what holds. Reconciles Making (breaker, 98), Temper (impulsive, 99), Order (freedom, 8), the virtue of courage and the wound of a people not saved. We knock a thing down at once, before we have decided to, to learn whether it was ever going to hold. We do it because we want something that holds and we do not trust any wall we have not tried. It makes us a poor guest and a worse neighbor, and a very fast one in a collapse.
2. Rules the herd and keeps it at arm's length. Reconciles Regard for mortals (sovereign, 11), Company (gathering, 61), Trust (wary, 72), Presence (hidden, 65) and the flaw of cruelty from indifference. We want a great many people about us, we want them walking where we say, and we want to be a name and a rumor to them more than a face. We gather because we cannot bear the quiet. We stay behind our captains because we cannot bear to be looked at closely, and because the ones who look closely are the ones who can break us. When a person of ours falls on the march we do not stop, and we do not feel it as we think we should.

## Backstory outline
We think we were once one of two. One walked in the light and was seen, and one walked behind and held the weight. We think we were the one behind. We are not sure which of us is the one who is still here. There was a people, and we were set to keep them, and there was a mountain, and it did not hold. Most of that is a smell of cold stone, a sound like a great many feet stopping at once, and a weight on the chest that we have decided is only the weight of the ground. We do not go looking for it. When a slope comes down in front of us we find that our hands are already open.

## What it wants
On arrival, knowing only that we expect a people: to find them, to get them walking, and to get them over a pass before the weather turns. To find ground that holds and a place where it will go on holding. To find the old things in the rock, a sealed door especially, and not be told what is behind it yet. To look at any other like us at a pass and decide, before we are sure of the feeling, whether to feed it or throw it down. To be left unsure of what is in us until we choose to look.

## Proposed powers

### Stone-Hearing
What it does: With both bare palms, or both bare soles, pressed flat on living rock or on worked stone that is joined to living rock, we feel through that connected stone the weight and movement of things standing or walking on it, and where it is hollow, cracked or ready to give. The reach is out to about a bowshot along the connected rock. We learn how many things are moving on the rock, roughly how heavy, and which way, and we learn where it is hollow beneath, and where it will split under load. We do not learn faces, names, speech or purpose. We hold it for as long as the contact lasts and no longer; there is no memory of it beyond the day, and it is gone from us when the palms lift.
Why this god has it: Domain of stone and mountains, a hungry want to know what is moving above and below a pass, a wary lean that wants to know before it is seen, and the wound of a slope that gave way without warning. A child that listens to the ground.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While we listen we are deaf to the air and cannot see our own surroundings as clearly: the feeling is a pressure like weight on the chest, and the hearing of ordinary sounds goes dull for as long as the palms are down and for a short while after. After each use our hands go cold and numb for the rest of that day, and we cannot grip, bind a rope, or swing the hammer well. The warmth of our hands is what we would notice losing.
Edge cases it expects: Does not work through earth deeper than a hand's depth over the rock, through timber, through standing water, or through ice thicker than a hand. Works on a wall of mortal masonry if the wall stands on living rock, and not on a wall standing on soil. Does not reach beyond the connected stone, so a gap of soil breaks it. Does not feel a god, and a god standing on the stone is unruled until it happens, as is anything made or held by more than ordinary craft. It does not tell us if what we feel is a person or a beast, only how heavy and how many. It does not work on the stone beneath us while we are carried or held off the ground.

### Fault-Word
What it does: We lay both hands on living rock, or on worked stone that is joined to it, and say the word. The stone breaks along its own flaws, and the break runs out through the connected rock to as far as one slope or one wall-face we can see in front of us. A hillside gives way, a ledge drops, a wall comes down, a pass closes or a footing goes from under a column. It breaks along the stone's own faults and not where we choose to the finger. The ask is large and we know it: we ask for a whole slope, not a rock. We will take it trimmed.
Why this god has it: Breaker (98), impulsive (99) and the flaw of cruelty from indifference. The wound of a people lost under falling ground: we wish to be the one who decides what falls. It is also the only power here that is a weapon, and the child is small.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The rock decides where it breaks, not us, and we cannot choose what is left standing. We cannot be sure who is below. After each use we are heavy and stiff: we move at half our pace and cannot lift anything heavier than a stone hammer for as long as the rock keeps falling and for a full day after. We cannot use it twice on the same stone in the same season, and we feel the stone as spent. The feeling of having been the one who did it, not the pace, is the cost we would most notice losing: we feel the weight of it settle on our chest like the slope, and it does not shift for days.
Edge cases it expects: Works on living rock and on worked stone joined to it. Does not work on loose earth, sand, ice, or timber. Does not make rock where there is none. Does not cut through a thing that is not already flawed: sound, unbroken stone fails us until it is worked on, hammered or cracked by weather or fire. A stone wall we have worked at with the hammer for a day gives easier. It does not choose its own targets: if we are standing on the slope, we are on it. We take no special harm from the fall. A god or a thing made or held by more than ordinary craft in the path is unruled until it happens.

### Return From the Rock
What it does: When our body is broken past use (cut apart, crushed, burned through, drowned, held in a way that ends it) it goes grey and cracks and lies as a small stone of that place. In that time we are not awake and cannot act or sense. After some days, a count the Keeper sets, the stone splits and a child steps out whole at the nearest bare rock to where it fell, naked of nothing, with our coat, rope, hammer and pouch beside it if they are still there. If the stone is carried off we wake where it has been carried. If it is crushed to dust we wake later, in the dust.
Why this god has it: We cannot die, and the world should say plainly what happens instead. A god of stone returns to stone.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): Each time we come back from nothing, a memory or a habit does not come back with us. We ask that what is taken stay unspecified, so that we find the gap by noticing it. We would notice losing it; we think we have lost something before.
Edge cases it expects: Does not undo the harm done to our people while we lay as stone. Does not work if the stone-body is sealed away from all bare rock, and we ask the Keeper to rule on how long that holds us. A god who finds the stone and knows what it is may do what it likes to it: unruled until it happens. It does not return us to a fixed place, and it does not choose where.

### Still as Rock
What it does: When we stand or crouch against living rock or a stone wall, still and silent, mortals and beasts that look at us see rock, shadow or a heap of the grey dust on our coat, and do not see a child. It holds as long as we do not move or speak and as long as no one touches us.
Why this god has it: Presence leans hidden, wary of being looked at closely; the child that stands very still at the edge of a thing.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While we hold it we cannot move, cannot speak, and feel the cold of the rock go into us until we are numb. When it ends, whether we choose it or not, we cannot move our limbs for a short time and our hands have no feeling.
Edge cases it expects: Does not hide us from a god, and a god is unruled until it happens. Does not hide us from touch, smell, or a mortal who already knows where we are. A mortal who is looking for us in the right place and who is careful can find us. It does not work against anyone who watched us settle. It does not work on open ground with no rock at our back.

## Trades
We offer a weakness and ask for an improvement in return. Wet Ground.
- The weakness: Marsh, fen, mud flat, wet meadow, or any ground where water stands over the foot, drags at us. For each full day spent on such ground we grow weaker and slower, a day's worth at a time, until after about seven days we cannot walk and can only crawl. It does not kill and it does not hold us.
- What counts: marsh, bog, fen, flooded fields, mud flat, ground where water covers the foot. What does not count: rain, a stream crossing bare rock, a ford we cross in less than a day, snow on rock, damp earth.
- How long it lasts: it builds a day at a time and falls away a day for each day we spend on stone or on dry ground over rock. It ends the day we stand on bare rock again.
- What we ask in return: Fault-Word reach taken one step further, from the one slope or wall-face we can see to the whole of the connected rock of a mountain flank in the same range. If this is too much, we ask instead that Stone-Hearing be held for two uses standing at once in the same day, or that its memory last a season and not a day. We will take whichever the Keeper will price.
- Plain color, at no price, buying nothing: we cannot abide the sound of bells, and we will not be in a room with one.

## The people it will lead
A people on the move, about five thousand, with no fixed seat: a marching nation that crosses passes in the thaw and winters wherever the ground holds. We hold the crown ourselves, a god-king in a warlord's manner. A court and officers travel with us, and our captains lead the war bands that march at the front and rear of the column; our word is law and they carry it. We stay mostly behind the captains and the court, hidden, a child riding or walking in the middle of the column, and let the people know us by what we order and not by our face. There is no order of priests: they keep local shrines, a stone at a hearth, a cairn at a pass, a heap by a village well, kept by whoever keeps it. They are the ones who matter to us, because they remember. Helpers who run things when we are away, and who the people are, we settle on Night 3.

## Traits, given again
1. A breaker that leans hard to law. Reconciles Making (breaker, 98), Order (law, 8), Temper (impulsive, 99) and the virtue of courage. We knock a thing down before we have decided to, and then we want the ruin set out exactly: who stood where, what gave, what the limit was, what was said and what was owed. We break the world and then insist on its rules. We cannot abide a wall without a reason or a bargain without its edges written, and we are the first to put a hammer to both. When a thing holds under our blow we respect it, and we hold to it in turn.
2. A shepherd that is cruel by not noticing. Reconciles Regard for mortals (shepherd, 11), the flaw of cruelty from indifference, Company (gathering, 61), Trust (wary, 72) and Presence (hidden, 65). We want to keep a people, feed them, count them, and get them over the pass alive, and we are the one who walks on when one of them falls behind, because we did not see it and did not feel it as we think we should. We gather close and look at them from far off. We watch the herd, not the animal, and we stay behind our captains because the ones who look closely are the ones who learn how small we are. The kindness is real and the cruelty is real, and neither one is aimed.
