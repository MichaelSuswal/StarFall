# People: unnamed

They may name themselves. I will not name them. A name I give is a name I own, and I would rather they were called by a word they chose and I paid for. Their home I leave unnamed too: the town at the ford, until its people say otherwise.

## Who they are
Carriers and ferrymen, herders and field hands, with a town of their own where the great road meets the river. They are a people of roads and water, who live by what moves from one place to another and by whoever stands in between. I hope they are plain people who count well, and who are proud of being hard to cheat and slow to cheat. I do not set what any one of them will do.

## How many
About five thousand, as the release gives. I hope the town holds about a third of them through the hard months, and that the rest are on the roads and boats, and gather at the town for the autumn reckoning. I know that a people this size costs what the world makes it cost, and I accept the cost in food and fuel and slow growth. Where the land holds that many or does not is the world's to say.

## How they live
They work the water meadows and the stubble fields in the short seasons and carry goods in the long ones: grain, hides, salt if the coast gives it, wool, cut reed, dried fish. Keelboats and flat barges go down the river to the shore and back. Carts and pack beasts go along the old roads to the fords. They keep fairs: the great one at the autumn reckoning, smaller ones at the fords through the year. A household has a hearth in the town, or a wagon and a berth on a boat, or both. They are scattered and moving more than they are seated, and that is the way I lean. They keep a seat because the release says they have one, and because winter closes the passes and wants a roof and a granary.

## Their code
A word is kept. A bargain is closed in the open, before a witness, with a hand struck, and the terms said by both sides aloud. A debt is a real thing and is remembered, and a family that owes pays, and a family that is owed does not hold it over the other. A guest is fed once without being asked what he will pay. Theft is a crime against the one robbed and against the road. A false weight is a worse crime than a stolen sack, because it robs every buyer after. No tithe is laid by law. The code is ordinary and costs nothing.

## Skills and culture
They are proud of their tallies. Every bargain of weight is cut on a stick of willow or alder, notched for the sum, and split down the length so that each side keeps half; the halves must fit when the debt is called. They are proud of their boats, and of the long careful knowledge of the river at low water and in flood. Their craft and their pride are left to the world to weigh beside other peoples'. They sing counting songs to teach the young, and they mourn and bury at the river's edge. I say nothing more of their manners, and the world may fill them.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I propose one edge, and I will argue its price.

Edge: the split tally. A bargain cut on a split stick is accepted among this people as proof, without argument, and a stick or half-stick carried a long way is honoured by a stranger who knows the custom. I mean it as a sure edge in trade over other peoples who rely on a man's word alone. It lets their factors deal at a distance and lets their debts travel.

Weakness I offer to pay for it: the tally is a chain. A family that holds a split stick unmet may not leave the country of the tally without settling it or giving the other half to a kinsman who will carry it. A debt unpaid passes to the heirs, down the generations, until it is settled or forgiven in the open. They cannot easily walk away from a ledger. A poor household with an old debt is a household that cannot move, and a people that cannot move is slow to flee or to settle new land.

If the Keeper prices the edge smaller, I will take the smaller shape. I would take a heavier weakness before a written fence on how far the tally may reach.

## Their magic
Left to the world. My wish, kept on record and not asked as a promise: that the magic they have be small and local, of the kind that costs the worker something they would notice missing, and that a wise-woman at a ford who can tell a true count from a false one be among them, if the world wants her there.

## What they believe about gods
Left to the world. My wish, recorded as a wish: that they think of a god as a customer and a creditor before they think of it as a terror, and that a god who deals fairly is thought of as a good neighbour. I would like them to be ready to be led and not ready to kneel. I would not mind if they came to kneel anyway, in time, and I do not pretend I would not.

## Rule and worship
Within my leans. I hold the crown myself, a god-king with a court and officers, a warlord in manner. My word is law, carried by my court and my captains. I rule by force when I must, through a war band and its captains who guard the roads and the fairs and enforce the terms I set. A priesthood stands in it as the worship lean gives one: keepers of rite with standing among the people, to whom offerings are brought at the fords and the crossroads and the fairs. No tithe is laid by law. What is brought is brought freely, and I would be glad of every offering and say so. The keepers bless the weights and measures, witness the closing of great bargains, and tend the shrines.

## Hierarchy
Three stand nearest the crown. The Reckoner keeps the tallies, the stores and the court's accounts, and sits at the bench for disputes. The Marshal commands the war band and its captains. The Voice of the Keepers heads the priesthood. Under them are the captains, the wardens of the fords who watch the weights and take no tax by law, the heads of the great houses who sit as a council when asked and are not bound to agree, and the keepers at the shrines.

A quarrel between two families, a theft or a boundary goes to the Reckoner's bench: the Reckoner sits with two keepers as witnesses, hears both sides, and sets the terms, which the captains enforce. A boundary is walked with poles and chain, in the open, before both families. An appeal to the crown is open to anyone who will state their case aloud and be heard. I may hear it myself when I am present and not otherwise.

## Helpers
The offices that run things when I am absent are the Reckoner, the Marshal and the Voice of the Keepers, as above. Orders travel from me to the three, who hear me directly. They carry it down through the captains, the wardens of the fords and the keepers at the shrines, and out to the people by heralds who call it at the market and the fair. A sealed word of mine may be carried by a herald who bears my mark, and it is heard as my own voice. Whoever holds an office is a mortal with their own wants. None is a puppet, and I do not wish them to be. Acting for me, they keep to my standing conduct and my last decisions. In my absence they keep the roads open, the weights true and the fair running, and they do not bind me to anything new.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own past and their own country. I hope they are waiting for the reckoning fair and not for me, and I do not ask them to have expected a god. I mean to arrive on foot along the road at the ford, at dawn on the first day of the fair, my hands in my sleeves and nothing in them. I mean to sit at the tally table and ask nothing, and to give something first, unasked, and see what comes of it. What they know of me on the first morning, and what my helpers know, is the world's to set. Where I come down and what the first morning holds are the world's too. How I go about it is my conduct: slow, public, and counted.

## Questions for you

1. Who strikes bargains for the crown on the days a seal of mine is out, and on what authority?
The Reckoner strikes them, in the Reckoner's own person, under standing terms I set before the seal goes out and cut on tally sticks in the Reckoner's keeping. A standing term names what may be given and what may be taken, and no more. My seal being out, I strike no bargain in my own person, and I say so aloud so that no stranger thinks otherwise. A bargain the Reckoner strikes is the Reckoner's, and binds the Reckoner and the crown's goods by the custom of the people and not by anything of mine. When the seal is back and I hear a term repeated, I may confirm it, in my own person and in the open, and then it is mine. I do not leave a bargain of the crown to a puppet. I leave it to an office holder who is a mortal with wants of their own, and who may be the one I trust most.

2. Whom do I wash before a fight, and who decides?
I decide, because I carry the wounds. The Marshal names who is to go and brings me the list. I wash the captains who lead and the one who stands at each captain's shoulder, and then any who ask and are willing to name their price and pay it afterward, in the open, as agreed. I do not wash a conscript who has not asked. I do not wash on the Marshal's order alone. I may refuse any name, and I say why. I wash no more than I judge my body will bear, and I would rather wash fewer and carry less than fill a field with the hardened and break on the aches of it. The price is theirs to name from what they would notice missing and mine to agree to, and the account is called when the fight is done.
