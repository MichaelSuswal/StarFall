# Deep history: campaign sim-03

Written by the Keeper on creation Night 3b, 2026-11-21, to the depth the age scale set (10: ruins under ruins over thousands of years). Years are counted back from the night the gods came down and written BA (before the arrival). The arrival is Year 0 evening; play opens in Year 1. Every place carries its map id. Nothing here is released whole. What any people holds of this is in its own record, and is partly wrong.

Dates that were rolled: the fall before, 22,000 BA [r-0172]; the earlier throw, 2,470 BA [r-0173]. Every other date is set from those two, from the records written on Night 3a and from the pace table, and is logged in the keeper log.

## Before the story

22,000 BA. The star before this one came down on this ground. Nothing human here remembers it. Three things on the sheet are older than that night.

- The Sleeper [f44]. It lay under the middle of the old country and came through the fall as itself. It has slept since on the Dun Back [r09], and the hill mass has taken its outline. It is small for its kind and slow.
- The Graith. They sat out that night in the deep rock of what is now the Scree Marches [r03] and woke in the new country as themselves, fewer. Their oldest saying is "the roof held." Behind the Sealed Door [f49] is the hall where they sat: its walls and floor are blue-grey glass that rings when struck, and those of them who did not wake are still seated there, grey and without faces. They shut it and do not open it.
- The great dead things. Two bodies of the Sleeper's [f44] kind did not come through. One is the Long Bones [f38] in the limestone of the Holed Wood [r18]. The other is the Stone Keel [f61] in the Western Teeth [r01]. No one living has seen the second.

The same blue-grey glass lies under everything, very deep. It can be reached in three places: behind the Sealed Door [f49]; in the lowest gallery under Swallet Mouth [f37]; and under the paved court at Averley [f39], where no one has dug.

The Sallow were in the reed water before any human people came. They are of this land. They keep no telling of where they began, and their cords go back about 300 cord-mothers and then stop.

## The first age: the Mound Folk, 5,900 to 4,700 BA

The first farmers on the sheet settled the bowl round the Dun Back [r09], which was then deep black soil and the richest ground in the land. They built in turf and timber. Their one city was Ummer, which stood where the Nameless Mounds [f57] are now: about 8,000 people at its height, granary mounds in rows, a causeway south. They raised their dead in mounds and carved a mark for each house on a post.

Cause of the end: nothing came against them. Over twelve centuries the house marks grew fewer and plainer, then stopped. Children were named later and later. By 4,750 BA the people of Ummer pointed in place of naming. The last of them walked onto the Dun Back [r09] in ones and twos over about forty years and did not come back. What remains: the Nameless Mounds [f57], with no mark on any post hole stone; the lowest course of the Sunk Road [f55]; a soil that was black and is now thin brown heath.

Story 1. Ansa of Ummer, about 4,750 BA. She was the last woman of the city who answered to a name of her own, and she walked south off the heath with two children instead of north onto the hill. A walking, singing people met her at the southern lip of the bowl and asked her the name of everything she could remember. Trace: Ansa's Spring [f58] carries her name, brought back and said over the water by the same walking people in 310 BA. It is the only water on the heath called for a person.

## The second age: the Road People and the Burying Folk, 4,400 to 3,300 BA

East: a people of the dry country cut wells along the hard pale ground and carried salt north and hides south. Their city was Qarrin, at Seven Wells [f68]: about 12,000 at its height, seven stone-lined wells, a walled yard for 2,000 beasts. They laid the Pale Road [f26] and built the way-stations, the Trough Station [f27] among them. Cause of the end: the wells dropped over three generations as the Pallow [f06] shifted its bed east, and in 3,310 BA the city was left in one season. What remains: Seven Wells [f68], choked; the Pale Road [f26]; the stations, roofless.

Story 2. Tobe Seven-Rope, about 3,350 BA. Well-warden of Qarrin, who cut the seventh and deepest well when the other six failed, and bought the city forty more years. Trace: seven strokes cut side by side, his mark, on the rim of the trough at the Trough Station [f27] and on the lip of the deepest shaft at Seven Wells [f68]. The Lowlamp take the mark for a tally of something.

South-west: the Burying Folk farmed the low country along the Chalkwater [f04] and carried their dead up into the Holed Wood [r18], because the ground there swallowed water and they held that it swallowed the dead as kindly. They cut burial niches in the upper galleries under Swallet Mouth [f37] and laid thousands more in the deep soil of the dry valleys. The unnamed town stands in one of those valleys, and its burial ground is the oldest burying ground in the region. They cut the first course of the Strand Road [f33] to carry biers. Their villages stood along the line of springs where the limestone meets the clay. The forest came back over the villages and the springs alike, which is why the old forest and the old ruins line up: both followed the same water under the rock. Cause of the end: three wet decades from about 3,400 BA drowned the low fields into what is now the Sump [r17], and the people scattered east. What remains: the niches; the graves under the deep soil; the grave words, which passed to every people who buried there after, worn down to three short sentences.

North-east: the first fort at Three-Age Fort [f48] was raised about 3,400 BA by the Road People's hill kin, a dry-stone ring to watch the saddle. It is the lowest of the three courses there.

## The third age: the three who came before, 2,470 to 1,380 BA

2,470 BA. Three gods came down in one night, thrown from a fall that is not among the entries of the plane section: it lay farther off than any of them, and the section holds only what lies round the starting lands. None of the three is on the ground now. No one alive knows there were three.

The Ring King. It landed in the low country between the rivers and was taken for a king. Its steward laid out a city in rings round the court where it sat, the city that is now Averley [f39]. It ruled about 60,000, was held in mind by all of them, and grew fast. In its last forty years its shadow paled, the court stones softened at their edges, and prayers said in the court came back late. In 2,190 BA, on a night when the star dimmed, it was no longer on the ground. Its people said the king had become something more and watched the court. They kept the court empty and went on being ruled by mortal kings in its name, the Court Kings, until 1,020 BA.

Story 3. Orra Lathe, about 2,440 BA. First steward of the Ring King, who paced out the rings with a knotted cord and walked each boundary once. Trace: the cord's knot pattern is set in darker stone across the paving of the court at Averley [f39], and the law of Averley [f39] that a boundary once walked is never walked again is hers.

The Table King. It landed in the High Table [r04] and built a hall and terraces where the King's Stair [f54] is now. It held the passes and let few in. In 2,205 BA it was seen at its own table a hand's breadth off its seat, and it set down shaken. Fifteen years later word came over the Sunk Road [f55] that the king in the low country was gone from his court. After that the Table King kept its people few, its feasts small and its passes shut, for eight hundred years: the kingdom that held its passes for a thousand years. It ended from inside. In 1,413 BA a party of its own stewards, wanting a greater king, began a harvest feast of remembrance in every hamlet against its word. On the harvest night of 1,410 BA, a night when the star dimmed, it rose from its seat at the long table before the whole hall. The stewards said the king had left the table in anger and would come back. The captains of the passes fought over the hall for sixty years and broke it.

Story 4. Sele Tarn, 1,410 BA. Cupbearer at the long table, who had filled the king's cup that night and who laid its place again the next harvest and every harvest until she died. Trace: the Arroway's seat laid for the last king is her custom. Her house mark, a cup over a line, is the oldest bread stamp in the basin and is still used by one house in Brennhal [f12]. Her grave is under the second terrace of the King's Stair [f54], with a clay cup in it.

The Hart. It landed in the Deepwood [r06]. It ruled a hunting realm from the forest, made a law that the deep wood be kept wild, and was honored at springs and standing stones. It heard of the Table King's lifting and chose the other road: it gave itself away, a little to a spring, a little to a breed of hound, a little to a stone, for six hundred years. The Hound Spring [f64] is the greatest of its givings: a hound that drinks there keeps its nose into old age. The Walked-Round Shrine [f53] is one of the least. By 1,650 BA its hunting realm had thinned to a few lodges and it was a small, quiet thing kept in mind by one people, the Hidden Folk, who had gone up into the Folds [r05] with it and built walled seats made to be overlooked: Hollow Seat [f51], the stair now called the Old Stair [f52], and the dressed stone under Stillgate [f09]. They left small things at the foot of the stair for it. In 1,380 BA, thirty years after word of the Table King's rising reached the Folds [r05], the Hart walked north-west out of the Folds [r05] and off the edge of the sheet, and all but a few of the Hidden Folk went with it. They emptied their seats without a siege. It has not come back and does not within any span this campaign can reach. What it gave away stayed where it was given.

Story 5. Dern Holloway, 1,380 BA. Last door-warden of the Hidden Folk. He and forty-one others would not leave. They went into the vault they had cut for stores, and he barred Door Under Root [f50] from the inside. Trace: forty-two short strokes scratched on the inner face of the door, the last one longer. Outside, a fallen tree has grown over the lintel.

In the same age:

- The Sunk Road [f55]. Built 2,380 BA by the Ring King's realm, from Averley [f39] north across the bowl toward the Table Gap [f15], to carry salt to the basin and wool and tin back. Under the Court Kings the bowl was farmed again along it, from about 2,300 BA. The grain there went thin and tasteless over the centuries, and the farms were left between 1,100 and 1,000 BA. The road lies under turf; three lengths of it run in cut tunnels through the low hills.
- The second fort at Three-Age Fort [f48]: the Table King's eastern garrison, 2,400 to 1,410 BA, mortared stone on the old ring.
- The travelers from afar, first coming. 1,960 BA. Two walkers came over the range from the north, the last of a line that had set out from under the star of plane entry [p04] more than thirty generations before. Neither could say the name of the country their line began in. They carried a slab of worked grey iron, of a making no smith here has matched, and knew only that it was to be struck. What it cost: the basin's smiths spent four generations and a forest of charcoal failing to make another.

Story 6. Wenlow of the Bell, 1,960 BA. Captain of the northern pass, who took the two walkers in and hung the iron in the pass to be struck at need. Trace: Bell Pass [f16] is named for it, the iron still hangs there, and his name is cut in the hanger stone under four later captains' names.

- The Turning of the Aver [f05]. 1,150 BA. In a run of dry years the Court King's knot-wives, 140 of them working together for nine days, drew part of the Sarn [f03] out of its bed to water the low country. The Aver [f05] has run to the sea by itself since. Where it leaves the Sarn [f03] the ground went bare for a mile across, the Bare Reach [f59], and it is bare now: grass with no seed heads. Ninety-six of the workers ended unable to say their own names or know their own children. The Court Kings fell within four generations, in 1,020 BA, in the famine the old tellings call the bland years.

Story 7. Ysolt Arne, 1,150 BA. Knot-mistress of the court, who led the Turning and was the first to go blank. Trace: a waist-high staff stone at the Bare Reach [f59] with a cord pattern cut round it; and the law of Averley [f39] that no worker of knots may serve the crown.

## The fourth age: captains and hunt kings, 1,410 to 450 BA

The basin. After the hall was broken the Arroway's forebears lived under pass captains and no king, and have had no king since.

The eastern range. The households of the Table King's eastern garrison, cut off, took to walking a circuit between high grass and winter holds about 1,400 BA. They are the Column's forebears. Their ring had five holds.

Story 8. Hesk Greyhand, about 1,090 BA. A captain who made himself lord of the eastern passes for one lifetime and raised the third and highest course of Three-Age Fort [f48]. Trace: his name on the gate lintel, and a rock-cut cistern that still holds water.

Story 9. Edda Corr, 620 BA. Marshal of the older ring. In the thaw of 620 BA the west wall of the fifth pass came down on the hold beneath it, which the older ring called Westcellar and which lies under that fall, with some 300 wintering there and the Marshal among them. That is the Shut Pass [f47]. The Column has had four holds since and a law against camping under a slope. Trace: her horn, found on the scree below the fall about thirty years ago, hangs at Greybay Hold [f21]; no one there knows whose it was.

The bowl. 900 BA. The Hethren came up out of the eastern grass onto the empty heath with sheep and small horses. About 400 BA they began to hold a child's name back until its seventh winter, after three generations in which children of the camps nearest the Dun Back [r09], named at birth, stopped answering to their names by their third year.

The Deepwood [r06]. 930 to 452 BA. A line of mortal kings, the Hunt Kings, took up the Hart's old law as their own and ruled the wood's edge from the Horn Hall [f63]. They built Hartwall [f08] about 700 BA as a gate lodge.

Story 10. Alder the Late, 452 BA. Last Hunt King. He rode west into the deep wood after a white boar in the mast season and did not come back. His steward, Bryn, kept his room at Hartwall [f08] ready, and the Wardens of the Door have kept it since. Trace: the kept room; the saying about the hounds lying down at the Door, which was Bryn's joke about how the hounds knew their master's step; and the king himself, who lies with his boar spear beside the Hound Spring [f64], where he drank and died of a broken thigh.

The reeds. About 260 BA the road families of the steppe took the name Lowlamp.

Story 11. Ninefinger Ro, about 260 BA. Cord-mother of the Sallow, who struck the first night-market bargain with the road families: reeds from the ford's east bank for bronze, cut by the road people only where a Sallow stake stands. Trace: the cord of that bargain hangs in the long house at Nightstake [f45], with nine knots. The reed tally unmet for three winters is a quarrel over where the stakes stood.

## The fifth age: the league and the low kingdom, 800 to 410 BA

The travelers from afar, second coming. 800 BA. A man and a girl came down the Sarn [f03], the last of another line out of the north, from under the star of plane entry [p04], by their own count thirty-three generations walking. They could not name where the line began. They brought the split stick: a debt cut across one stick, split, each party keeping a half. What it cost: with it came their rule that a half outlives its holder, so that a debt passes to the heirs. Every people on the great river keeps debts on split sticks now, and none remembers that it was brought.

The League of the Fords, 780 to 410 BA. Five ford towns of the lower Sarn [f03] swore to one weight and one tally: Tallyford [f30], Withyford [f66], Sedgeby [f65], Nettlebridge [f41], and the crossing town that is now Stakewater [f28]. Their hall was the Weighhouse, whose footings are the Old Weighhouse [f34]. In 702 BA they cast a master weight in bronze in two halves that fit, one kept at the Weighhouse and one at Sedgeby [f65].

The low kingdom. 600 BA. The present line of Averley [f39] began.

Story 12. Isaude of Averley [f39], 600 BA. A salt-house heiress who paid the debts of the last interregnum lord and took the seat in exchange. Trace: her charter is cut on a pan-side stone at Saltsteps [f40]: salt is the crown's and is sold, never given.

The Weight War, 418 to 410 BA. Averley [f39] charged that the league's weight had gone light. It had.

Story 13. Jory Pell, 418 BA. Weighmaster of the league, who had shaved the Weighhouse half of the master weight over eleven years to cover his town's short harvests, and who wrote down every shaving. Trace: his ledger, in a chest in the cellar under the Old Weighhouse [f34], with the shaved half of the weight. No one has opened the cellar since the roof came down. The other half went to Averley [f39] as plunder when Sedgeby [f65] burned in 412 BA, and lies in the treasury there, uncatalogued.

The league broke town against town. Withyford [f66] was left in 415 BA after its ford shifted. Sedgeby [f65] was burned in 412 BA. In 411 BA Averley's [f39] levy salted the league's western fields along the Strand Road [f33] so they could not feed a town: the White Acres [f67]. Averley [f39] bought the league's debts, and holds half-sticks from that time against many houses of the Marrans, who are what is left of Tallyford [f30] and the league's carriers.

Story 14. Tamar, 405 BA. A widow of the salted fields who led about 300 people west along the Strand Road [f33] into the limestone forest, to the valley of the old graves, because no one would follow them there. They are the forebears of the unnamed people, who have been in the Holed Wood [r18] 405 years. Trace: hers is the oldest of the town's own graves in the unnamed burial ground, a plain stone with no mark. The people's manners about salt and fire turned on land come from the White Acres [f67], and they have half forgotten it. They found the grave words already cut on a slab at the head of the valley and took them up.

## The near past, 410 BA to the arrival

- 400 to 74 BA. Averley [f39] held the crossing at Stakewater [f28] with a garrison and a crown reeve.

Story 15. Pell Stake, 74 BA. A ferryman's son who led the ferry houses when they put the garrison across the river in one night without a death. Trace: the treaty marks are the newest cuts on the Toll Stone [f29], at its foot: Averley [f39] keeps a tenth of the toll and no spear at the crossing. The Weirfolk have kept the crossing without a crown for the three generations since. The count cut on the stone is one tenth of the toll in kind; Averley's [f39] clerks now reckon it in salt weight, which is more.

- 310 BA. The walking, singing people last came through. They said Ansa's name over her spring, went on north-east, and left the sheet by the Pale Road [f26].
- 14 BA. The travelers from afar, third coming. One woman, the last of a line that set out four generations before from the country under plane entry [p01], reached the Shore Hills [r20] by the Pale Road [f26].

Story 16. Ibbet, 14 BA to now. She was born on the walk and never saw the country her line left. She keeps a walled plot of a blue-flowered bean at Farwalker's Cot [f60]. Trace: the bean, which crops heavily for three years and leaves ground that grows nothing for six after. The goatherds below will not plant it twice.

- 11 BA. The queen of Averley [f39] died. Osmer, a salt-house lord, has been regent since for an heir now fourteen.

## The carriers and the thinners

The carriers. A walking people, few and human-seeming, about 140, who ask the names of things and sing them back as lists. The Keeper calls them the Canters. Peoples here call them the list-singers, where they are remembered at all. They walk between stars. They were here in 4,750 BA, 3,290 BA, 1,395 BA and 310 BA, among other times, and each time walked a round of the settled country. After 310 BA they walked north-east to the country under plane entry [p01], and stayed there about a century and a half. Near that country's end they asked its keeping-house for its names and were refused. They left about 85 years before the arrival and have been walking back. They carry a few wayside names of that country learned by ear, with a held beat sung where each great name should be. Their coming in play is in the event schedule.

The thinners. Pale herds: heavy cattle-like beasts all of one bone color, with no scent and no dung. They range the empty country off the eastern edge of the sheet. On a cycle of years a herd of some hundreds walks in across the Far Grass [r13] and the Pale Steppe [r11] as far as the edge of the Rattlereed [r12], stands a few weeks, and walks out again. The Lowlamp call those years white years and will not hunt them. The Sallow say the white ones leave no track in mud. The cycle is in the event schedule.

## What each people's record says, against this history

- The unnamed people: here 405 years (their record: about four centuries). The graves were there before them: so, by more than three thousand years. The meeting holds: they wait for nothing, and nothing in this history sent them a sign. No change.
- The Thessen: descendants of the Hunt Kings' foresters and hound-keepers. The kept room is 452 years old. What was honored and let go was the Hart; they do not know it. The meeting holds: the seat was kept for a mortal king, and no one is watching for the saying.
- The Column: on the circuit about 1,400 years. Three-Age Fort [f48] and the Shut Pass [f47] as above. They have had no god over them: so; the Table King's garrison were soldiers of a king, and the Column does not remember him as a god. The meeting holds.
- The Marrans: what is left of the league. The meeting holds.
- The Lowlamp: the name is about 260 years old; the road and stations are the Road People's, more than three thousand years older. The meeting holds.
- The Arroway: the common people of the Table King's basin. The seat is 1,410 years old. The meeting holds: the seat is laid for the last king and not for a guest.
- The Ussel: came into the Hidden Folk's seats about 700 BA, out of the Deepwood's [r06] northern edge, when the Hunt Kings pushed their law north. They took up the leaving of small things at the stair without knowing whom it was for. The meeting holds: they are not waiting.
- The Weirfolk: three generations without a crown, since 74 BA. The meeting holds.
- Averley [f39]: the present line 600 years, the city 2,440. The Hethren: 900 years on the heath. The Sallow and the Graith as above.

## Checked against the deep lore

Nothing here says why any of it is so. The fall before, the glass, the things that came through and the things that did not are bodies. The Mound Folk's end and the Hethren custom are effects with no cause given. The three who came before show a rising, a refusal that failed, and a giving away, and no one in the record knows what a ceiling is. The Turning shows the cost of a great working and no one says where the cost goes. The carriers carry and the thinners walk. Nothing here answers what the deep lore keeps mysterious across campaigns.
