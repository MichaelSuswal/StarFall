# People: the Lowlamp

Campaign: sim-03. God: god-dreams. Agreed on creation Night 3a, 2026-11-20, from the god's `people-hoped.md`, its negotiation and the world. Named by the god as a wish, and the world keeps the name: they call themselves the Lowlamp. They call their winter ground the Ford, as the god wished; on the map it is Lampford [f25], the name road traders use, since a hundred fords are called the Ford. Lines in block quotes are the god's own words, agreed as ordinary life at no price; what the god wrote as a wish about the land, the past or what mortals will do is a wish, and the world's answer is given beside it.

## Who they are
Road traders and herders of the Pale Steppe [r11] and the edge of the Rattlereed [r12]. Human.

> Road traders and herders of the steppe and the reed country, who live on tolls, carriage, hides, wool, felt, reed mats, salt and dried fish, and on being the people a stranger can trust to count honestly. They are not a tribe of one blood. They are a people made of terms: families who joined the road by a bargain and stayed because the bargain held. They are clever and wary with strangers, plain and generous at their own fires, and they keep every debt in the open. Their word for themselves is the Lowlamp, from the hour after the lamps go low when the day's accounts are closed and the night's are not yet opened.

History, as the world has it: families who joined the road by a bargain over many generations, on a road and at way-stations older than any of them (the Trough Station [f27]; whose wall and trough it is, they do not know, and they use it). Neighbors: the Sallow, the reed folk of the Rattlereed [r12], who trade by night and are their oldest customers; the Column, who come down the Cairn Way [f20] for salt; the Weirfolk far down the Sarn [f03]. The trouble their god did not ask for: the Sallow say the winter reed-cutting at the ford takes reeds that are theirs, a tally of it has stood unmet for three winters, and the Sallow have stopped coming to the night market.

## How many
About 5,000, the usual number. No size cost is argued.

## How they live
Home: Lampford [f25].

> A seat that moves. In winter they stand at a ford town where the road meets the braided river: the roofless wall and the stone trough by the road may be theirs from other years, and I record that as their belief and my wish, not as fact. Houses of felt over reed frames and a few of stone, with the wagons drawn up in rings around the lamp fires. In the open seasons they go out on the road in long trains with draught beasts and herds, and the ford town is held by the old, the sick, the clerks and a watch. Every camp is drawn the same way: lamp fire in the middle, the Book wagon beside it, the herds outside, the watch outside the herds. A stranger who knows one ring knows them all.
>
> Their days: herding, hauling, reed cutting in the marsh while it freezes, felt making, mending harness, tallying, and trading at the ford. Half the camp keeps the night watches in turn and sleeps by day in shifts, so the camp is never wholly asleep or wholly awake.

## Their code
> 1. A bargain said aloud with a thing exchanged or a day named is a bargain, and it is kept past the point where it pays.
> 2. Every debt is written on a split stick, one half to each side, and a debt not written is not owed and not forgiven.
> 3. A guest at the fire is not robbed or sold to for a night. After the night they are customers.
> 4. A debt of the dead passes to the heirs, is read out at the burial, and is kept open for a season so that nothing is lost for good that can be found again.
> 5. Nobody is sold into a debt they did not read. A term that cannot be read aloud and understood is void.
> 6. Quarrels are settled by tally and not by blade, and the blade is a last line, not a first.

## Skills and culture
> Tally sticks, knot cords and the Book of the camp, which is a ledger of the debts of the whole people. Reed boats and mats, felt, harness, draught beasts, wagon building, bargaining songs, night reckoning by the stars on clear winter nights, and a craft of tending sleepers: warm wraps, herb teas and quiet, all ordinary. They bury their dead at night with a lamp burning at the head for a season. They are proud of their tally keeping and their honest weights. I leave these as their craft and their pride, and I do not ask for them to be an edge over other peoples. Where the world judges their worth beside others, I will take its judgment.

## Edges and their weaknesses
**Edge: the Night Watch.** Between dusk and dawn a camp or town of the Lowlamp cannot be taken by surprise by any mortal force: no factor for surprise is counted against it in those hours, and it stands to arms as quickly as another people does at noon. It is a habit of half the people and no more than that: it sees nothing a wakeful sentry would not see, and gives nothing against stealth by more than ordinary craft, which is unruled until it happens.
Measurable line: dusk to dawn, the surprise factor is never counted against them.

**Weakness: the Day Sleepers.** From mid-morning to mid-afternoon a camp or town of the Lowlamp is half abed. In those hours the factor for surprise counts double against it, and it counts even against a force seen coming at a bowshot. Outdoor work done in those hours goes at four fifths of the rate. Breaking camp takes the people half a day longer than it would take another people of their size, because the night shift and the day shift load the same train and do not meet well. It holds in every season. From dawn to mid-morning and from mid-afternoon to dusk neither the edge nor the weakness applies, and the ordinary factors hold. It ends only if the people give up the night watches, and then the edge ends with it.
What it bought: the Night Watch.

The Coin on the Stone, asked for the office of the Chief Keeper of the Lamp, is a power of the god's and is on its card (ledger L-0035), not an edge of the people.

## Their magic
Fitted to the world: the god wished for a worker in one village in three; the world gives less. Four or five among the 5,000: dream-readers and a charm-seller, paying in sleep and memory. None is named.

## What they believe about gods
The world's answer, part of the god's wish: they hold that any power can be bargained with and held to terms. The saying that the longest night brings a lender to the lamp exists among them, as a thing said to children. Most take gods for travelers' stories.

The god's wish, on record:
> My wish, kept on record, and not a claim of fact: that they hold a god to be a creditor and a customer, not a storm, a being you can bargain with, hold to its terms and be held by them. They say the longest night brings a lender who will come to the lamp. The world may give them whatever older belief it likes, and I will see what it gives. I will not tell them what I am beyond what the bargain requires.

## Rule and worship
Within the god's rule lean and worship lean; it departs from neither.

> Within my leans and with no departure. I hold the crown myself, a god-king with a court and officers, a merchant-prince in manner: I rule by bargain, debt, toll and trade, and my word is law, carried by the officers. I sit at the court's table as a child sits at a ledger, and I decide on terms.
>
> The worship is a priesthood: the Keepers of the Lamp, who keep the rite of the lamp turned low at the hour after lamps go low, and stand at the middle fire with standing among the people. Offerings are brought, a coin, a measure of oil, a knot in a cord, and there is no tithe by law.

## Hierarchy
> Advisers: the Court of Terms, six officers sitting with me at the Book wagon. Under me, the Steward of Terms rules when I am absent. Disputes: the Reckoners, three elders to a ward chosen by tally, one of them a Keeper of the Lamp. They settle quarrels, thefts and boundaries by the sticks. Any ruling may be brought to me, and I settle it on the terms stated and on nothing else.

## Helpers
The offices below exist among the people and are filled on the evening the god comes down; they are the offices its rule lean gives it. No holder is named; none is a puppet. They become the god's helpers as it is known and takes the crown, in play: until an office has heard the god, it runs on the people's own custom. From then on, in the god's unplayed time, the helpers carry out what it last decided and what its conduct allows, and no more. No refusal or dismissal is recorded.

> Offices, not named persons. Each holder is a mortal with their own wants and none is a puppet.
>
> 1. The Steward of Terms. Hears me, carries my word to the others, and rules inside my standing conduct and my last decisions while I am away.
> 2. The Master of Tolls. Keeps the fords, the road tolls and the weights.
> 3. The Keeper of the Book. Heads the tally clerks, who write every debt and every term.
> 4. The Captain of the Road. Leads the trains, the herds and the draught beasts.
> 5. The Captain of the Night Watch. Leads the watches and the Lamp Wards named below.
> 6. The Chief Keeper of the Lamp. Heads the Keepers of rite.
>
> How orders travel: I speak to the Steward of Terms, in person where I can. The Steward gives the word to the officer it concerns, and the Keeper of the Book writes it. Anything sent by messenger is carried on a split stick, one half with me and one half with the Steward, so that a word that does not match is not mine. I reserve the right to refuse any helper I judge a thief, and I will say why.
>
> My standing conduct, for when I am away: keep the terms, keep the watch, hide no debt, hold no war without my word, never let the lamp fire go out, and never shut me in a place with no light.
>
> An ask for pricing. One holder of one office, the Chief Keeper of the Lamp, may once a season, by a power of mine, leave me one wordless thing where I will find it: a single copper coin on a flat stone, which means only "come". It is the holder's own choice and the holder's alone, and it ends with the office or the life. The mortal pays in the body as mortal workers here pay. I pay something more at the taking up. It does not undo any limit agreed on my card, and it makes no mark. I ask that it be priced as a power on me, and I will hear the price.

## How the god meets them
Already there, as the god hoped, wintering at Lampford [f25]. What they know on the first morning: nothing. A child stands at the edge of the lamp ring and asks to be heard.

> They are already there, wintering at the ford with their own past. I come in at the hour after the lamps go low, a child in a coat cut for a larger child, with a satchel, and I stand at the edge of the lamp ring and ask to be heard. I will be heard out. I put the terms plainly: a watch kept for me and a crown given to me in exchange for a road, a ledger and a people nobody can sell. I take nothing on their faith. I do not ask them to believe I am a god, I ask them to sign. Whatever they know of me on the first morning is the world's to set. I hope the first to hear me is the Chief Keeper of the Lamp, and I hope they are curious. Hope is heard and not promised.
