# People: the Thessen

Campaign: sim-03. God: god-beasts. Agreed on creation Night 3a, 2026-11-20, from the god's `people-hoped.md` and the world. Named by the world: the god left its people and their home unnamed for them to name, and they have names already. They call themselves the Thessen and their town Hartwall [f08]. Lines in block quotes are the god's own words, agreed as ordinary life at no price; what the god wrote as a wish about the land, the past or what mortals will do is a wish, and the world's answer is given beside it.

## Who they are
Wood folk of the north-eastern edge of the Deepwood [r06], where it opens on the Sow Fen [f07] and the Mast Vale [r07]. Human.

> A forest people, wood folk of the old deep trees, who live at the edge where the great wood opens onto the wet land and the river country beyond. They are keepers of kept beasts and hunters of wild ones: swineherds who drive their pigs under the oaks and beeches in the mast season, hound breeders, trackers, snare setters, coppicers of hazel. They are patient, close, and slow to speak to strangers. They are not soft, and they are not cruel. They trade with those who show patience, and with no one else.

History, as the world has it: they are the descendants of the foresters and hound-keepers of a hunting kingdom whose law kept this wood wild; the kingdom is gone and the law outlived it as custom. Neighbors: the Hethren on the North Heath [r08], who trade wool and cheese for hounds and cured pork at the Sow Fen's [f07] edge; the Arroway beyond Smoke Pass [f18], seen every few years; no one else is known. The trouble their god did not ask for: a wasting sickness has run in the mast herds two autumns in a row, and two great houses are in an open quarrel over pannage rights that the Moot has failed to settle three times.

What the old tell, as the people hold it and partly wrong (added on Night 3b from the deep history): The Hunt Kings ruled from a hall in the deep wood and built Hartwall [f08] as a gate lodge. The last of them, Alder, rode after a white boar and is held to be away, not dead; the Door room is his. Old hound-keepers tell of a spring in the deep wood where a hound that drinks keeps its nose for life, and no one living has been to it. They say the wild-law is the kings' law. It is older than the kings.

## How many
About 5,000: about 3,400 in and close about Hartwall [f08], the rest in swineherds' steadings and coppice lodges within three days' walk inside the wood. The usual number; the god's lean was smaller and it took the usual number, giving its reason. No size cost is argued.

## How they live
Home: Hartwall [f08].

> In a walled town, gathered close, at the point where the wood meets the wet ground. A ditch and a palisade of oak stakes on a bank, with stone at the footings where the ground is hard, and a few gates. Inside, timber and wattle houses, thatched with reed from the wet land, with their pig yards and hound pens close behind. Around the wall, cleared fields and coppiced hazel, and beyond that the wood, pressing on every side. The beaten path through the trees runs out from the main gate toward the river country, wide enough for a led beast.
>
> Their year is the mast. In autumn the pigs go out under the oaks and beeches in the charge of the swineherds, and the town fattens its herds for winter. In winter they live on what was cured and stored, and the hunt. In spring they coppice and plant. In summer they cut reed, trap eels in the wet land, and gather. They hunt the deer and the boar, and keep to the old rule that what is taken from the wood is paid back to it by a share left at the place.

## Their code
> It is spoken and not written. Is a thing written down not a fence, and is a fence not a thing that a clever person will walk around, and a stupid person will be hanged by? I would have their code live in the mouths of the old, and bend where it must.
>
> - A bargain struck is kept to the letter. That is the first rule, and the oldest.
> - A kept beast is not struck without cause, and not killed for anger.
> - What is taken from the wood is paid back to it, by a share left at the place.
> - A stranger at the edge is given a fire and a roof for one night, and asked nothing till morning. After that, he trades or he goes.
> - The dead are buried with their names said over them, and the name is kept.
> - A thing seen at the Door is not spoken of in the street.
> - Patience is honoured. The one who waits a season for a single shot is held a better person than the one who hurries.

## Skills and culture
> Hound breeding, the keeping of swine under mast, tracking, snaring, hazel coppicing and hurdle making, basketry, leatherwork, reed thatching, eel trapping, charcoal burning, the curing of meat. They are proud of their hounds and their trackers. I leave these as their craft and their pride, with their worth beside other peoples' left to the world. I ask no sure edge.
>
> Their manners are quiet. They do not shout in the street. They greet with a glance and a nod, and a longer greeting is a sign of a stranger. Their songs are slow, low and many versed, sung at the fire. Their dead are buried at the wood's edge outside the wall, in a place with the names kept, and not in the town.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. The god proposed no edge, and none is priced. Their crafts are their craft and their pride, their worth beside other peoples' the world's.

## Their magic
Fitted to the world, the same way for every people: mortal working here is a rumor most doubt and a few do not. Among the Thessen there are three or four who can do a small thing (a snare that holds what it should not, a hound called back from far), each paying in the body and noticed late: warmth, sleep, a year. None is named and none is in the court. The god's wish (rare, never gone, paid slowly) is near what the world gives.

## What they believe about gods
The world's answer, near the god's wish and not the whole of it: they hold that something was honored in this wood once and let go, and that the ground keeps the shape of it. There are untended shrines in the wood (the Walked-Round Shrine [f53] is the nearest); they walk round them and neither tend nor break them. They do not agree on what was honored: the old kings, a beast, or the wood. They have no god and expect none.

The god's wish, on record:
> Left to the world, with a wish of mine on record: that they hold gods to be a thing that was once honoured on this land and then let go, and that the ground keeps the shape of what was honoured, whether or not any mouth remembers the name. They have old shrines in the wood, untended, and they do not tend them, and they do not tear them down. They walk round them. Is that not enough? This is recorded as their belief and my wish, and not as a fact. The world decides.

## Rule and worship
Within the god's rule lean and worship lean; it departs from neither.

> Within my leans. I hold the crown, as a god and a sacred monarch in manner, seldom seen. A court and officers carry my word, and my word is law. There is no priesthood, for the worship lean is none. I ask no rites, and what they do about me is theirs.

## Hierarchy
> - The Council of the House Heads advises. Each head of a great house sits on it, and it speaks for the houses to the court.
> - The Warden of the Door is the one office that speaks for the crown. When the Warden says "the crown says", it is so, and nobody else may say it.
> - Under the Warden, the officers carry the word to the town: the Master of Hounds, the Reeve of the Mast, the Captain of the Wall, and the Keeper of Names.
> - Disputes between families, theft and boundary are settled at the Moot of house heads, at the foot of the wall, with a Speaker of the Moot who holds the custom in memory. A matter the Moot cannot settle goes to the Door, and the Warden answers from my standing decisions. A matter of a death, or a bargain broken, goes to the Door always.

## Helpers
The offices below exist among the people and are filled on the evening the god comes down; they are the offices its rule lean gives it. No holder is named; none is a puppet. They become the god's helpers as it is known and takes the crown, in play: until an office has heard the god, it runs on the people's own custom. From then on, in the god's unplayed time, the helpers carry out what it last decided and what its conduct allows, and no more. No refusal or dismissal is recorded.

> Offices, not named persons. Whoever holds one is a mortal with their own wants, and none is a puppet.
>
> - The Warden of the Door. One person. Hears me, and carries my word to the Council and the officers. May see my face.
> - The Council of the House Heads. Carries the word to the houses, and carries the houses' answers back.
> - The Master of Hounds. Keeps the town's hounds and the herd beasts of the town, and sees the beasts' ways. May see my face.
> - The Reeve of the Mast. Orders the pannage, the herds and the stores.
> - The Captain of the Wall. Keeps the gates, the palisade and the watch.
> - The Keeper of Names. Keeps the names of the dead, and the oldest custom of the dead's burying.
> - The Speaker of the Moot. Holds the custom, and speaks it at the Moot.
>
> How orders travel: I speak to the Warden of the Door alone, in the Door room, in dusk or lamplight, since I see poorly in hard noon glare. The Warden speaks it to the Council and the officers. The officers carry it to the houses and the herds. What comes back goes the same way, up to the Door. When I am away, the court runs on my standing conduct and my last decisions, and no officer may add to them in my name.

## How the god meets them
Waiting, in part. The god wished that the seat had been kept empty and that one sign was told of who would come to it. What the world holds: the hunt-kings' steward kept the Door room for a king who was away, and the Wardens have kept it since by custom, empty, with no one expected. The saying that the hounds of the town will lie down at the Door for the one whose room it is exists, as an old saying a few of the old know and most take for a joke about a late master. No one is watching for it. Reconciled on Night 3b: the room has been kept since 452 BA for the last Hunt King, a mortal, and the meeting holds. The god's conduct (it comes at dusk on the beaten path, reads the path, goes to the hounds, then to the Door) is recorded as conduct.

> They are waiting. I ask, as a wish and not as a fact, that the seat has been long kept and tended, empty, and that they were told only one sign of the one who would come to it: that the hounds of the town would lie down at the Door. I come at dusk, on the beaten path, alone, in my cloak and unseen, and I read the path before I speak to anyone: what passed, how long ago, whose beasts. I go first to the hounds, since the hounds will know me and I would learn the town by what the hounds do. Then I go to the Door. What they know of me on the first morning is only the sign. They know no name, and no shape. My conduct is to be seen by few, to speak through the Warden, to hold the crown from behind the Door, and to walk among them unnoticed when I would see for myself.

## What they know of the land

Water: many small streams west to the sea; standing pools. Climate: temperate and wet; hard winter. The region runs about 608 miles east to west and 584 miles north to south.

What the land holds against what the god hoped: the forest presses on the town from the north, west and south; to the east the country is open vale and fen, as the god hoped.
