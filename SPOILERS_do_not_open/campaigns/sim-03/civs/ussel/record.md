# People: the Ussel

Campaign: sim-03. God: god-secrets. Agreed on creation Night 3a, 2026-11-20, from the god's `people-hoped.md`, its negotiation and the world. Named by the world: the god left them unnamed and will take the first name it is given. They call themselves the Ussel and the seat Stillgate [f09]. Lines in block quotes are the god's own words, agreed as ordinary life at no price; what the god wrote as a wish about the land, the past or what mortals will do is a wish, and the world's answer is given beside it.

## Who they are
Hill and forest people of the Folds [r05]. Human.

> A hill and forest people of about five thousand, already there, with a past of their own that I will learn by listening. I hope, and do not claim, that they live in more than one fold of the hills: a walled seat in the middle fold, on the slope above a stream, built where old dressed stone was already laid, and the rest of them in hamlets and steadings along the neighbouring valleys. The smoke I am told of, more than one thread of it, would be them. I hope they are a people who keep their own counsel and do not explain themselves, who are slow to say what they have and quick to know what the neighbour has. I hope they have been waiting for something, and do not themselves know what. These are my wishes, not facts. The world will say what they are.

History, as the world has it: they came into walled seats that an older people built to be overlooked and left without a siege (Hollow Seat [f51] still stands empty); Stillgate [f09] is laid on that people's dressed stone. Neighbors: none they deal with. The Thessen are a rumor to the south-east. A trader comes perhaps once in two years and does not come back. The trouble their god did not ask for: Lowstair [f11] is hungry this winter, a blight took its grain in the autumn, and no one outside Lowstair [f11] has been told.

What the old tell, as the people hold it and partly wrong (added on Night 3b from the deep history): They hold that the seats were empty when their forebears came up out of the great wood, some seven hundred years ago by the keepers' count, and that the people before them are not dead but elsewhere. The leaving of small things at the stair they found already old.

## How many
About 5,000, the usual number: about 1,800 in Stillgate [f09], the rest in hamlets along the neighboring folds (Underfold [f10], Lowstair [f11] and smaller). No size cost is argued.

## How they live
Home: Stillgate [f09].

> In country like this, in the folds. Timber and charcoal from the slopes, pigs in the oak woods, sheep and goats on the thin grass, small terraced fields of grain and flax on the sunward sides, streams for mills and for fish, and the winter long enough that every house lays in stores and keeps them close. Houses of timber on stone footings, one door each, and the seat of dressed stone older than they are. Their days are work and weather. In winter they are indoors, each house with its own fire and its own quiet. Do they not tell each other less in winter than in summer? I hope so. They have bronze and stone and fire, and whatever the world gives them of iron, and I ask for nothing beyond what a people of this level has.

## Their code
> Ordinary, and free of cost. I hope for these, as customs and not as laws I have made:
> - What is not asked is not owed. A house does not ask after another house's stores. To ask is rude, and to answer is a gift.
> - A thing said before a keeper of rite is kept to the letter. The exact words are the bond, not the meaning. Is that not fair?
> - A lie told in passing is a small thing and a lie told under the keeper's hearing is a great one.
> - What a person knows of the crown's business is the crown's. To say it to a stranger is the worst crime they have, and the crown does not ask for reasons.
> - A debt is counted exactly, in favours or in goods, and paid in the same coin.
> - To answer a question with a question is a courtesy, and to answer one plainly is a mark of trust. (My own habit, taken up by them. It costs nothing, and I would find it amusing.)

## Skills and culture
> They are proud of their stonework: the mending of old walls, the cutting of stairs in rock, the laying of dressed blocks without mortar. They are good with timber and charcoal, with wool and hides, with the long cold and what to lay in against it. They have a low singing that is done in company at rite and at work: many voices on one low note, under which a private word can be said and not overheard. They bury their dead with the mouth bound in a strip of cloth, and the name spoken once and then not again for a year. These are their crafts and their pride, and I ask for no edge from them. Their worth beside other peoples' is left to the world.

## Edges and their weaknesses
**Edge: closed mouths.** This people tells a stranger nothing of its numbers, its stores, its strength, its roads through the folds or who stands where. Any attempt by one not of this people to learn those things by talk, trade, gifts, drink or living among them takes the worst step against it that the odds allow, for as long as the people hold to the code. One of them taken says nothing of those things for a year and a day from the day of the taking, whatever is done, and only then breaks or does not, as mortals do. It is a discipline and no magic: it hides nothing that eyes can count from a ridge, nothing a power takes without asking, and nothing from one of their own who chooses to sell it. The crown, the court, the keepers of rite and the god are not strangers; a traveller who has lived among them a short time is. The code is the code as your people file has it (what is not asked is not owed; a thing said before a keeper is kept to the letter; the crown's business is the crown's). A people that drops it loses the edge.
Measurable line: the worst step against any stranger's asking; a year and a day of silence in a captive.

**Weakness: they hide their own hardship.** A failed harvest, a sickness or a hunger in a house or a hamlet is kept inside it. It is not told to the Reeve, the court or the crown for one full season after another people would have cried out, and the deaths from it are half again what they would have been. What your own ears take is yours, as your card has it, and changes nothing here: the houses still do not tell, the Reeves still do not know, and help that is not asked for is refused at the door for that season.
And, for as long as the edge stands: no stranger settles among them and few come twice; what the roads would bring (news, craft, new seed) comes late; and every bargain one of this people strikes away from its own country goes one step worse for it.
What it bought: closed mouths at the measure above.

## Their magic
Fitted to the world: three or four among them, of whom the people say that working is being overheard. Each pays in memory, a name, or sleep. None is named.

## What they believe about gods
The world's answer, near the god's wish: they hold that what is not said is kept by something. They leave small things at the foot of the Old Stair [f52], a custom they found the older people's leavings of and took up without knowing what it was for. They have no name for anything that might come, and they are not waiting: a few of the old say something will come to the stair, and are not listened to.

The god's wish, on record:
> Left to the world, with a wish on the record: that to them a god is a rumour, a shape in the treeline, a story the elders contradict, and that they believe the unsaid is kept by something, though they could not say what. I hope they have a custom of leaving something at the foot of the old stair for whoever keeps what is not said. If the world makes it otherwise, so be it. I hope they know no name for what comes down in winter, and are waiting to give one.

## Rule and worship
Within the god's rule lean and worship lean; it departs from neither.

> As the leans say, and I depart from nothing.
>
> Rule: a god-king with a court and officers. I hold the crown myself. My word is law, and it is carried by the officers and not by me. I rule by fear and my own will, in manner a tyrant, and the people serve me. I walk rarely among them and am seen on the days I choose to be feared. Is a people that is always overheard and seldom seen not the more obedient?
>
> Worship: a priesthood. The keepers of rite have standing among the people. They keep the rite of the hush, the saying of a thing before a keeper and the keeping of it, the binding of the dead, the low singing. Offerings are brought, of food, wool, wood and small goods, laid at the keepers' door and at the foot of the old stair. There is no tithe by law. They are, in their way, my ears, and the people know it and do not say so.

## Hierarchy
> - The Steward of the Seat holds the crown's voice when I am not there, and rules under me. One office, one holder, chosen by me and answerable only to me. Orders go to the Steward and from the Steward to the rest.
> - The Court is a small body of officers under the Steward: a Warden of the Wall (captain of the seat and of the folds), a Keeper of Stores (grain, fuel, salt), a Reeve for each fold (the head of that valley, answering to the Steward), and the Door Warden (who lets in and keeps out).
> - The Keepers of Rite are a priesthood apart from the court, with their own head, the Eldest Keeper, who answers to the crown and to no one else in the court.
> - Disputes between two houses, a theft, a boundary: the Reeve of the fold hears it, with a keeper present to hold the words exact. A matter that crosses a fold, or touches the crown, goes up to the Steward. A matter of death, treason, or the crown's business said to a stranger comes to me, and I give a short answer and a hard one.

## Helpers
The offices below exist among the people and are filled on the evening the god comes down; they are the offices its rule lean gives it. No holder is named; none is a puppet. They become the god's helpers as it is known and takes the crown, in play: until an office has heard the god, it runs on the people's own custom. From then on, in the god's unplayed time, the helpers carry out what it last decided and what its conduct allows, and no more. No refusal or dismissal is recorded.

> These are offices, not named persons, and every holder is a mortal with wants of their own. None is a puppet.
> - The Steward of the Seat. Hears me, carries my word out, and acts for me inside my standing conduct and my last decisions when I am away. One holder.
> - The Warden of the Wall. Holds the seat's gates and the roads through the folds. Reports to the Steward.
> - The Keeper of Stores. Counts, lays in and gives out. Reports to the Steward.
> - The Reeves, one to a fold. Settle what can be settled, send up what cannot.
> - The Door Warden. Keeps the door of the hall and hears who comes and goes.
> - The Gatherers, three plain officers of the Door Warden's household, who carry to the Steward any small grey thing found about the seat, and say nothing of it.
> - The Eldest Keeper and the keepers of rite.
> How orders travel: I speak to the Steward, or when I am away the Steward holds what I last said. The Steward gives it to the court, and the court to the reeves and the people. No one carries my word but the Steward and those the Steward names.

## How the god meets them
Already there. Not waiting, though the god hoped they were. What they and the court know on the first morning: nothing; something large is in the wood above the smoke.

> They are already there, with their own past and, I hope, waiting for something. I come down in winter into the snow and the dark wood, above the smoke, and I do not go down at once. I stay where I can hear before I am seen. I set an ear on the first thing I find that belongs to them, and listen to what is said of the fold and of whatever it is they are waiting for. I go to the seat at dusk, when everything is half said. Children and beasts are not afraid of me, and I shall let them come close first and watch what the grown ones do. I use no power on the first night, and I take no question from any mortal that I cannot hear the whole of. I ask the court who they are, and I let them tell me. I let them name me, and I take the name I am given.
>
> What I hope they know of me on the first morning: that something came down in the winter, that it is large, and that it listens. What I hope the court knows: that the crown is come, and that its word is law. These are hopes, not conditions, and I do not promise what any mortal will do.
