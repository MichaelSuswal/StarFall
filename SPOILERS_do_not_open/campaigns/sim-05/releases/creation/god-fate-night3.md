# Release: god-fate, Night 3

## Why you come down
Gods come down here to lead a people: to find or gather them, to build, to
rule, and to spread. That is what the world below expects of you.

## The land you come down into
A wide slow river with marsh at its edges. You come down on a reed bank at a marsh edge, by a ford.

What the land shows from there, in autumn:

- The ford.
- A tollhouse, in ruin.
- Carts on a road.

That is all the land shows. Who lives there, how many they are, what they are called and how they live are tonight's to settle with you. Nothing here is a distance or a direction: those come from the map when it is drawn.

The whole land is roughly a thousand miles across. On a map of it cut into about twenty squares, a day's walk is a small fraction of one square.

## What is so of the world for a people
- The peoples you will find are at about this level: before the medieval era, no steel forges. That is where the world begins, not a ceiling. You may bring in what you know, but it is built here by the people, from what the world has, at the pace of the work, and you say how it is to be made; nothing is granted by naming it.
- Mortal magic, for pricing: it is simple, and it costs the worker. If your people have any, the Keeper fits it to the world tonight.

## The people you lead
You come to a people with a seat, a court or council, and helpers who run
things when you are not there. Who are they?
Size: small: one to three thousand. The usual number, about five thousand with
a town or city, is the middle of the range, not what is expected of you.
Rule: a mortal ruler under the god, a merchant-prince.
Worship: local shrines.
Build within these, or give your reasons not to. A larger people
is not priced against a weakness; it costs what the world makes it cost (more
food and fuel, slower growth, harder to move, a longer gestation for a larger
kind of people).

What is asked of you, as it is asked of every god:
- Who they are: what kind of people, how they live, what they do with their days.
- How many.
- Their code: what is always so of them, what they will not do, what they honor.
- Their skills and culture.
- Anything that gives them a real edge over other peoples, with the weakness you offer for it.
- Whether any among them works a thing that is more than craft.
- What they believed of gods before you: what they say at a grave, over a bad harvest, or when the water comes up in the night.
- Who runs things when you are not there: which offices, and how an order of yours travels from your mouth to the last household.
- Who settles disputes among them.
- The morning you arrive: whether they know you; whether they have been waiting for something or you come as a stranger; which offices stand already under their own law, held by people who have never heard of you, and which are yours from the first hour.
- Their name, and the name of their home.

## Options
- Ordinary custom, culture and skill are free. Helpers are not priced: a people comes with those who run things while its god is elsewhere, and they carry out what your decisions and your standing conduct allow.
- A price comes in only where your people would hold a real edge over other peoples. Each edge is priced against a weakness of your people that you offer. For size: one weakness buys an edge about as large as the smallest power on your card.
- A mortal who is honest, able or loyal by character, oath and custom is free; it sets who that mortal is and promises nothing of what the mortal does under pressure. A mortal bound in fact, one who cannot do otherwise, is more than craft: that is an edge, priced against a weakness of your people, with a cost on that mortal for the Keeper to fit.
- A paid guard of ordinary size for a town is not priced. What it costs is its food and pay, out of your people's stores, which the Keeper sets tonight.
- What you say of your people is yours to set. What you say of the land or its past (an old wall, who was there before, what lies beyond the hills) is recorded as your people's belief or as your wish, never as fact. The world decides, and you will see. Naming a home does not place it.
- You set who a mortal is, never what that mortal will do.
- Names. The name of your people and of their home are yours to give. "Unnamed" is an answer.

## Questions for you
1. You will write bills for mortals. Do your own people ask them of you, and does your merchant-prince?
2. Your people move and have no fixed seat. Where is their account kept, and who keeps it when you are a season away with flies about you?
