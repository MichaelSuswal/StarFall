# People: the Orvanni, of Kes Orvan

Agreed record, written by the Keeper on Night 3a, 2026-11-23, from `god-storms`'s `people-hoped.md`. No edge was proposed and none was priced, so no negotiation round was held; the god's own words stand as agreed wherever they describe who the people are. What the god said of the land, the past, or what mortals will do is its hope or its people's belief. Home: Kes Orvan [f21], their winter town; they move with the herds.

The god on the names: The people call themselves the Orvanni, which in their old speech is "those under the open sky." Their winter town is Kes Orvan [f21], "the Hearth of the Year." These are the names I hope they hold. They are theirs to keep or change.

## Who they are
A people of the high steppe and river country beneath the ice mountains. They are herders and riders, grave and proud, slow to trust and slow to forget. They have little love of walls and a great love of the sky. They are used to hard weather and read it as scripture. A promise made to them is remembered for generations, and a promise they make is the most costly thing they give.

## How many
About eleven thousand: roughly three thousand winter in Kes Orvan [f21], the rest in great herd camps within a few days' ride. Within the god's size lean (large). Not an edge and priced against nothing. The practical costs, which the god accepted in its own file and the Keeper applies in play: winter fuel is short on the steppe and a hard winter costs them stock and old people; the herds need more grass than any one camp ground gives, so the people must keep moving and cannot gather for long; the whole people moves at half the foot rate; growth is slow.

## How they live
A close band on the move, with no fixed seat beyond the winter town. In spring they follow the green up from the river country behind the herds. In summer they hold the high grass beneath the ice. In autumn they come down and gather in the stores. In winter they hold Kes Orvan [f21] and the camps near it. They live in felted tents and in wagon houses on great wheeled frames, and in Kes Orvan [f21] in low timber and sod halls, heavy and turf roofed. Their days are herding, milking, mending, tending fire and horn, and watching the sky. Their wealth is horses, cattle and sheep, felt and hides, and their word.

## Their code
They keep their oaths, even at cost. They do not break a sworn word. They do not kill a guest who has eaten at their fire. They do not speak falsely under open sky, and an oath is sworn there and nowhere else. They honor the one who says a hard true thing. They do not leave one of the band behind to the weather while any can still walk. They will not take what is not theirs from within the band, and the penalty for it is shame, never blood, unless an oath was broken.

Arrangements the god set out in answer to its questions, kept as its hope of how its own conduct and its people's custom meet:
1. The rite is the Rite of the Open Sky. At first light, or at the first thunder of a storm, the one who makes it stands under open sky, names me aloud as the Warden of the Weather, speaks the words I teach, and burns the first fat or the first milk of the herd. Any of the people may make it, and the keepers hold the form. I will teach it to the priest-king and to the High Keeper in the first hour that they stand before me, and to every keeper who will learn it within that day. A first rite is made the same day, so that the count does not begin with a lapse. It is short enough to be taught in one morning.
2. They speak unheard under a roof: in the felted tents, the wagon houses and the timber halls of Kes Orvan [f21]. I allow it, and I do not wish to hear there, nor can I. A people with nowhere to be silent would lie to me in the open. I ask only that every oath be sworn under open sky, so that I may hear it and the oath be kept.
3. The priest-king asks, after the council has agreed and the High Keeper has judged the need. The ask is made in the rite, with the debt named aloud, so that the people know the cost before the working. I decide whether to give it. The priest-king tells the people the debt when the contrary comes. I will not call a great working without the people knowing that it will be paid for.

## Skills and culture
Horse and herd craft, felt making, leatherwork, bone and horn work, wagon building, bows, spinning and weaving, salt and fat and smoke for keeping meat through winter. They work copper and bronze by trade and a little by their own hands, and have no steel. They read weather by the sky, the grass, the animals and the ache in old bones, and the best readers are honored. They sing long songs of the weather and of what the ancestors owed, and they keep their dead on high ground, open to the sky.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. None was proposed, and none was priced.

## Their magic
Fitted by the Keeper to the world's level, the same way for every people: about one worker known by name to every six hundred people, simple, and it costs the worker (what is most her own: a name, a face, a memory), and something near goes duller. About eighteen workers known by name, scattered through the camps, weather-readers most of them. The god asked that it be rare and doubted: it is doubted, and it is not rare; the world's level is the same here as everywhere. The keepers of rite hold that the Sky is owed for each working, which is partly wrong.

## What they believe about gods
They do not know me. They hold an older belief, which I record as their belief and not as fact: the Sky keeps the count of the world, and every weather is a debt paid or called. They have no named god. At a grave they say, "Let the sky keep the count," and leave the dead open on the high ground. Over a bad harvest or a failed grass they say, "The sky is owed," and the herd-heads sit together and tally what the band has done wrong that year, and make an amends. When the water comes up in the night they strike the tents at once without waiting for morning, and say it is the sky's debt coming due, and move to the high ground. They have a priesthood of old standing, the keepers of weather lore and the count, who hold the rites to the unnamed Sky. These are theirs under their own law.
These are beliefs. Whether any is true is the world's, set on 3b.

## Rule and worship
Rule: a priest-king. One ruler, Tavrin, a man of about fifty, honest and stubborn by character and custom, known for keeping a law past what it costs him. I set only who he is. What he does when I ask a hard thing of him is not mine to set. He rules under the unnamed Sky today, by the consent of the herd-heads and the keepers. He will rule for me once he kneels, and I will not demand it before I have weighed him.
Worship: a priesthood, the keepers of rite. Offerings are brought and there is no tithe by law. The keepers already stand, serving the unnamed Sky, and will serve me when I teach them the rite and they take it up. No one is forced.

## Hierarchy
The priest-king rules. He is advised by a council of twelve herd-heads, who speak for the herds and camps, and by the High Keeper, who leads the keepers of rite. Disputes are settled by the priest-king and two keepers sitting together on the oath ground, which is open ground outside the camp and always under open sky. Small disputes are settled by the herd-head of the camp. A matter of oath goes to the oath ground, where I may hear it by the wind.

## Helpers
At landing the god has no helpers in fact. Its meeting says its people do not know it, so the rule that holds for every god holds here (ledger L-0039): the offices below exist and are filled, each runs on the people's own custom, and each becomes the god's helper on the day its holder hears the god and takes its orders, in play. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away, its unplayed time runs on its own conduct alone, and its people go on under their own law. An office the god hoped to hold "from the first hour" is a hope: the world listens and does not obey. Nothing was charged for this and nothing was bought by it; no edge was priced, so no step was given. The offices, all under the Orvanni's own law: the priest-king (Tavrin), who rules today under the unnamed Sky by consent of the herd-heads and the keepers; the High Keeper; the Marshal of the Road; the Speaker of the Herds; the Oathwitness; the Wain-masters; the council of twelve herd-heads. Orders, once any serve: to the priest-king, spoken at the morning gathering, carried by herd-heads and camp-reeves, the ram's horn for the simple ones.

The god's own account of its helpers and how orders travel, kept as its design:
The helpers who run things when I am absent are these offices, all standing under their people's own law and held by mortals who have never heard of me:
The High Keeper, who keeps the rite and the count.
The Marshal of the Road, who leads the march and the herds and sets the camp.
The Speaker of the Herds, who carries the council's word to the camps.
The Oathwitness, who stands at every oath and keeps the memory of it.
The Wain-masters, who keep the wagons, the stores and the winter town.
An order of mine travels from my mouth to the priest-king, by the wind where I may, or by the High Keeper in person. The priest-king speaks it at the morning gathering to the herd-heads. The herd-heads carry it to the camp-reeves, the camp-reeves to the households. The ram's horn calls the gathering and carries the simple orders: gather, move, hold, strike camp. A rider carries anything that must go further. Nothing is carried against the people's own law, and an order that crosses it goes back to me for judgment.

## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history. They do not know the god. It comes down in spring on a bare ridge of the Rimeward Range [r01] above the Orvan Steppe [r05]. What is its own from the first hour is only what it does: no rite to its name exists until it teaches one and someone takes it up. Its hunger counts from the day it wakes, as its card says.

The god's own words:
I come as a stranger, down on a bare ridge above the steppe in spring, and I hope the people are watching for the first great storm of the year, as they do each spring, and see me in it. I do not hope that they know me. The priest-king, the High Keeper and the keepers already stand under their own law. What is mine from the first hour is only the rite to my name, which I will teach at once, and the right to weigh them before I accept their kneeling.

## The trouble they have that their god did not ask for
Fuel ran out in the last winter before the thaw and Kes Orvan [f21] burned its wagon frames. Two herd-heads accuse each other of a broken oath over winter grazing, and the oath ground has not settled it. The High Keeper keeps the rites to the unnamed Sky and holds them his own.
