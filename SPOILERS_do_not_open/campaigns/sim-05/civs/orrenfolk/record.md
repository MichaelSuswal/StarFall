# People: the Orrenfolk, of Orrenmark

Agreed record, written by the Keeper on Night 3a, 2026-11-23, from `god-harvest`'s `people-hoped.md`. No edge was proposed and none was priced, so no negotiation round was held; the god's own words stand as agreed wherever they describe who the people are. What the god said of the land, the past, or what mortals will do is its hope or its people's belief. Home: Orrenmark [f26].

The god on the names: The people call themselves the Orrenfolk, "those who count the sheaf." Their home is Orrenmark [f26], a walled town on the black plain at the ford, with the farm villages round it. The names are mine to give; they are recorded as their own, and naming a place does not place it.

## Who they are
River farmers of the black plain: growers of wheat, barley and flax, with orchards and bean-fields, and herds of goats, sheep and oxen. They are a settled, close-gathered people who live inside and about a single walled town and the villages within a few hours' walk of it. They are plain, orderly and patient, fond of weighing, tallying and fair measure, and they trade grain, cloth and flax-oil up and down the river.

## How many
About twenty thousand: roughly eight thousand within and against the walls of Orrenmark [f26], the rest in some forty villages on the Orren Plain [r21]. Within the god's size lean (very large). Not an edge and priced against nothing. The practical costs, which the god accepted in its own file and the Keeper applies in play: they must sow about twenty thousand acres and bring them in inside a month, so every hand is in the fields at harvest and nothing else is done then; fuel is short on a treeless plain and is brought by river; a flood year or a blight is felt by all at once; growth is slow, since the plain is nearly full; the people cannot be moved at all.

## How they live
By the farm year. Sowing and reaping set everything, and the town lives off the plain and the plain pays into the town. Days are spent in the fields, at the threshing floors, in the mills and at the looms; in the workshops of potters, carpenters, smiths of copper and bronze, rope-makers and boat-wrights; and on the ford, where ferrymen and toll-keepers take their share. Houses are mud-brick and timber with thatch. Every household keeps a tally-cord, and children learn to knot it before they learn to count aloud.

## Their code
- A bargain spoken in form is kept. Breaking one is the worst thing a person can do short of killing.
- A debt is owed until it is paid, and passes to the heirs. Nothing is forgiven by being forgotten.
- Kneel to the god. Stand to a bargain.
- Waste is shameful. No grain is burnt, no bread thrown out, no measure shaved.
- The tenth is owed, in the open, by the end of harvest.
- They will not cheat a tally, break a boundary stone, or foul a field.
- They honor those who count well: stewards, millers, tally-wives, and the old.

Arrangements the god set out in answer to its questions, kept as its hope of how its own conduct and its people's custom meet:
1. A mortal who stands under the Weight and stays standing is free of it for good, and is seen to be. My court does not kill such a one, and does not hide them. They are enrolled as the Upright, housed in the temple court, and made plain-speakers and tally-witnesses, the only ones who may tell me a thing to my face without fear shading it. They are watched, they are given no command of arms, grain or tithe, and their sons are not free of me unless they too stand. The Upright are a use and a danger, and I know it.
2. The tenth is gathered by the tithe-warden of each village, counted before the village elder at the threshing floor, and carried to the bin-master of the granary. When a field's tenth is short, the owner answers first to the tithe-warden, who answers to the bin-master, who answers to the Chancellor of the Bins, who answers to me. A short tenth is made good in the next days, by labor and a double tenth the next year, and a field that cannot be made good is dropped from the blessing and walked again only after the debt is paid. A tenth hidden by lie is a worse thing and is judged under the code. I feel every field that turns, so a hidden shortfall is not hidden from me for long.
3. No. My people do not know it, and I do not mean them to. Salt is dear and scarce and the state holds it: the great salt-store sits under the Chancellor's seal and the Warden's guard, and salt in the temple is spoken of only as an offering. This guards the road of salt and me together. It is a risk, and I accept it. If it comes out, it will be by someone who learns it from my own conduct.

## Skills and culture
Grain farming, irrigation by ditch and dyke, flood-reading, milling, baking, brewing, linen and flax work, pottery, brick-making and masonry of the town wall, copper and bronze work, boat-building, and ledger-keeping on knotted cords and notched sticks, with a simple marking script the priests use for the tithe-rolls. They have a strong custom of public measure: weights and bushels are kept in the temple, and every market uses them. They sing at the reaping. They tell the year in sheaves, and at the end of harvest each household's name is called aloud in the temple court and answered, so that no house is forgotten.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. None was proposed, and none was priced.

## Their magic
Fitted by the Keeper to the world's level, the same way for every people: about one worker known by name to every six hundred people, simple, and it costs the worker (what is most her own: a name, a face, a memory), and something near goes duller. About thirty-three workers known by name, hedge-wives and field-wardens in most villages, as the god's file put it. Each working takes something of the worker's own. They are not of the god's court.

## What they believe about gods
They hold, as their belief and not as fact, that the river and the field each have a keeper who must be paid, and that the old harvest-mother, whose half-sunk altar stands at the edge of a field, was loved once and then let go, and that a god who is not paid will not come back. At a grave they say, "Counted in, counted out," and lay a sheaf. Over a bad harvest they count the household and the dead aloud so that nothing owed is forgotten. When the water comes up in the night they knock three times on the door, give the river a loaf and stand at the dyke until morning. Among their old tally-priests there is a saying, only a saying, that a counter will come down at the edge of a harvest. I wish this to be so, and I know I cannot say it is.
These are beliefs. Whether any is true is the world's, set on 3b.

## Rule and worship
I accept the leans. I am a god-king, a tyrant in manner. I hold the crown myself, with a court and officers, and my word is law, carried by them. I rule by fear and my own will, and the people serve. The state cult stands under the throne: temples, tithes and a priesthood. The Weight does the first work of ruling by itself, and I do the rest by custom, the count and the tithe. Laws are few, written in the temple, and kept to the letter, because I keep my word to the letter and I expect the same. The tithe is public and counted. Worship is the counting of names at each harvest: every house called, every household answering, and the god named. That naming is also how I am told, each harvest, what my people call me.

## Hierarchy
- The Chancellor of the Bins: advises on grain, tithe and the stores. Carries my word on the harvest and answers for the whole count.
- The High Reckoner: heads the priesthood. Keeps the tithe-rolls, the measures and the cult.
- The Warden of the Gate and Wall: commands the town guard and the wall.
- The Elders' Moot of the villages: holds old farm law over boundaries, water and inheritance. It stands under its own custom and was there before me.
- The Ferry-Wardens: hold the ford and its toll under their own old charter.
- Disputes: settled first by the village elder, then by the Reckoner's court in the town, and finally by me, with the Chancellor and the High Reckoner at my side. I judge by the letter of the bargain and the tally.

## Helpers
At landing the god has no helpers in fact. Its meeting says its people do not know it, so the rule that holds for every god holds here (ledger L-0039): the offices below exist and are filled, each runs on the people's own custom, and each becomes the god's helper on the day its holder hears the god and takes its orders, in play. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away, its unplayed time runs on its own conduct alone, and its people go on under their own law. An office the god hoped to hold "from the first hour" is a hope: the world listens and does not obey. Nothing was charged for this and nothing was bought by it; no edge was priced, so no step was given. The offices that stand under the Orrenfolk's own law: the Elders' Moot of the villages, the Ferry-Wardens of the ford, the old tally-priests with their head (whom the god hoped to make its High Reckoner), the keeper of the town's bins (its hoped Chancellor of the Bins), the warden of the gate and wall with a paid guard of ordinary size, the tithe-wardens and bin-masters, who today gather the temple's old tenth for the common store. The crown, the granary and the temple court are no one's to take by saying so: the Orrenfolk have seen no god, and what they do when one walks in is theirs. Orders, once any serve: to the keeper of the bins or the head of the tally-priests, by herald to tithe-wardens and elders, cried in the temple court and cut in a tally.

The god's own account of its helpers and how orders travel, kept as its design:
- The Chancellor of the Bins runs the stores and the tithe while I am away.
- The High Reckoner runs the temples, the rolls and the cult.
- The Warden of the Gate and Wall holds the town and the guard, which is of ordinary size and paid from the stores.
- Tithe-wardens, one for each village, and a bin-master for each granary, gather the tenth.
- Heralds and runners carry orders.

An order of mine goes by my mouth to the Chancellor or High Reckoner, then by herald to the tithe-wardens and village elders, then by the elder to each household, and every order is cried in the temple court and cut in a tally so that it can be checked. Helpers are loyal by character, oath and custom, as people are. I set who they are and never what they will do under pressure.

## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history. They have seen no god. It comes down in late summer at the edge of a reaped field above Orren Ford [f68], with the smoke of Orrenmark [f26] in sight. A few old tally-priests have a saying of a counter who comes at the edge of a harvest; it is a saying.

The god's own words:
I come down at the edge of a reaped field above the ford, in late summer, with the smoke of the walled town in sight. The people have seen no god. A few of the old tally-priests have waited, by their own saying, for a counter at the edge of a harvest, and I wish them to take me for it, but it is only their belief. On the first morning the Elders' Moot, the Ferry-Wardens and the old tally-priests already stand under their own law, held by people who have never heard of me. The crown, the great granary, the temple court and the guard are mine from the first hour, by walking in and saying so. I will meet them at the ford, in the open, in my full form, and let the Weight go before me.

## The trouble they have that their god did not ask for
Seed has bred poorly three years together, and true seed is bought dear from the south coast. The Elders' Moot and the tally-priests are at odds over who holds the old tenth. The Ferry-Wardens keep the ford under a charter older than the town's memory and answer to no one for its toll.
