# People: the Ferrow, of the Reach

Agreed record, written by the Keeper on Night 3a, 2026-11-23, from `god-rivers`'s `people-hoped.md`. No edge was proposed and none was priced, so no negotiation round was held; the god's own words stand as agreed wherever they describe who the people are. What the god said of the land, the past, or what mortals will do is its hope or its people's belief. Home: no seat; camps along the Tessen [f07] and the Meld [f04].

The god on the names: The people call themselves the Ferrow, which in their own speech is close to "those who carry across." They have no name for a home. They call the whole of the water they walk "the Reach", and that is a way of speaking, not a place. This is my wish and their custom as I hope it to be; the world decides.

## Who they are
River folk who live by crossing, fishing and trading along one long water, from the headwater springs down through the middle reaches. They are ferrymen, fishers, netmakers, reed cutters, rope twisters, ford keepers and small traders. They are not a nation with a throne. They are families and camps bound by a shared custom of the water and a long memory of who owes whom.

## How many
About eighteen hundred, in some thirty camps. Within the god's size lean (small). No costs beyond the ordinary.

## How they live
They move with the water. In spring they come up to the headwater springs and the first fords, when the channels fill and the crossings are busiest. In summer they work the long middle reaches, fishing and ferrying and trading from bend to bend. In autumn, at the low clear water, they put by what they will need. In winter they settle in sheltered camps on the banks where the middle of the river stays open. They return to the same bends and fords year on year, so their camps are known, but they hold no fixed seat. They live in skin and reed shelters that can be struck in half a day, and travel by boat where the water allows and on foot along the banks where it does not. They eat fish, eels, waterfowl, reed roots, gathered seed and the grain and dried goods they trade for. By day they work the crossings, mend nets, trade and tell debts. By night they sit at the fire and recite who owes what.

## Their code
What is always so of them:
- A bargain sworn with a hand in running water is kept. A person who breaks one is not driven out, but is not served at a ford until it is mended.
- A crossing is paid for fairly, and a traveller in flood or at night is not turned away.
- Nobody owns a spring or a ford. A camp may keep one, and may charge for the crossing, but not close it.
- Nothing is poured into the water above a camp that the camp downstream will drink.
- A debt is remembered to the third generation and is paid or forgiven aloud, never quietly dropped.

What they will not do: foul a spring on purpose, hold a traveller to ransom at a crossing, or sit still while a flood takes someone off the bank.

What they honor: ferrymen, midwives, weighers of grain, and anyone who is good at asking and is willing to be wrong in front of others.

Arrangements the god set out in answer to its questions, kept as its hope of how its own conduct and its people's custom meet:
1. Who holds what office when I arrive: the camp elders, the Ford Wardens, the Reckoners and the Gathering, all under their own law and none mine. As for whether I refuse them, dismiss them or never use them: I refuse them, plainly and for my own reasons. What is leaned on becomes owed, and I will not be owed by a people who might come to need me. I am also afraid, and I will call it caution: if they put me at the head of their table and I begin to be fond of them, I know what comes after. So I will not sit in their councils or take their offices or give orders through them. If they ask my mind, I give it once, like a price, and I leave. They go their own way, and I will deal with them as with any people at a ford. I have dismissed no one, because they never were mine to dismiss.
2. Yes, they already swear at water. A bargain is sworn with the right hand held in running water, the words said aloud, a ferryman or Ford Warden by as the witness who hears it in full, and the water is said to carry the words down to the lender. Nothing is marked by it among them but shame. It is theirs, and I did not give it to them.
3. When something follows me and I cannot stop, I tell no one why and I tell no one where. By my own wish, I would tell one person only, the Ford Warden at the nearest ford if I have done them a kindness, and only the one word, up the water or down it, and I would ask them to say it to no one. That is my wish and not their promise. I set who they are, not what they will do.

## Skills and culture
Boatcraft: hide boats over frames, log rafts, reed floats, and rope ferries hauled across fords. Fishing weirs, traps, nets and long lines. Reading a ford: how deep, how fast, what the bed will bear, and when it will rise. Rope, twine and cord work of a high standard. Reed and basket work. Tanning and sewing of waterproofed hides. Careful accounting by notched tally sticks and knotted cords. A habit of putting something by, because the cold teaches it. They sing work songs for hauling and for crossing, and they tell stories that explain why each ford is called what it is called. They speak plainly and bargain well.

## Their magic
Fitted by the Keeper to the world's level, the same way for every people: about one worker known by name to every six hundred people, simple, and it costs the worker (what is most her own: a name, a face, a memory), and something near goes duller. Three workers known by name along the water, each a ford-wife or net-mender who also works; the camps call it "listening" and hold that it is only listening, which is partly wrong: each working costs the worker something of her own.

## What they believe about gods
They do not think much about gods. They say the water is lent: the rain lends to the spring, the spring to the stream, the stream to the sea, and the sea pays it back in cloud. Nothing is owned outright. At a grave they put a pebble from the bed into the river and say the person has been paid back to the lender. After a bad harvest they say the second year is the hungry one, and they count and ration for it. When the water comes up in the night they say the river is calling in a loan, they tie boats high and drag the children up the bank, and nobody goes down to the water until light. Of the nameless things that live in the reed beds and at spring mouths they say "we do not call that anything, we only keep away." They have an old saying that a stranger at a ford in the thaw looks like one more thing the melt has brought down, and they are polite to strangers for that reason. They do not wait for a god. Whether any of this is true is for the world to say.
These are beliefs. Whether any is true is the world's, set on 3b.

## Rule and worship
No one rules them as a king. Each camp is run by its own elders under the custom of the water, and the camps meet at the great fords in season to settle what concerns more than one. They ask no worship of me and I ask none of them. I give no rites, take no offerings and expect no shrines. What they do about me is theirs.

## Hierarchy
- Camp elders, usually three, chosen by the camp from those known to keep their debts. They advise and settle small matters.
- The Ford Wardens, one for each great ford, hold the crossing, set its fee, and keep the tally of who has crossed and what is owed. They settle disputes about crossings and sworn bargains.
- The Reckoners, who keep the knotted cords of the debts and bargains between camps in memory and on cord.
- The Gathering at the great fords, when several camps meet, where the elders and Wardens settle disputes between camps by custom, and the oldest Warden present speaks last.
- Disputes among them go to the camp elders first, then to the Ford Warden, then to the Gathering. I do not settle them.

These offices already stand under their own law, held by people who have never heard of me: the camp elders, the Ford Wardens, the Reckoners and the Gathering. None are mine from the first hour. They run things when I am away, as they always have, by their own custom. Orders of mine, if I ever give any, would go by word at a ford: I say it aloud once, to whoever is present, as a trader names a price, and the Ford Wardens' boatmen carry it camp to camp along the water at the pace of the crossings. ## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history. They do not know the god and were waiting for no god. It comes as a stranger at the First Ford [f67], below the Ledge Spring [f70], in the thaw, and does not say what it is.

The god's own words:
I come as a stranger at the ford below the spring, an old man with an ash staff, standing in the thaw. They have not been waiting for me. I do not announce what I am. I ask my questions, I trade fairly, I help where help is asked, and I leave them to their own law. If they find out what I am, they find it out by nearness and by what I do, and I will not name it for them.

## The trouble they have that their god did not ask for
A debt feud three generations old between the uppermost camps and the camps of the middle reaches, each holding the other's cords false. Below Meldry [f27] the Meldrin [the kingdom of r14] have begun to charge the Ferrow a king's toll at fords the Ferrow hold nobody owns. Fish runs at the fords were thin last autumn.
