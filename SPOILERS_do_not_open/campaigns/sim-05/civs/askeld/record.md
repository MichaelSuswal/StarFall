# People: the Askeld, of Kellgarth

Agreed record, written by the Keeper on Night 3a, 2026-11-23, from `god-beasts`'s `people-hoped.md`. No edge was proposed and none was priced, so no negotiation round was held; the god's own words stand as agreed wherever they describe who the people are. What the god said of the land, the past, or what mortals will do is its hope or its people's belief. Home: Kellgarth [f25].

The god on the names: The Shepherd gives the names as its hope. The people may call themselves otherwise. Askeld is their own word for "those who are counted in," and Kellgarth [f25] is their word for the walled yard where the town keeps its herds.

## Who they are
A herding, farming and hunting people of the edge country, living where grass meets forest. They keep sheep, goats, cattle, pigs and geese, grow barley and oats in the river fields, cut timber at the tree line, and hunt the wild herds by law at the season for it. They are shepherds and drovers first, then farmers, then carpenters, tanners, weavers, potters and smiths of bronze and soft iron. They are broad and weather-brown and loud at table, quick to share and slow to forget a debt. They have no steel forges.

## How many
About nine thousand: a walled town of about five thousand and the farms, steadings and summer pastures round it. Within the god's size lean (large). Not an edge and priced against nothing. The practical costs, which the god accepted in its own file and the Keeper applies in play: more grain than the river fields give in a poor year, so the herds are eaten down after a bad harvest; winter fuel for a town of five thousand keeps the tree line moving back, a little each year; growth is slow; the town cannot be moved, and the herds can only be moved at half the foot rate.

## How they live
A walled town gathered close, on the grass at the forest's edge, with the river on one side and the hills behind. The town is twelve wards, each with its own yard, well and ward-head. The wall is timber on a stone footing, with a gate to the river, a gate to the grass and a gate to the trees. Outside it lie the barley fields, the summer pastures and the folds. The year runs by the herds: calving and foaling in spring, the long grazing of summer, the Reckoning and the great hunts in autumn, and the thrifty keeping of winter. Days are spent on the herds, the fields, the wood and the crafts. The evening meal is shared by the household, and on feast nights by the whole ward.

## Their code
- Every head is counted, and a count is told to those it concerns. A number is never told false. A man who tells a false count to a Reckoner is put out of the Reckoning for a season and pays the difference threefold.
- No beast is taken unnamed. A kill is lawful only when it is named aloud beforehand, marked and brought to the tally.
- A guest is fed before he is asked his name. The table is open to any who come in peace, and the host may not strike a guest or be struck by one.
- A debt is written and paid exactly, in the kind it was owed.
- A shepherd does not leave a straggler. A loss is mourned, entered on the tally and told.
- They will not slaughter in the spring, and will not hunt a nursing mother.
- They honor the count, the herd, the table, and the one who keeps the law.

Arrangements the god set out in answer to its questions, kept as its hope of how its own conduct and its people's custom meet:
1. The count is heard by the Reckoners, the Steward of the Count and the ward-heads, and in the open at the autumn gathering by the people. The Shepherd tells only the figures it knows to be true. Since its own count by eye and finger comes to it one short, it counts again before it speaks. When a count comes short, it is said aloud and cut on the tally at once, and the Steward asks where the head went: wolf, weather, thief or neglect. A loss to wolf or weather is mourned and entered, and no one is blamed. A theft is paid back by the thief threefold, in kind. A loss by neglect is paid by the household that kept the beast, in kind, from its own stores. A false figure told to the Reckoner is put out of the Reckoning for a season and pays the difference threefold. The Hornwarden then looks for the cause, and the Master Drover sets watchers on the herd until the count holds.
2. A lawful hunt is named aloud, by the Shepherd or the Hornwarden, from the temple steps before the people. The name states the kind, the number, the ground and the day, and is cut on a hunt-tally and carved on the post. The hunt begins with the horn blown three times, and every lawful kill is notched on the tally at the Reckoning-house, with the carcass brought in to be shared by the tithe and the ward. A kill with no named hunt, no horn and no notch is not lawful, and anyone watching can tell: the horn was not blown, the post does not name it, the carcass is not brought to the notch. Because The Count of the Herd bars the god's hunt for two dawns after a count, the great hunt of a counted kind is named on the third dawn. A herd that has been called is not hunted lawfully, for the call may not be betrayed, so a named hunt is made on the wild herds that have not been called, and a kind that has been called is released and left alone for the year. Where a hunt would be at the god's word on a kind that is called, the Shepherd says so aloud before the kill, in the people's hearing, as its price requires, and the people understand that the kind will not answer again for a year.
3. The people keep and eat sheep, goats, cattle, pigs and geese, and the Shepherd will not speak with any of those kinds. It speaks with the wild red deer, the horses of the open grass and a few more: the hare, the wolf and the crow, which the people do not eat. The Shepherd does not intend to speak with a kind the people keep and eat, because it would never again eat that kind and could not sit at the head of a table where it is the dish. If a kind is later needed for speech, the Shepherd will say so aloud, name the kind and give it up at the table before it speaks.

## Skills and culture
Herd husbandry, breeding by tally, wool, hide and leather work, cheese and butter, salting and smoking of meat, timber building, fieldwork, bronze and soft iron smithing, rope and net, horn and antler craft, and the keeping of tallies on notched sticks and knotted cord. They sing the herds home. They hold a spring blessing of the young, a summer drive to the high pasture, and the autumn gathering, the greatest feast of the year. They have no letters; their law is written in notches and cords, and in carved wooden posts at the temple steps. We hope they would take up written letters from travelers, and we say how only when it comes.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. None was proposed, and none was priced.

## Their magic
Fitted by the Keeper to the world's level, the same way for every people: about one worker known by name to every six hundred people, simple, and it costs the worker (what is most her own: a name, a face, a memory), and something near goes duller. About fifteen workers known by name, a hedge-woman in most wards and at the larger steadings (the god's file hoped for one or two). Trusted and a little feared. The Askeld hold that the price is named beforehand, which is partly wrong.

## What they believe about gods
We record this as their belief, not as fact. They hold that the herds are lent, not owned, by something older than the town that lives in the hills, and that a debt of thanks is owed to it each autumn. They tell of a Lead Animal who will one day come down to the water and walk before the herd of the people, as the lead beast walks before a herd. At a grave they say "counted home," and tie a knot in a cord and lay it with the dead. Over a bad harvest or a short count they say "the hill is owed," and give a share of the next kill to the hill. When the water comes up in the night they ring the ward bells and drive the herds up to the high ground, and say "the herd first, then the house." They have no name for a god, only for the hill and for the Lead Animal.
These are beliefs. Whether any is true is the world's, set on 3b.

## Rule and worship
We hold to the leans. The Shepherd holds the crown itself, a god-king with a court and officers. Its word is law, carried by them. It rules as a priest-king in manner: through rite, the autumn gathering, and its own visible presence. A state cult stands under the throne: a temple at the center of the town, tithes in grain, wool and beasts, and a priesthood that keeps the calendar and the count. The temple is a long open-sided hall, like a table out of doors, with the law-posts at its steps.

## Hierarchy
- The Shepherd: crown and final word.
- The Steward of the Count: first officer, keeps the tithe, the stores and the tally of the whole people, and speaks the Shepherd's order when it is away.
- The Hornwarden: keeps the wall, the paid town guard of ordinary size, and the lawful hunt.
- The Lawsayer: keeps the law-posts and settles disputes.
- The High Tallyman: chief of the priesthood, keeper of the temple, the feasts and the calendar.
- The Master Drover: chief of the herds and the drovers.
- The twelve ward-heads, each over a ward, and the household-heads under them.
Disputes go first to the household-head, then to the ward-head, then to the Lawsayer. Appeal goes to the Shepherd at the autumn gathering.

## Helpers
At landing the god has no helpers in fact. Its meeting says its people do not know it, so the rule that holds for every god holds here (ledger L-0039): the offices below exist and are filled, each runs on the people's own custom, and each becomes the god's helper on the day its holder hears the god and takes its orders, in play. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away, its unplayed time runs on its own conduct alone, and its people go on under their own law. An office the god hoped to hold "from the first hour" is a hope: the world listens and does not obey. Nothing was charged for this and nothing was bought by it; no edge was priced, so no step was given. The offices that stand under the Askeld's own law: the twelve ward-heads, the Lawsayer, the Master Drover, the elders of the herds, and the town's own keepers whom the god hoped to make its Steward of the Count (today the keeper of the Reckoning-house), its Hornwarden (today the captain of the wall, with a paid guard of ordinary size) and its High Tallyman (today the keeper of the autumn rite to the hill). The crown is no one's until the Askeld give it. Orders, once any serve: spoken at the temple steps, cut on a tally and a law-post, carried by hornmen to the ward-heads.

The god's own account of its helpers and how orders travel, kept as its design:
The Steward, Hornwarden, Lawsayer, High Tallyman and Master Drover run things when the Shepherd is away, each in their own charge, and they carry out what its decisions and its standing conduct allow. An order is spoken by the Shepherd to the court at the temple steps. The Steward has it cut on a tally and carved on a law-post. Hornmen carry the tally to the twelve ward-heads, who read it aloud in the ward yard at dusk, and the household-heads carry it into the household. A horn signal marks every order of the whole people. The tally copy stays at the Reckoning-house. Offices that stand already under the people's own law, and are held by people who have never heard of the Shepherd, are the twelve ward-heads, the Lawsayer, the Master Drover and the elders of the herds. The crown, the Steward of the Count, the Hornwarden and the High Tallyman are the Shepherd's from the first hour.

## How the god meets them
Already there, with their own history. They do not know the god by name. It comes down in autumn on open grass by the Brindlewater [f10] and kindles its antlers at dusk to be found. They have a song of a Lead Animal; it is a song, and whether they take the god for it is theirs.

The god's own words:
The Shepherd comes down on open grass by the river at the forest's edge, in autumn, with herds at the water. We hope the town lies within a day's walk, and we say only that we hope it. They have not known us by name. We hope they have been waiting, in the way of their song of the Lead Animal, but we record it as a hope and not as fact. At dusk we kindle the antlers so that we may be seen and found. We count the herd at the water first, and then speak to whoever comes: drovers or watchers, we hope. We say that we are come to lead them, we ask their count, we ask their names, and we ask to be taken to their table. We do not take the crown before they have heard our word and their elders have answered it.

## The trouble they have that their god did not ask for
The autumn count has come short two years running, and no one agrees whether it is wolves, thieves or something out of the Uncounted Wood [r13]. The keeper of the hill's rite holds the share given to the hill and will not gladly see it go elsewhere. Good iron is short; their smiths work soft bog iron.

## What the old tell

- Kellgarth [f25] was walled in the time of the first Lawsayer, five hundred years ago.
- The Long Mound [f58] is where the Lead Animal first lay down.
- The downs to the north-east, the Sleeper's Downs [r15], the old one asleep, are the mother of all herds, who will get up when the count comes right.
