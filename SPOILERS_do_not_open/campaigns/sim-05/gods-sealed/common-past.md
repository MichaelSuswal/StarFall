# The common past

The far country that the gods who came down here are the remainder of (deep lore, Truth 5). None of them is a person from it. Each is one of its most-held patterns: an office, a custom, a habit of a place, as its people remembered it. What follows is what those patterns were and how the country ended. Where it lay, how far off, and what else lies about this world, is set on Night 3b and is not given here.

## The country

Sarn Veth was a river country. One great river, the Marrow, ran the length of it from stone springs in the uplands, the oldest of them the Spring of Arle, through braided middle reaches, to a slow tidal mouth and the harbor town of Calder Hythe. It was old when it ended: its outer country had long gone plain, and what was left lived close along the river.

Its peoples kept themselves by counting and marking, and by one yearly gathering. At the turning of the year, on the water meadows of the middle Marrow, they laid a long table out of doors, the Holm Table. The herds were brought down to the river and counted there. The year's disputes were heard there by a moot of many voices, the Court of Sennet, which sat the rest of the year in a hall cut into Sennet Hill, with a door at either end. The year's weather was thanked and bargained with there: the country knew its weather as two, a hard clear thunder off the uplands and a wet one off the sea, called Hesk, and held that the Court kept the balance between them. And the corn-name was said there, by everyone at once: the word for the keeper of the great granary-hall at Brede, on the black soil below the meadows.

Above the meadows lay the Liberty of Asken, a district without walls whose whole law was its boundary. An office called the Lineward walked that line every year with chain and stakes and stood its stones back up, from one written record, the roll.

## Near the end

The year of the short count. At the Holm Table the keeper of the common herds, the herd-lord, gave a count that came out one head short. The Court of Sennet held the herd-lord to its bond and had it chained in a gorge of the upper river, the Narrows, until the count should come right. The count never did. The missing head had been taken for the Court's own table by the Court's own officers, and the Court's clerk knew it.

The flight of the clerk. The clerk, a small exact office the Court had made out of itself to keep its tallies and its door, could not get the matter reopened. In the next session it shut the hill door on the Court as it sat, from the outside, and ran, taking the door-tally with it. The Court was not heard again. No one unchained the herd-lord.

The breaking of the second weather. With the Court shut, nothing stood between the two weathers at the next turning. Hesk came in off the sea and did not go back, and drove the tide up the mouth of the Marrow. The upland thunder came down on it and broke it for good. The country had one weather after that, and its thunder had nothing to answer it.

The drowning of Calder Hythe. The breaking came too late for the harbor. The water rose in the town a hand's breadth at a time through one night. The harbor's own custom, a lamp lit in a seaward window and a count of boats in, held to the end, and the count came out short. The ferrying habit of the Marrow took boats upriver all that night and did not take everyone. The river below the meadows ran foul for years after.

The names at Brede. The survivors of Calder Hythe were fed from the granary-hall at Brede. The price was that they were entered in the Brede tally under the corn-name and said it with everyone else; the harbor's own name stopped being said within a generation. Brede grew by it. Later the Holm Table stopped being laid, and then the corn-name itself stopped being said, and the face on the hall's stone wore off.

The cuts at Asken. The year after the drowning the Lineward found the line, as the roll gave it, running through ground that had gone plain. It struck that stretch from the roll and walked a shorter line, leaving the hamlet of Tharrow Green outside. It did the same in later years. At the last it destroyed the roll, so that no one could read how far the line had come in, and stopped walking.

Then the star of that country came down, and the country ended. What was most held in it was thrown clear.
