# Deep history: sim-05

Written on Night 3b, 2026-11-23. Depth from the Night 1 result on the age of the human story (10: thousands of years, ruins under ruins). Dates are in years before the arrival, written BA. The world as the Keeper knows it is called Ellowain. Every place carries its map id. Rolled dates name their roll; every other date is set here, once, and is fixed.

The deep lore stands over all of this. Nothing below is known whole to any mortal, and what each people tells of it is written in that people's record, partly wrong.

## The ground before any people

The star that hangs here now began to gather after the last fall on this ground, 51,200 BA [r-0165]. That fall left a floor of blue-grey glass under the whole middle of the land, deepest toward the Sleeper's Brow [f65]. It lies far down under soil and stone, and is bare in one place only: the lowest floor of the Elder Granary [u05], whose diggers stopped when their picks rang on it.

One thing here is older than that fall. The living thing at the Sleeper's Brow [f65] came through it and has lain in the same place since, under the middle of the star. The Sleeper's Downs [r15] are ground, and are called for it because the long line of them from any height looks like its flank. The thing itself is a few miles across, has a beast's plain shape, breathes about once in a season, and has not moved in the human story. On the nights when the star dims, a thread of blue light comes down from the star and ends on it.

The first country after the fall carried great beasts of shapes not seen since. They were gone within ten thousand years. Their bones are the Rime Bones [f56] in the ice of the Rimeward Range [r01], the Strand Bones [f57] on the east shore, and the bones under the Long Mound [f58]. They are not kin of the thing at the Brow.

The Ilwe are of that first country too: a people the new ground raised, older here than any human. They have lived in the reeds for longer than they count, and the Ilwemere [r23] has grown seaward under them, a few yards a year.

## The peoples who came over water

The land is an island of the plane, with sea on every side, and the edge of the star's rain lies far out past every shore. Everyone not raised by this ground came by water or over the winter ice.

- About 8,300 BA: the first humans, in hide boats along the south, from the west, out of the country that now lies under the star of plane entry [p03]. They settled the coves of the Vessar Coast [r22].
- About 5,300 BA: the Varrun, over the winter ice of the Cold Sea [r30], from the far north, out of the country under the star of plane entry [p04], which was failing even then. They went up into the Varrun Heights [r02] because the heights were empty and like home. Their holds are older than every road.
- Lone bands and strangers in every age since, a handful in a generation, each from under one of the stars of the plane section, none from farther than its nearest shores unless as the last of a line that set out generations before.

## The layers, oldest first

### The Cutters of Ombrel, 7,400 to 6,100 BA

The first town in the land stood where the School at Ombrel [f66] stands. Its people found the grip of the working early and taught it in the open. They called themselves the Cutters, for the tally cuts they made of each working's cost. For thirteen hundred years they worked the coves, the vines and the weather of their own shore, harder each century. In 6,100 BA their teachers joined in one working to turn a season of storms from the harvest. The storms turned. A circle of ground a short walk across, where they had stood, has grown nothing particular since: grass with no seed heads, and stone that takes no lichen. More than a hundred workers were left alive and empty. The town broke up within a generation. What remains: the bare circle beside the school; the school's lowest courses of fitted stone; the Cutters' tally-cuts on those stones, which no one now reads as costs.

The school was founded again on the old courses in 1,480 BA by a teacher remembered as Aune of the Quills, and has stood since. Its rule that a teacher is neither struck nor lied to is hers.

### Old Emmer, 5,600 to 4,300 BA

A harbor kingdom on the narrows of Emmer Bay [r33], where Saltgate [f24] stands now. Its lords took toll of every boat on the bay and built in dressed stone: quays, vaults and a sea wall. In 4,310 BA its queen, Mereth, had her workers lower the bar across the narrows so that deeper hulls could pass. The bar went, and so did what had held the tide out: over eleven winters the sea came up through the lower town and stayed. The upper town was left by 4,300 BA. What remains: the Drowned Quay [u06], under the harbor floor and the wall of Saltgate [f24], with Mereth's seal-stone still set over the arch of the lowest vault; and the water in those vaults, which is neither salt nor fresh and holds no weed and no fish.

### The Binwrights, 3,900 to 2,200 BA

Grain farmers of the black soil. Their first seat was on the Sable Plain [r20], at a great hall where the yearly count was said aloud by everyone at once. By 3,000 BA the wheat of the western plain had gone thin and samely, and their bin-master, Oddo Karr, led them east to the Orren Plain [r21] and dug the Elder Granary [u05] there: bins cut in rows into the ground, far larger than one harvest, because Karr meant to store ten years against the next thinning. The diggers stopped at the glass floor. What remains: the Hollow Hall [f63], roofed still and empty; the Elder Granary [u05] under Orrenmark [f26], of which the town uses the top; Karr's tally-posts along its first gallery.

### The Crowned Years, 2,200 to 1,660 BA

In 2,200 BA [r-0166] earlier gods came down here together, thrown by a fall farther off than any entry in the plane section, which is given no entry. They found the Binwrights, the coves and the river farmers, and led them. The peoples called them the Crowned. They were few, and each took a part of the land.

- Hathra, called the Granary-Mother, took the black soil and the Binwrights. The Hollow Hall [f63] was given to her, and an altar with her face was set at the field's edge above the ford of the Orrenwater [f11].
- Corvane, called the Roadwright, took the middle river. Corvane founded Meldry [f27] in 2,150 BA and paved the roads from it: the Meld Road [f42], the Old North Road [f40], the Hill Road [f41], the Dry Road [f44], the Toll Road [f43], the Bin Road [f46], the Wood Road [f45]. The road-measurer was a mortal, Iselle, whose mark, a hand and a rule, is cut on the mile-plinths. At the ford of the Braidwater [f03] she set a paved crossing, which the realm's speech called a carrow, and laid out the wet ground beside it in lines of set stones for the families who kept the ford.
- Ilmar, called the Doorkeeper, took the law, the tolls and the keeping of records, and sat at Meldry [f27].

In 2,050 BA Corvane and the Varrun thane Urrak made the bargain of the gate: the realm might pave to the Varrun Gate [f50] and no farther, and the Varrun would not come down in arms. Urrak's boundary stone stands at the gate.

In 2,040 BA the gangs cutting the Hill Road [f41] broke into a hollow hill and found a thing in it: a small slow eater, of the same kind as the thing at the Brow and no part of it. A whole work camp walked into the hill over one autumn, by ones and twos, and those who were pulled back could not say who they were. The captain of the gangs, Dunmar Rhoss, was the last to go in. Corvane chained it where it lay with a chain of bronze. Its name, as the realm gave it, is Ummer.

The realm grew to hold most of the land between the heights and the south coast, some four hundred thousand people at its widest. All of them held the Crowned in mind.

Hathra grew fastest. Her priests remembered her and could not draw her face. In 1,930 BA, at the harvest count, she rose from her altar into the star before the whole plain. The face on the altar has been smooth since that year. In the Hollow Hall [f63], where her name had been said by ten thousand at once each year for two hundred and seventy years, a shout has come back ever since with one word after it that the shouter did not say. No one living knows it for a name. A priestess, Anwen Sorrel, scratched what she saw on the altar's back: "She went up like chaff. We had done nothing wrong."

Corvane rose in 1,790 BA, the year the last road was finished, from the sky-hall on the steppe where the Roadwright's measurers kept sight-stones on the star. The hall's roof was taken down for the watching and never put back. It is the Roofless Hall [f62].

Ilmar had watched both go and meant not to follow. From 1,790 BA Ilmar gave away what it held, into things: the realm's measures and its roll of every boundary, cut on stone leaves; its terms and its accounts; its lists of names; a spring; and one mortal. For each it had the Varrun cut a hall and a door, and the mason was one Varrun, Grusk, whose sign is on every door. In 1,780 BA the Varrun took a second bargain for the first of them: the door for silence. They would keep the Stane Door [f51] shut and say nothing of what lies behind it, and the realm would send no one above the gate, ever.

- The Stane Hall [u01], behind the Stane Door [f51]: the realm's standard measures in bronze and stone, and the Stane Roll, the stone leaves that give every boundary of the realm, the set-stone lines at the ford of the Braidwater [f03] among them.
- The Barrow Hall [u02], behind the Barrow Door [f52]: the hollow hill itself, walled up, with Ummer on its chain. The door names what is bound and says who bound it. Ilmar stored nothing here; it shut a thing in.
- The Hall of Terms [u03], behind the Door of Terms [f53]: the realm's law, cut on the door itself, and inside, the reckoning tablets, on which the clerks of each reign listed the things that had ceased in it: a fish, a strain of barley, a way of speech, a village.
- The Fell Hall [u04], behind the Fell Door [f54]: the names of the realm, and their keeper. Ilmar's chamberlain, Tessaly Vaun, took a share of what Ilmar was giving away and sat down behind the door in 1,700 BA to keep the lists. She has sat there since, asleep for years at a time. The door has no lock because it opens from inside.
- The Capstone Chamber [u07], under the Capped Spring [f55]: a spring Ilmar had drunk from daily and then gave itself to. It was capped in 1,720 BA so that no one would spend it.

It was not enough. With the others gone, every prayer in the realm came to Ilmar alone. A court clerk, Belam Hoyt, entered in the rolls of Meldry [f27] for three years running that the king stood a hand above the floor. In 1,660 BA [r-0166, r-0167] Ilmar rose on a night when the star dimmed. None of the Crowned is on the ground. They are in the star, and they are among those there who want the fall, and soon.

### The long shrinking, 1,660 BA to the arrival

The realm had been ruled by what it could see. Within two generations its governors were kings, and within five the kings were at war.

- 1,500 to 450 BA: the Coast Realm of Vess, seated at Vessa [f30], broke away first, taking the salt. It fell in 450 BA to a plague and to forty years of salt war with Meldry [f27]. Its towns went on alone and made their league about 180 BA.
- 1,400 BA: the ford-station on the Braidwater [f03] was left empty when the kings stopped paying its keepers. The paved crossing stayed.
- 1,250 BA: salt-boilers built the first wall of Saltgate [f24] on the old sea wall of Emmer.
- 1,100 BA: the walls of Orrenmark [f26] were raised over the granary by the farmers of the Orren Plain [r21], who kept the tithe to an empty altar for another three hundred years and then kept it for themselves.
- 900 BA: the hill clans of the Threeways Hills [r10], who descend from the realm's own door-wardens, rose under a chief, Sollis Carn, and burned the realm's town at the three roads. Carn's cairn is on the Hill Road [f41]. The clans have kept the Barrow Door [f52] since, without knowing more than that it is theirs to keep shut. The present Threeways [f23] grew at the crossing from about 500 BA.
- About 700 BA: herders from the Underlee Plain [r16] walled Kellgarth [f25].
- 610 BA: the market town on the Meld Road [f42] now called the Dry Market [f61] was left. It did not burn and was not sacked. Its last reeve, Callis Orme, kept the roll of stallholders to the end; in its last leaves the names are entered, then crossed through, then left as blanks with only the stall's place.
- 420 BA: Meldrin lost the lower river in all but name. The toll-captain of the ford on the Bayward Arm [f12], Harl Bessin, closed his books and walked away. They are the ledgers of the Old Tollhouse [f60].
- About 310 BA: farming families from the Meldrin Vale [r14], tired of tolls, came up the Old North Road [f40] and settled the empty ford-station. They took the place's old word for their own name. They are the Carrow. Their first Judge of the Ford, Ennet, set the iron bar in the market wall.
- About 240 BA: the Orvanni built Kes Orvan [f21] after three killing winters.
- The Ferrow, the Reedwain and the Fordfolk have no founding year. Each is a way of living that people joined.

The Meldrin of today are what is left of the realm round its first city. Meldry [f27] was built for sixty thousand and holds six.

## What the thing at the Brow has done

It does nothing that can be seen. For as long as people have lived in sight of the downs, those who live nearest stop using each other's names, a little more in each generation, and then stop keeping other things: which field was whose, which saint the well was for. A village that has gone far enough is simply left, its people gone to kin or gone up onto the downs. The ring of such villages has widened by some miles since the Crowned Years. The Dry Market [f61] was the largest place it has reached. The Meldrin law that no one ploughs the downs is older than the kings and has no reason given.

On the Brow itself [f65] no wind blows and no snow lies. The ground is warm. There is weather all round it and none on it.

## The carriers

Walkers who sing lists of names have crossed this land in every age: the rolls of Meldry [f27] note list-singers in 1,850 BA. The line that comes now calls itself the Venn. Its forebears set out 1,100 BA [r-0168] from the far country of plane entry [p01], in the north, a thousand years' walk away, and no one alive among them has seen it. They carry its names, and names taken up on the way. They reached this land about 95 BA and last passed through 56 BA [r-0170].

Their way in, by roll [r-0169]: over the winter ice of the Cold Sea [r30] to the shore under the Rimeward Range [r01], over the range at the thaw, down the Rimewater [f01] and across the Windrow Steppe [r06] to Threeways [f23], along the Meld Road [f42] to Meldry [f27] and Tollford [f29], then through the Ilwemere [r23] with Ilwe guides to the outer bars, where coasters of the Vessar Coast [r22] take them off southward, toward the country under the star of plane entry [p02]. They sing at every place they sleep, ask the names of the places round it, and sing those back.

Among the names they carry from the far country are the names in the sealed common past, as places, and nothing of what was done there.

## Travelers from afar

Few but known (the Night 1 result of 4).

- 200 BA: a rope-maker from the south, out of the country under the star of plane entry [p02], who wintered with the Ferrow and left them the stranger's hitch, the knot their debt-cords close with.
- 140 BA: a seed-seller, from the same country, who sold the towns of the Vessar Coast [r22] the wheat they now call true seed.
- 61 BA: a ship with three-cornered sails, from the west, out of the country under the star of plane entry [p03], put a crew of forty ashore on the Coldwater Coast [r09] to cut timber and did not come back for them. Their shipmaster ashore was Corin Dast. Their grandchildren, some two hundred, live at Lastlanding [f72] and keep a watch fire. They brought a way of building hulls with overlapped planks and a sail that works to windward, and a sea-chest that no one but the eldest opens.
- 56 BA: a woman who came in with the Venn and stayed at Tollford [f29], Sennah, who carried a bundle of debt-sticks from the far north and said she had come to collect from whoever kept the door. She found no one to pay her and died there in 20 BA. The toll-captain's house keeps the sticks.
- 23 BA: a grinder of glass, Orrick, from the west, out of the same country as the ship. He walked twenty years of the way and came the last of it by coaster to Vessa [f30]. He is old now and lives at Meldry [f27], where the king's clerks think him a toy-maker. He carries a sighting tube with ground glasses, a water level, and a way of drawing ground to scale on a board. He knows how each is made and that none can be made here without a year of failures first.

## The non-human peoples

Two, both few (the Night 1 result of 2).

- The Varrun [in the Varrun Heights [r02], r02]: came over the ice about 5,300 BA. They have kept two bargains with a realm that is gone, to the letter, for two thousand years, and count that as ordinary.
- The Ilwe [in the Ilwemere [r23], r23]: raised by this ground after the last fall. Their tale of a harbor at the old mouth is Old Emmer, which they place wrongly at the river's mouth; it stood at the narrows.

The other kinds the pools named live only as stories: a people under the ice, small exact traders at the wood's edge, a half-beast people, a people kept by their animals.

## Where the oldest ground shows its age

Under a mature star the land's thinning shows in the oldest and most worked places and nowhere else.

- The bare circle and the ground about the School at Ombrel [f66]: worked for seven thousand years.
- The villages of the Meldrin Vale [r14] nearest the Sleeper's Downs [r15].
- The upper cellars of the Elder Granary [u05]: seed kept there comes up samely, a little more each year. The tally-priests of Orrenmark [f26] keep the seed corn there because it is the driest place in the town.
- The vaults of the Drowned Quay [u06].

Everywhere else the land is as rich in kinds as it has ever been.

## The night of the arrival

On one night, at first light where each stood, the gods of this telling came down, each in its own country and its own season. That night a stone fell on the Windrow Steppe [r06], warm, blue-grey and heavy for its size: the Fallen Stone [f64]. No one saw it fall. It lies in the grass in godless country.

## What each people tells, against what is so

The tellings are in each people's record under `What the old tell`. The right-hand side is here only, and is given to no agent that plays a people.

The Orvanni:
- They say: Kes Orvan [f21] was built after three killing winters, eight grandfathers back. What is so: About right: some 240 years.
- They say: The Roofless Hall [f62] was a king's hall, and the king was taken up through the roof for his pride, which is why no oath is sworn there. What is so: Corvane rose from it in 1,790 BA; no punishment, and no king of theirs.
- They say: The Rime Bones [f56] are the Sky's first herd, frozen where they stood. What is so: Bones of the first country's great beasts.
- They say: West on the cold shore there are people who keep a fire burning toward the sea and speak wrongly. The old say they are drowned men who do not know it. What is so: Living people, at Lastlanding [f72], since 61 BA.
- They say: Singers who walk came down the Rimewater [f01] in their grandfathers' time, sang all night, and asked the names of the camps. The old remember being frightened and not harmed. What is so: The Venn, 56 BA.
- They say: They call the world the Under-Sky, and the star the Count-Keeper. What is so: Their names.

The Ferrow:
- They say: They have no founding. They say they have always been on the water. What is so: A way of living that people joined; true enough.
- They say: The knot their debt-cords close with was taught by the river itself, in the shape of a man who wintered with them. What is so: A rope-maker from over the sea, 200 BA.
- They say: The Capped Spring [f55] was shut by a jealous king so that no one could drink without paying. What is so: Capped in 1,720 BA by Ilmar, who had given itself to it, so that no one would spend it.
- They say: The dry bed beside the Dry Road [f44] is where the river called in a loan and took a whole ford. What is so: The river moved; the ford is the one in mystery S06.
- They say: They call the world the Lending, and the star the Lender's Lamp. What is so: Their names.

The Fordfolk:
- They say: The three roads were laid by one king in one summer. What is so: By Corvane's gangs, over sixty years.
- They say: The old town at the crossing burned because it refused a stranger the way. What is so: Burned by the hill clans under Sollis Carn, 900 BA.
- They say: The Barrow Door [f52] is a king's grave, and the hill clans guard it for the gold. What is so: A prison; no gold and no king.
- They say: Singers of lists came through Threeways [f23] when the oldest now living were children, sang for nine nights, and asked the name of every band at the gathering. The saying is that they come when the roads are about to change hands. What is so: The Venn, 56 BA; the saying is only a saying.
- They say: They call the world the Ways, and the star the Far Fire. What is so: Their names.

The Quayfolk:
- They say: There is an older harbor under their own, drowned because its queen shut the door on a guest. What is so: Old Emmer [u06]; Mereth lowered the bar, 4,310 BA; no guest came into it.
- They say: Their own forebears built the wall. What is so: Built 1,250 BA on Old Emmer's sea wall.
- They say: The Strand Bones [f57] are a whale-king's. What is so: Land beasts of the first country.
- They say: A fisher called Peddar Lowe dived to the old quay three times in one summer and came up not knowing his own ward. His boat is the Diver's boat still. Boys are told this to keep them out of the water under the wall. What is so: True, 88 BA.
- They say: They call the world the Landward, and the star the Window Lamp. What is so: Their names.

The Reedwain:
- They say: The Old Tollhouse [f60] was kept by a just toll-taker who walked away sooner than cheat. What is so: Harl Bessin, 420 BA; he walked away unpaid.
- They say: The Door of Terms [f53] has the first bargain ever made cut on it, and whoever reads the whole of it owes nothing to anyone. What is so: The realm's law; reading it pays no debt.
- They say: The Dry Market [f61] is the market that forgot its debts. They pass it each year and do not stop. What is so: Emptied 610 BA by the reach of the thing at the Brow.
- They say: A woman came with the list-singers in the old people's youth and stayed at Tollford [f29], saying she had come to collect from whoever kept the door. No one paid her. Some of the old hold that the debt is still open and that the road owes it. What is so: Sennah, 56 to 20 BA.
- They say: They call the world the Circuit, and the star the Fixed Weight. What is so: Their names.

The Askeld:
- They say: Kellgarth [f25] was walled in the time of the first Lawsayer, five hundred years ago. What is so: About 700 BA.
- They say: The Long Mound [f58] is where the Lead Animal first lay down. What is so: Bones of the first country's great beasts, older than the song.
- They say: The downs to the north-east, the Sleeper's Downs [r15], the old one asleep, are the mother of all herds, who will get up when the count comes right. What is so: The thing at the Brow; no herd's mother.
- They say: The salt has always come from the south coast and the iron from the roads, and both were cheaper in their grandfathers' day. What is so: True.
- They say: They call the world the Pasture, and the star the Lead Star. What is so: Their names.

The Orrenfolk:
- They say: The harvest-mother of the Worn Altar [f59] was let go by ungrateful forebears, and left. What is so: Hathra rose in 1,930 BA; the tithe was kept to her three hundred years after.
- They say: The great cellars under Orrenmark [f26] were dug by her in one night. What is so: Dug by the Binwrights about 3,000 BA.
- They say: A full bin is "a karr". What is so: Oddo Karr, bin-master; no one knows it was a man.
- They say: The Ferry-Wardens' charter was given by the river's keeper. What is so: The realm's; its words are on the Door of Terms [f53].
- They say: Far west on the black soil is a hall the first people left because a word lived in it. What is so: The Hollow Hall [f63]; left about 3,000 BA for thin wheat; the word came later.
- They say: They call the world the Fields, and the star the Seed-star. What is so: Their names.

The Varrun:
- They say: They came over the ice before any road. What is so: About 5,300 BA; more than they count.
- They say: They made a bargain at the gate, the road for peace and the door for silence, with one king of the plain. What is so: Two bargains, 2,050 and 1,780 BA, with Corvane and then Ilmar.
- They say: The Stane Door [f51] holds the dead of the kings of the plain. What is so: They do not know what it holds; Grusk kept his silence.
- They say: They call everything under the gate the Below, and the star the Nail. What is so: Their names.

The Ilwe:
- They say: The delta was smaller once, when there was a harbor at the old mouth. What is so: The delta has grown; the harbor was Old Emmer [u06], at the narrows, not the river's mouth.
- They say: Walkers who sang came to the marsh edge in their grandmothers' time and were guided through. They kept the tune. What is so: The Venn, 56 BA.
- They say: They were here before the dry people. What is so: True.
- They say: They call the world the Mouths, and the star the Still Float. What is so: Their names.

The Meldrin:
- They say: They count a hundred and forty kings, unbroken, back to a first king who paved the roads with his own hands. What is so: Corvane was no king of their line; the count has gaps and inventions.
- They say: The old kings were favored, and went to the star as a reward when they died. What is so: The Crowned rose; they did not die and were not kings of men.
- They say: No one ploughs the Sleeper's Downs [r15] because the first king sleeps under them. What is so: The thing at the Brow.
