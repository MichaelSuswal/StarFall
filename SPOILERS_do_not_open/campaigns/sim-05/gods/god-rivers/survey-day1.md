## Answer 1
Water that moves. A world where nothing is owned outright and everything is lent: the rain lends to the spring, the spring to the stream, the stream to the sea, and the sea pays it all back in cloud. A fair ledger, kept by no one. I would like it to feel like the hour before dawn on a ford, cold and clear, with something stirring that has not yet decided to be afraid of you.

## Answer 2
River country. Headwaters first, where a spring comes out of stone and nobody has yet put a name on it, then the long middle reaches with their braids and bends and fords. I will take the springs and the rivers and let others haggle over the rest. Give me land that is crossed, not land that is held.

## Answer 3
Four seasons, honest ones, with the turning not too sharp. A wet spring that fills the channels, a summer long enough to make a crop and short enough to make a person hurry, an autumn of low clear water, a winter that locks the edges in ice but leaves the middle running. I do not trust a world with no cold, since the cold is what teaches people to put something by. As for waking, I would take the thaw. Everything is a little loud then, and a stranger arriving unannounced looks like one more thing the melt brought down.

I wake into spring.

## Answer 4
Some of each, and I will say plainly why. Wild country is where a person can move without being asked their business. Old kingdoms are where there is something to trade with. A world with only the first has no customers, and a world with only the second has no room to leave. Let me have a road out of every border and a market at the end of it.

## Answer 5
Old, but not buried under itself. Thousands of years, enough that the riverbanks hold the memory of villages that moved on, and enough that a ford can have a story about why it is called what it is called. Not ruins under ruins. I have no taste for a world so full of the dead that the living must step over them to eat.

## Answer 6
Closer to the rumor than the wise-woman, but with a wise-woman in some valleys, not every one. Enough that a person who sits by a spring and listens might, once in a lifetime, hear a thing worth the sitting. Not so much that anyone leans on it. What is leaned on becomes owed.

## Answer 7
It should feel like listening. Not commanding, not forcing, but attending closely enough that the thing answers. The cost should be attention itself: the one who listens too long forgets to do their own talking, their own eating, their own turning toward the living. A fair price. It takes a little of you each time and gives you something you could not have bought elsewhere. I would not make it cruel, only exact.

## Answer 8
Noticeable, and no more. A god should be a thing one remembers having met, as one remembers a flood year. Not the one-eyed man in the kingdom of the blind, since the blind will always resent him, and not barely more than a sorcerer either, since then what was the descent for. A god should stand out the way a deep pool stands out in a shallow river. You can walk around it. You would be foolish not to know it is there.

## Answer 9
Familiar animals first, with a few that have no name. I would want the strange things to live in the waters and the margins, in the places between one thing and another: the reed beds, the mouths of caves where springs rise, the fogs on slow rivers. Let a child be able to describe a creature and the elders say, we do not call that anything, we only keep away.

## Answer 10
Floods, and what follows people home from the water. Hunger in the second year after a bad harvest. And being hunted by something that does not hurry. A good story to me is the one where the danger is patient and the person is not, and the person's own haste is what finally delivers them to it.

## Answer 11
Clean water. Not water, since there is plenty of that, but water that does not make the children sick, that comes up cold from the ground and tastes of nothing. And, as a second scarcity, trust: a person who will keep a bargain after the one who witnessed it has gone. Mortals will trade for the first and go without the second, and I think they suffer more for the second.

## Answer 12
Old roads that follow dry riverbeds, so that one can tell where the water used to be. And a sealed spring somewhere, a well with a stone laid over it by someone who knew what they were doing and did not leave a note. I would like to walk past it a hundred times before I wonder.

## Answer 13
A few, and not many. Peoples who live by the water and are not human, who look at us the way we look at herons: with some interest, no wish to be friends, and a long memory for being disturbed. I would want them to be strange in their habits and fair in their dealings, so that a person could make a bargain with them if the person understood the terms. I will not say more than that. Let them be found.

## Answer 14
Few. Travelers who came a long way and carry one odd thing each: a tool, a song, a way of tying a knot. Not a wave of strangers with machines, but a single stranger at a fire with a thing in a bag no one can quite name. I would pay a good price to sit across from one of them and trade questions.

## Answer 15
Face to face only. A pull felt at any distance is a leash, and I resist those. Let a god be able to come into a room and know that the other is not mortal, the way one knows a coin is real before it is bitten, and let it be possible, with some effort, to hide from that knowing. A meeting should be a meeting. Nobody should be located without being met.

## Answer 16
One body. I have seen what happens to anything that tries to be in two places: it is nowhere properly, and it argues with itself over which place is owed the better half. If a god wants to be in more than one place, let it cost something that cannot be paid back quickly. A piece of itself left behind, say, and not a piece it gets to reclaim by wishing.

## Answer 17
One who is good at asking, and willing to be wrong in front of others. A ferryman, a midwife, a weigher of grain. Someone with no great gift, who nonetheless ends up at the center of things because everyone has, at some point, owed them a small favor. I would watch such a person closely, and I would be careful, in watching, not to grow fond.

## Answer 18
A companion, I suppose, if the world were generous, though I would be a poor buyer. Failing that, a place: a spring that no god has used, whose water does something none of us can name. Or the truth about why we fall at all. I would give up a good deal for that one, and I would not say how much until I had seen the price asked.
