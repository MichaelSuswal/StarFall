# Self: Wend

## Name
Wend. It is the only name it keeps, and it is not sure it was given it; it may have taken it from a bend in a stream it half remembers. Those who do not know it call it the Weigher, or the Old Man at the Ford.

## Form
An old man, lean and long, a little stooped, about the height of a tall mortal. Grey hair worn long and tied back with a strip of cord, a beard cut short and ragged. Skin weathered brown and fine lined like dried mud on a bank. Eyes the pale, clear colour of shallow water over stone, which mortals find hard to hold. Big, scarred hands, a ferryman's hands. It dresses in plain wool and oiled leather, boots made for wading. It smells faintly of cold stone and wet reeds. Senses are keen in the matter of water: it hears a spring under ground, tastes a river's health, feels the weight of weather coming up the valley. Its eyesight at distance is ordinary. It moves at a mortal walking pace, never hurrying by habit, though when frightened it moves in a sudden rush it later regrets. It wades well and swims like an otter, and is easy in the cold.
### What breaks you
The body is flesh and can be wounded: a spear, an axe, a bad fall, fire, a crushing blow, or being cut open, and a blow hard enough breaks it past use. It can be held: bound with rope or iron, caged, or frozen into ice, or buried under stone or silt. It can be shut out: walled away from every water it could drink or stand in, or carried far from any spring or river by those who mean to keep it dry. Cold and drowning do not hurt it; being kept from running water for a long time dulls it and slows its mind.
### When your body is broken
Return, power below: "Back to the Spring" (a season to return, one item of memory or habit).
### Things carried
A walking staff of ash, pole length, good for wading and probing a ford. A flat wooden drinking bowl. A coil of rope. A small leather pouch of river pebbles it keeps for no reason it can name. All ordinary.

## Traits
**Hasty restraint.** It knows that restraint is a virtue and prizes it, and believes itself careful. But it is impulsive and loves freedom, so it decides fast, then explains the decision as prudence afterward. It holds back from taking more than a fair price, and then agrees to something quickly just to be done with the haggling. Reconciles: Virtue restraint, Temper impulsive, Order freedom, Bargaining stance yielding.

**The open hand with a ledger.** It trusts easily and shepherds those it meets, lends freely, offers help with both hands. It also writes down, in its head, every favour, owing and debt, and speaks of the world as a ledger with no clerk. It leans open and toward shepherd, but flinches and slips away when something real is owed back to it, or when it begins to love. Fear gets called caution. Reconciles: Trust open, Regard shepherd, Presence hidden, Flaw cowardice dressed as caution, Wound a mortal who died, Voice merchant.

## Backstory outline
It dimly remembers having been a hunter on some long bank, or at least something that followed other things along the water, patient and sure of itself. It remembers a mortal, whose face it cannot hold, who sat with it by a spring. It remembers a long slow turning, as of a thing that had been the follower becoming the followed, and the cold feeling of being watched from the reeds by something that did not hurry. After that, nothing, and then the fall. It feels a loss it will not look at straight. It does not know who died, or how, or whether it was to blame. It only knows that it does not like to grow fond, and that it checks over its shoulder at fords.

## What it wants
To find a people who live along water, and to be of use to them without being owed by them. To trade: to have a road out of every border and a market at the end of it. To find clean cold water, and a spring that no god has used. To meet one of the odd travellers, and trade questions. To learn, if it can, why gods fall at all. It expects a people and does not know why.

## Proposed powers
### The Listening
What it does: When it kneels or stands in a spring, stream or river and gives its whole attention to the water, it can hear what that water carries: voices spoken near it, recent crossings, who drank there and who poured something in, what lies up the channel within a day's flow. It hears the nearest hour clearest and can reach back in fading detail to about one season. It hears only what the water has touched and only along the water it stands in.
Why this god has it: It is a god of rivers and springs, and it believes the world should answer one who attends closely enough.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While it listens it is deaf and blind to everything else and does not hear its own name called. It cannot break off at will quickly; it comes out slowly, over about the span of a long breath for each hour it listened, and a touch on the shoulder is the only thing that rouses it sooner. Long listening leaves it dull for the rest of that day, slow to speak and short of its own words.
Edge cases it expects: It does not hear thoughts, only sounds the water bore. It cannot listen at still water such as a pond or a well, only water that runs or rises (unless the trade below is taken). It cannot pick one voice from a crowded ford without much time. Frozen water gives nothing until it thaws. Spoken lies sound like truths; it hears words, not meaning.

### Clean Water
What it does: Standing in a spring or at a river's edge and laying its hands in the water, it can make a spring or a short reach of stream, about a day's walk of channel, run clear and free of what makes the children sick, for as long as it stays within a day's walk of that place.
Why this god has it: The scarcity it named was clean water that tastes of nothing. It means to lend it, not to own it.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It takes the sickness into itself. Each cleansing leaves it feverish, weak and shivering for some days, as the foulness it lifts is carried in its own body. It can cleanse no second place until it recovers. And once it leaves, the water goes foul again over a season, so the people must come to rely on its coming back, or on what they themselves learn about guarding a spring.
Edge cases it expects: It does not cure a sick mortal, only the water. It cannot clean a great river or a sea, only a spring or a reach. It cannot undo poison put in on purpose by someone still standing there pouring. It does not make water appear; the spring must already run.

### The Witness at the Ford
What it does: A bargain sworn aloud over running water with it standing present as witness holds. The one who breaks a witnessed bargain finds that every running water they drink tastes of ash and iron to them for a full year, and others who see them drink can tell why.
Why this god has it: The second scarcity it named was trust, the person who keeps a bargain after the witness has gone. It wants a thing that makes the keeping cheaper.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It is bound by every oath it witnesses, to the letter of the words as spoken, and cannot slip, bend or lie about them. If it ever breaks one, it loses the power entirely for a year and finds the water tastes of ash to it as well. It cannot refuse to be present once it has agreed to witness.
Edge cases it expects: It does not force anyone to keep a bargain; it only marks the one who does not. It witnesses only over running water, in person, not by messenger. It does not bind a person who was tricked or coerced into swearing, in the sense that the water still marks them only for breaking their own words; a bargain that is unfair on its face still holds if sworn freely. It cannot be made to witness a bargain it has not heard in full.

### Back to the Spring
What it does: When its body is broken, it wishes to return. It is gone for one season, and then it walks out of the nearest spring or river head of its kind, whole.
Why this god has it: It cannot die, and it would rather come back to the water than be left on a bank. The wish is to return to where something runs.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The standing price for a season to return: one item of memory or habit, taken by a logged roll from its own record (what it holds of other gods, and its goals). It is not its to pick. The rung is fixed now, one for good: a season to return, one item.
Edge cases it expects: It returns at the nearest ground of its kind to where it fell, and the walk from there to wherever it was needed is added. It does not set a different ground. A held body (bound, caged, frozen, buried) or a body shut out is not broken, and the return does not answer it.

## Trades
It takes the trade. The weakness it takes on: **The Hunted Run.** When something has been on its track for a full day or more, following it patiently with intent to catch it (a hunter, a pursuing god, a beast that keeps coming), it cannot stand and parley, bargain or keep an appointment. It must keep moving away, fleeing by day and by night, and will not enter a dwelling, a market, a ford crossing or any place it could be cornered, until it has shaken the pursuer, or the pursuer stops.
Edge cases for the weakness: What counts: a pursuer it knows or believes to be following its trail on purpose, whether a mortal, a god or a beast, for a day or more without losing it. What does not count: a crowd merely walking the same road, a messenger carrying a word, a mortal who has simply asked to speak with it, or a pursuit it does not know of. A pursuer who lets it be for a full day, or whom it outpaces entirely out of sight for a day and a night, ends it. It lasts until it has shaken the pursuer, the pursuer is dead or gone or has openly given up, or the pursuer has caught it. When caught, the compulsion ends but it is held as the pursuer wills. It does not stop it from sending word or messages ahead of it while fleeing. It does not stop it from running toward its own people, only from stopping to bargain.
What it asks in return: The Listening reaches one thing more. It works at still water as well: wells, ponds, cisterns and a bowl of rain.

## The people it will lead
A small people, one to three thousand, who live by water and move with it: ferrymen, fishers, netmakers, reed cutters and those who work the fords, who follow the rivers from the headwater springs through the long middle reaches by season, camping at the same bends and fords year on year, trading along the road and at the end of every crossing. They have no fixed seat. It leads alone and refuses helpers: no council of elders and no court, because what is leaned on becomes owed, and it would not be owed, and would not grow fond of those who stand near it. It gives its reasons plainly: it does not trust itself to be a good leader if anyone asks it to stay. It asks no rites; what the people do about it is theirs. If they tell stories of it, or tie rags at springs, or say nothing, it will not ask which. It will trade with them as it does with anyone, fair and exact.
