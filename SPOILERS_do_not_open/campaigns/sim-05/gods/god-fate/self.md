# Self: Skerrit

## Name
Skerrit. It dimly holds the name as the sound a greater thing once made when calling it to heel, and it keeps the name anyway, because a name is a mark on an account. Those who do not know it will call it the Factor, or the Tally-beast.

## Form
A small beast, about the size of a large marten: long in the body, short in the leg, brindled grey and rust, with a ruff of iridescent green and blue hair at the neck and shoulders that shimmers like a fly's back. Narrow face, quick hands with a thumb on each forepaw, a bare tail that it carries curled. Its eyes are round and pale gold, with dark pips in them like the marks on dice. Its voice is dry and quick, a merchant's voice, and it sounds larger than it looks.
Senses: sharp sight and hearing, and a very fine sense of smell, best for rot, damp, sickness and spoiled food, which it reads as a trader reads a sample. It moves on four legs at a quick scurrying pace, climbs well, and can sit on a shoulder or a pack frame. It can stand on its hind legs to deal with people at table height. It cannot lift more than its size allows.
### What breaks you
Anything that can break a small animal body: a blow, a crushing weight, a fall onto stone, a blade, fire, drowning held under, a trap or snare, a cage that holds it (held, not broken). It can be shut out by a warded door or a sealed vessel it cannot pass. It cannot die. A broken body is a body struck beyond use, and it is gone from the place until it returns.
### When your body is broken
Return by the power "The Return to the Marsh Edge" below.
### Things carried
A small set of brass scales and a pouch of weights, hung from a cord at its neck, ordinary and true. The weights are honest ones and nothing more than ordinary objects. It carries nothing else.

## Traits
1. Exact and sharp-tongued, a beast that keeps every account. This reconciles its hard lean toward law (it wants every limit written exactly), its measured wary bargaining, its merchant's voice, and its domain of fate and chance, which it treats as a market where nothing is free and every price is posted. It is also the shape its virtue of justice takes: a fair trade, kept to the letter, owed to the small as much as the great.
2. Quick to leap and proud of it, a beast that makes the offer before it has finished weighing. This reconciles its hard lean toward impulsive, its lean toward breaker, its flaw of pride, and its wound of having fled a greater thing. It breaks a stalled bargain, a bad lock or a bad custom with one decisive act, and then must keep the terms of what it has done. The pride is the cost of the speed: it cannot bear to be thought cheated, and so it will pay too much to avoid saying it was.

## Backstory outline
It dimly remembers having been a servant, a counter of things for something very large that kept the great ledger and did not look down. It remembers the work: weights and tallies, small and exact. It remembers a morning when it left, ran, and did not look back, and it does not know whether it was dismissed, whether it stole something, or whether it was afraid. It feels the wound as a missing weight in the pan: when it sets its scales level, one side seems always to want something more. It cannot say what is owed, or by whom. It knows only that it will not serve again, and that it is, nearly, a thing that wishes to read its own page.

## What it wants
It arrives knowing only that it expects a people to lead. It wants first to find that people and learn what they are owed and what they owe, and to meet them with terms posted. It wants a moving people on a crossing country of roads, rivers and marsh edges, who can trade honestly. It wants honest weights and kept promises to be the rare goods it supplies. It wants to see the old tollhouses, sealed doors and ledgers, and to meet a stranger who arrives in debt. It wants no master on the stair, and it wants, without knowing why, to read its own page.

## Proposed powers
### The Weighing
What it does: When a bargain is spoken aloud in its hearing, with its terms stated and each party saying yes, and the god holds its scales or has its paws on the table, it learns for each party, as a plain yes or no, whether that party means to keep the terms as stated. It learns this only for the terms as spoken, and only for parties within arm's reach of its scales or within the same room. It does not tell it why, or whether the party can keep them, or anything else about the mind. A party who does not speak the bargain aloud is not weighed. The god may let the answer be seen or not.
Why this god has it: Fate and chance, and justice. A stranger's word that holds is the scarcest good, and it is the one who tells whether the word will hold.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): Any term the god itself speaks in a weighed bargain binds it, to the letter, as if it were weighed itself. If it says yes to a term, it cannot knowingly break it, and it cannot by any trick of wording avoid a term it understood when it spoke. It loses the freedom of the quick lie in any bargain and it loses its easy slip of the tongue. If it speaks a term and keeps its head down on the meaning, the meaning it understood binds it.
Edge cases it expects: A bargain made by signs or writing, with no spoken terms, is not weighed. A bargain in a tongue it does not understand is weighed only by the sound of assent, and it learns that the party said yes and nothing of the terms; the term it does not understand does not bind it. A party who lies to themselves is weighed by what they believe. A party who intends to keep a term and later breaks it was weighed true at the time. A god (another god) in the bargain is weighed too, if close enough, but it learns only yes or no. The terms bind it only when it spoke them or assented to them in the weighed bargain, not when another party spoke them without its assent.

### The Bill
What it does: A mortal who stands before it and asks, and states aloud the price in advance, can receive from it one small working of fate or chance, now: a throw that falls well, a ford that holds, a sickness that turns, a lost thing found, a day of fair weather on one road, a door that opens, a blade that misses, a debt that is forgiven. The working is a single turn of chance within what chance could do, not a miracle, and it cannot make the impossible happen. The price is named by the mortal and accepted by the god before the working, and it is collected when it falls due, exactly: a year of luck, a tooth, a name, a season of sleep, a promise of labour, a gift of goods. The god cannot collect more than was named, and the mortal cannot refuse it later. The bill is written on the god's record and is exact whether or not the mortal remembers it. There is no working without a bill and no hidden charge.
Why this god has it: Fate and chance, and the virtue of justice. Its Day 1 answer was that magic should feel like borrowing. It is a creditor of luck.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While any bill it has written remains unpaid and past due, the god cannot move faster than a slow walk and cannot leap, climb or scurry, and its pace is that of a heavy old man. When the bill is paid or forgiven the pace returns. Each bill unpaid at once slows it, and the slowing does not stack beyond the slow walk.
Edge cases it expects: A bill falls due when the mortal sets it or at the next turn of a season if none was set. A bill the mortal cannot pay is still owed; the god may forgive it, and then it is struck from the account at no cost. A mortal who dies owing leaves the debt unpaid, and it binds the heirs only if they agreed to it aloud. The god cannot grant a working to a mortal who does not ask, and it cannot grant a working for a price it does not accept. A working cannot hurt a person beyond what chance could do, cannot raise the dead, cannot change a past act, and cannot reach beyond one turn of chance per bill. A bill can be paid early. A bill is a power used only on mortals who agree, and not on other gods.

### Clean Rot
What it does: By touch, it can draw rot or sickness out of one thing: a store of grain, a well, a wound, a sick person, a spoiled barrel, a rotting foot. The thing is made clean, as it was before the rot began, and the rot passes into the god's own body. Seen in the world it is a medicine: the god takes the harm into itself so that others do not.
Why this god has it: Its seed is the lord of flies and rot who is also medicine. The slow danger that comes in with the grain and water is the danger it knows best, and it would be a merchant who keeps its own goods sound.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): What it draws out, it carries. For a season after each working it is sick with the rot it took: its coat falls patchy, its limbs ache, and flies gather about it and follow it, and every mortal near it can see the flies and smell the sickness. It cannot hide the flies, and it cannot hide the smell, and it cannot work a second Clean Rot while the first is still on it. The sickness cannot kill it.
Edge cases it expects: It cleans one thing per working: one barrel, one well, one body. It cannot cure a hurt that is not rot or sickness (a broken bone, an old age, a poison that is not a rot, a hunger). It cannot clean a land, a river, a plague, or a whole granary at once. If the rot is a plague, it takes the plague out of one person and the plague remains in the others. The season it carries the sickness begins at the working and ends a season later by the turn of the year, whichever comes first.

### The Return to the Marsh Edge
What it does: When its body is broken, it wishes this: that it return to the world, not at once, but as a debt coming due. After a season it comes back to a whole body at the nearest marsh edge, a place of wet and uncertain ground, a ford or a reed bed, to the place where it fell. The walk from that ground to wherever it was needed is added to the delay.
The price on the standing scale: a season to return, which costs one item of memory or habit, taken by a logged roll from its own record, what it holds about other gods and its goals. The rung is fixed now, for good: a season. It does not choose the rung at each return. A ground it sets now: it asks for no ground of its own, and takes the nearest marsh edge.
Why this god has it: It fled a greater thing once, and it will not be called back, and it will not be lost. It would rather return slowly and pay in memory than return fast and owe more.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The memory item it loses, and the season in which its people have no god among them.
Edge cases it expects: The return is not a way out of a held or shut body. A body that is caged, bound or shut out is not broken, and the return does not answer it. The memory item is not its to choose.

## Trades
It takes one weakness and asks one improvement.
Weakness: It cannot refuse to hear a bargain offered in the proper form. When a mortal stands before it, states the terms aloud and the price, and offers a hand, the god must hear the whole offer to the end and weigh it, and it may refuse the offer but may not walk away, leap off or silence the speaker before the offer is finished. 
Edge cases: It counts only when the mortal states both the terms and the price aloud and offers a hand or palm. It does not count against another god, against a mortal who speaks only a demand, or against a mortal who speaks in a tongue it cannot understand. It lasts for the whole of the world, never ends, and ends only if the god loses its ears or its hearing. It is not a duty to accept. The god may refuse after it has heard.
Improvement asked: that The Weighing also tells it, for each party, whether the party can in truth keep the terms as stated, a plain yes or no, not why. This is one thing more that the same power reaches.

## The people it will lead
A moving people of about one to three thousand, with no fixed seat, who cross a country of rivers, ford towns, marsh edges and harbors as caravan folk and boatmen and carters do. They are ruled by one mortal merchant-prince who answers to the god and rules for it by bargain, debt, toll and trade, not by decree. The prince holds the account books, sets the terms of passage and the tolls, and keeps the people's word good, and the god weighs the prince's own bargains. Worship is by household and village shrines, kept by whoever keeps them, with no order of priests: a scale-pan and a weight set on a shelf, a mark chalked on a door, a coin left at a ford, so that every household is its own temple. I lean toward the leans I was given and do not depart from them. The people are not a conquering people, and they are a people who are known by their honest weights. Their helpers are settled on Night 3.
