# People: the Reedwain, whose home is the Long Account

## Who they are
A people of the road and the river bank: carters, boatmen, ferrymen, toll collectors and small traders, who cross a country of fords, marsh edges and harbors with ox carts and flat reed boats, carrying salt, grain, cloth, hides and word. They keep their own accounts and have no fixed seat. They call themselves the Reedwain, for the wains they live in and the reeds they cut and bind. They call their home the Long Account, meaning the circuit of ford, road and market they travel each year and the book that records it. The name of the home is theirs and places nothing: it is a road they keep, not a spot on a map. They are one people of mixed blood, with some families who joined from the old river towns and some who joined from the marsh edges. They are not warriors and not rich. They are known, where they are known, for honest weights.

## How many
About eighteen hundred, in roughly three hundred and fifty households, with a few hundred oxen and a few dozen flat boats. This is within the small range and I ask nothing beyond it.

## How they live
They travel in a loose caravan in autumn, spring and summer, and winter at a marsh edge or ford town in a camp of wains drawn into a ring, the closed ledger of the year. They trade at fords and tollhouses, carry goods for a fee, and mend and make on the road: rope, reed mats, baskets, cart gear, salted fish, hides. Each household holds a wain or a share in a boat. Children ride in the wains and learn weights, tallies and the names of the road before they learn anything else. Days are for hauling, trading, counting and mending. Evenings are for settling accounts at the household fire.

## Their code
What is always so of them: a weight is true, a tally is split and each half kept, and a spoken bargain is said aloud before a witness. They will not use a false weight, and they will not break a split tally. They will not turn a debtor out in winter. They honor a kept word above a sharp one, a debt paid above a debt forgiven, and the witness who cannot be bought. They will not sell a person, and they will not take what is left at a roadside shrine. These are their custom and their pride, set as who they are. They promise nothing of what any one of them will do under pressure.

## Skills and culture
Weights and measures, tallying with knotted cords and split sticks, ox handling, cart and boat building from reed, withy and green wood, rope and net work, salting and drying, fording and reading wet ground, haggling, and remembering a road by its landmarks. They have a trade tongue that carries across the rivers and a gift for picking up the speech of whoever they meet. Their songs are lists of fords and debts. They count the year in four reckonings: spring loans, summer road, autumn reckoning, winter closed ledger. These are ordinary culture and free.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None.

## Their magic
None among them. They know of wise women in some valleys and hold them at a respectful distance, as they would a hard but fair creditor.

## What they believe about gods
They have not had a god, and they say so plainly. They hold that everything is owed to something: the river is owed its due when it rises, the land is owed a rest, the dead are owed a settled account. At a grave they say "the account is closed, and the road forgives what is left." Over a bad harvest they say "the year's debt has been called, and we pay it in hunger." When the water comes up in the night they say "the river is collecting," and they move the wains uphill and pay the bank a handful of grain when it is over. They believe that a stranger's word is the rarest thing on the road. They are waiting for no god, and they will weigh one as they weigh any stranger. This is their belief and not a fact about the world.

## Rule and worship
Rule: a mortal merchant-prince, one ruler who answers to the god and rules for it. They call the office the Prince of Accounts. The present holder is Maren Dask, a woman near sixty, a carter's daughter who rose by years of kept tallies, honest by custom and oath, known for exactness and for a dry temper. I set who she is and say nothing of what she will do under pressure. She rules by bargain, debt, toll and trade, and not by decree: terms of passage are agreed, tolls are posted, debts are written, and she sets the tolls and the order of the road with the wain masters' consent.
Worship: household and village shrines, kept by whoever keeps them, with no order of priests. Each household keeps a shelf with a scale pan, a weight and a split tally stick, and chalks a mark on its door. A coin or a pinch of grain is left at a ford. At a camp, a shelf is set at the ring's gate. The shrines were their custom before I came; they honor the true weight and the kept word, and not me by name until they learn of me. No tithe, no temple, no priest. This is within the leans I was given.

## Hierarchy
Who advises: the Reckoner, a keeper of the common account, and the wain masters, each of whom speaks for a score or so of households.
Who rules under me: the Prince of Accounts.
Who settles disputes: the Bench of Weights, three elders chosen from the wain masters by lot each autumn reckoning, who sit at a table with a true scale and hear both parties aloud and settle the matter by the posted terms or, where there are none, by the custom of the road. A party may appeal to the Prince of Accounts, whose word on custom is final among them. A dispute with outsiders is settled by bargain at the nearest ford or tollhouse, with witnesses.

## Helpers
The Prince of Accounts: rules as above and holds the Long Account.
The Reckoner: keeps the common book of tallies, cords and wax boards in the Account Wain, and records every debt and every bill.
The Wain Masters: one for about every twenty households, who set the order of march, the camp ring, and the watch.
The Ford Wardens: a small number who go ahead to read the water, post tolls and make terms at crossings.
The Shrine Shelf keepers: whoever in a household keeps its shelf; no office.
A small paid guard: about thirty, carters and boatmen who take a turn, paid in grain and coin from the common stores, ordinary and not priced.
How orders travel: from my mouth to the Prince of Accounts, or to the Reckoner if she is away, then to the wain masters at the evening reckoning, who carry it to each household's fire, with the Ford Wardens carrying it ahead to the road. An order reaches the last household in about two days of the camp's march, and a day when they are ringed in winter.

## How the god meets them
They do not know me, and they have not been waiting for me. The autumn caravan has come to the ford and stopped by the ruined tollhouse to settle accounts before the road is closed for the season, and carts are on the road. The Prince of Accounts, the Reckoner, the wain masters, the Ford Wardens, the Bench of Weights and the guard stand already under their own law, held by people who have never heard of me. None of those offices is mine from the first hour. I come as a stranger, a small beast with scales at its neck and a merchant's tongue. I will meet them as one meets any trader at a ford: I post my terms, say my price, and let them weigh me. I ask nothing of the land or its past, and I say of the ruined tollhouse only that I wish to know who held it and what is owed there, as my wish and not as a fact.

## Questions for you
1. Do your own people ask them of you, and does your merchant-prince?
My people ask bills of me as any mortal may: each stands before me, asks, and names a price aloud for himself or herself. The Prince of Accounts may ask a bill for herself like any person, and I hope she will ask rarely, because she holds an office. She may not ask a bill for another person, and no one may ask for a household without that person standing and agreeing aloud. I wish no bill to be laid on a person who has not agreed. Because only seven bills may stand open at once and no mortal holds more than one, I ask the Prince of Accounts to keep the order in which people ask, by the Reckoner's book, so that the seven go to those in the most need and not to the loudest voice. That order is theirs to set under their own law, and I do not set it. The god accepts no price that the Reckoner records as unfair.
2. Where is their account kept, and who keeps it when you are a season away with flies about you?
The Long Account is kept in the Account Wain, a covered cart that travels at the middle of the caravan and camps at the middle of the ring. It holds the common book of knotted cords, split tally sticks and wax boards. The Reckoner keeps it, and the Prince of Accounts holds the key, which is a bar and not a lock. Each household keeps one half of its own split tally, and the Reckoner the other half, so the account stands in two places. When I am a season away, the Reckoner keeps it and writes into it each bill I have written and each price it names, so that my record is kept in their book as well as in my own, and I read it on my return. The flies that follow me when I have worked a Clean Rot stay with me and not with the Account Wain. The Reckoner keeps the account without me, and my bills do not wait for me to come back to be paid.
