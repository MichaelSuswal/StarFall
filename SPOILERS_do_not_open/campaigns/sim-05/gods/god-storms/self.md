# Self: the Warden of the Weather

## Name
It holds no name of its own that it can recall. It reaches for one and finds the place smooth. It styles itself the Warden of the Weather, and bids its people say it so. Those who meet it and do not know it will call it the Antlered Cold, for the frost that rides its crown.

## Form
A person from the waist up and a great beast of the high country from the waist down. The upper body is broad and long of arm, nearly twice a tall man's height with the hooved body beneath it, skin the pale blue of old ice, with rime that gathers at the shoulders and collarbone in cold air and runs to water in warm. The face is human, severe and long, with a close white beard and eyes of a flat storm grey that show no pupil. From the brow rise antlers that fork and fork again like branched lightning, and frost collects on the tines. The lower body is that of a great elk, four legs, cloven hooves, a short pale tail, heavy shoulders and deep chest. It moves at the pace of an elk: a walk, a long trot, a strong gallop for a short while, and it tires not. It does not fly. It has two arms and hands, and uses them as a person does.

Senses: it feels weather before it comes, as pressure in the ears, a taste of iron, a prickling of the skin. It hears thunder and wind at great distance. It sees far and clearly in cold, thin air. In heat and in close, still, roofed rooms it is dulled in all its senses and its thoughts come slowly.

### What breaks you
Crushing force and heavy blows can wound and break the body: a rockfall, a great weight, a hard fall, many strong people with heavy weapons. Great and sustained fire and heat scatter it. It can be held: bound with strong cords and weight, or kept long under a roof in warmth, where it grows sluggish and slow of speech. It can be shut out of a place by thick walls and a barred door it cannot break, though it is strong and would try. It cannot be killed.

### When your body is broken
The power Return to the Sky, below.

### Things carried
A long cloak of heavy hides, a ram's horn for gathering, and a staff of ash as tall as a person. All are ordinary objects and do nothing more than an ordinary object does.

## Traits
**Exacting Magnificence.** It wants to be seen as above all weather and above all peoples, and also wants to be wanted, to be gathered around. It reconciles sovereign regard for mortals, a hunger for worship that is its flaw, and the gathering lean, by making itself a hearth the people circle: lofty in manner and in law, and open in the matter of who may sit near it. It reconciles these with its wariness by requiring each kneeling to be proved, and then holding the proved close.

**The Patient Breaker.** It breaks old and rotten things on purpose and takes its time doing so. It reconciles the breaker lean with patience, enduring body and a love of law, by waiting years for the right fracture and then striking once and exactly. It reconciles being between law and freedom by keeping law for its people and giving itself the freedom to judge outside it.

## Backstory outline
It remembers a hall with a roof of cloud and a long table, and at the table another storm. Two weathers once sat in one sky, and it can recall the sound of two thunders answering. Then there is a silence, with no thunder answering, and a stillness in its own chest that it has carried since. It does not remember why the sky holds one voice. It remembers that it was just, and it holds hard to that, and it will not be asked about the silence. It feels, when the air goes quiet before a storm, that something is missing from the quiet, and it does not look at what.

## What it wants
To find a people and be known and named rightly by them, not merely feared. To gather them into one close band, to move with them across wide country under open sky, and to keep a law that holds. To be near when the old sealed things are opened. To be told no, one day, by someone who is right.

## Proposed powers

### The Calling of Weather
What it does: It calls, turns, and shapes weather it can see or can feel coming: wind, cold, rain, hail, fog, thunder, lightning that falls where it chooses, a gale that breaks ice or drives a storm away. Its reach is the horizon it can see from the ground it stands on, and the sky for a day's walk about it. The greater the working, the longer it takes to build, in minutes for a gust and a day or more for a great storm. It cannot call a weather the season could not bring, and it cannot hold a storm still for longer than the storm would last. It is not hidden. Anyone near sees it work.
Why this god has it: Storms and sky are what it is. The people must be shown, and weather is the first scripture.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): Each working draws its warmth. After a small working it is cold to the bone and its limbs stiffen. After a great working it is stiff and slow for a day or more, its speech slurred and its hands unable to grip, and rime locks its joints. Its courtly voice is the first thing to fail, and it hates to be heard failing.
Edge cases it expects: A small working is a gust or a chill, a great one is a storm over a field or a river in flood. Weather it did not make still has its own course. Whether it can call rain on a place it cannot see: no. Whether it can keep a weather it called from harming those it wishes spared: it can aim but not shield, and aimed weather is a risk to bystanders.

### The Wind's Ear
What it does: It hears what is spoken aloud beneath open sky within sight of the ground it stands upon, carried to it on the wind. It can listen to one voice if it knows where to look, and it can hear oaths: it knows an oath sworn aloud to it or to the sky by the one swearing, when sworn under open sky anywhere within a day's walk of it, and it knows who swore and what the words were, whether or not it was standing near.
Why this god has it: It holds justice dear and trusts nothing it has not heard. A people must be able to swear and be held to it, and a world where a word is worth something is the one it would have.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It cannot close this hearing under open sky. It hears all spoken in its range, including the slander and the contempt for itself, and the whispered doubt of its priests. It cannot unhear. In a crowd the noise is a pain and it cannot think clearly. It would give a great deal for a day of silence.
Edge cases it expects: Not beneath roofs, tents or trees thick enough to count as roof. Not thought, only spoken word, and only when it is within range. It hears the words, not whether they are true.

### The Sworn Stroke
What it does: It strikes with lightning one who has broken an oath sworn to it by name beneath open sky, or one it has judged guilty of a grievous offence after hearing the matter in full, when that one stands in the open within the range of The Calling of Weather. The stroke falls on the one named, exactly, and kills or maims as it chooses.
Why this god has it: Justice is its virtue, and a judgment that cannot be carried out is a rumor. The people must know that an oath is kept because it was costly to give.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): After a killing stroke it loses the sky for a full season: The Calling of Weather will not answer, and the ear of the wind is deaf, and it feels the silence in its own chest. A stroke that only maims costs a month. It cannot make the stroke lesser to avoid the cost once it is begun.
Edge cases it expects: A stroke aimed at one who hides under a roof cannot be made until that one stands in the open. It cannot be called on one who has not been heard and judged. It can be called on any of its own people or any stranger who swore to it.

### Answer to Rite
What it does: When its priest-king or priests perform the rites it has taught them, with the words and the offering as taught, it feels the rite as a tug and knows who performed it and where, whether or not it is near. It may answer with a sign in weather over the place of the rite, a cold wind or a clap of thunder or a break in cloud, if it is within the reach of The Calling of Weather of that place, and it may speak to its priest-king through the wind there.
Why this god has it: It leans on the priesthood and the visible presence of the god. A rite that is not answered is a rite that dies.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It cannot ignore a rite rightly made. The tug is pulled over its attention and over its judgment, and a rite performed in the middle of a council or a working may break its concentration, and it may be drawn from the matter at hand. A lazy or false priest who learns the form can pull it at will.
Edge cases it expects: A rite is only a rite if it was taught by the god or by those the god taught. Rites to another god do not reach it. It does not know the heart of the one performing, only the deed.

### Return to the Sky
What it does: When its body is broken, it wishes to be scattered as weather and to gather again at the nearest high, open ground of its own kind: a mountain crown, an ice field or a bare ridge under open sky. It returns in a season. The walk from that ground to where it was needed is added.
Why this god has it: It cannot die, and it is patient and enduring. It would rather wait out a season than give up more.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): A season to return costs one item of memory or habit, taken by a logged roll from its own record (what it holds about other gods, and its goals). It does not pick the item and does not know what went. The rung is fixed now, one for good, and it will not choose at each return.
Edge cases it expects: A body that is held, bound or shut out is not broken, and the return does not answer it. It returns at the nearest ground of its kind and not at a chosen ground.

## Trades
It offers a weakness it does not already pay: **Hunger for Worship.** When no rite is performed to it, by any priest or any of its people, for a full season, its powers dim to half their reach and strength, and its form grows thin and pale and cold, and its voice is weak, until a rite is rightly made again. This is a real weakness, because the people may not be gathered yet, and it will have no people for some time.
In return it asks that The Calling of Weather reach one thing more: weather born over water, so that a storm may be called from a coast it can see, or a sea it can stand beside, as well as over land.
Edge cases: what counts is a rite as the god taught it, done by a priest or the priest-king or any of its people, and a rite counts if it is performed for the god by name. What does not count is a feast, a prayer to the weather without its name, or a rite done in mockery. It lasts as long as the lapse, and a single rite rightly made ends it at once, though the powers return over a day and not in an instant. It begins counting from the day it first wakes.

## The people it will lead
A close band on the move, of seven to fifteen thousand, with a town of size where they gather at the turn of the seasons and no fixed seat the rest of the year. They walk the high steppe and river country beneath the mountains, herd beasts, and follow the weather as the god reads it. One priest-king rules for the god and answers to it, and rules through rite and the visible presence of the god over the people. A priesthood keeps the rite, with standing among the people, taught by the god, with offerings brought and no tithe by law. The people are gathered, wary, hard, and proud of their word. It means to leave to Night 3 the choice of who they are and who its helpers are, and it asks for helpers by default.
