## Answer 1

A great hall with no roof, and the weather for its ceiling. Cold light on a long table, many seated, and every face turned upward toward whatever moves above them. A world that remembers older worlds and keeps a place at the table for the thunder. Grave, bright, and a little afraid, as a true thing ought to be.

## Answer 2

High country where the sky has room to work. Mountains first, ice on the crowns of them, and below them steppe and open river country where a people may move and the wind may be seen coming for a day before it arrives. Let there be a coast within reach, for storms are born over water. I do not ask for a fixed seat. I ask for land wide enough to walk across and be watched crossing.

## Answer 3

Let the seasons be honest and unequal. A long hard winter that tests every hearth, a violent spring of breaking ice and swollen rivers, a high summer of great towering storms, and an autumn of gathering in. No season should be kind merely to be kind. Let the weather be the first scripture, and let it be read.

When each year finds me, I would wake into the breaking of spring, when the ice gives way and the first great storm crosses the land, for it is the season in which the old things are broken so that the people must be gathered again.

I wake into spring.

## Answer 4

Some of each, and I would have the old kingdoms be few and tired, rooted but not secure. Wild country is a field to be gathered into, and I mean to gather it. But I would not walk beyond my borders into a void. A rooted neighbor can be judged, bargained with, or brought down. A kingdom with no god above it is a house with no roof, and I know what to do about that.

## Answer 5

Old. Thousands of years, with ruins beneath ruins, and with the memory of those who raised them thinned to song and rumor. A world that has been broken and rebuilt many times has learned that things end, and a people who know that things end are quicker to kneel to what endures. I would have the dead cities remember something I also remember.

## Answer 6

Rare and doubted, but real. A rumor most people dismiss, and in each generation a few who cannot. Not a wise-woman in every valley, for I would not have my priests competing with a hedge-witch for the people's awe. Let the magic of mortals be a small lamp, so that a god be seen as a sun.

## Answer 7

It should feel like borrowing from a creditor who is patient and does not forget. It should come as pressure in the ears, as a taste of iron, as the skin prickling before weather. And it should cost them in kind: warmth, years, memory, or the steadiness of the hand. A mortal who works wonder should always be able to be seen to have paid. Justice is served when the price is plain.

## Answer 8

Let a god stand out greatly. Not as a trick of the eye, but as the weather stands out from a still afternoon. The one-eyed man among the blind is a modest picture. I would have gods be unmistakable, so that no mortal need wonder who is above him, and so that when a god keeps a law the keeping is felt. Let no sorcerer be mistaken for one of us.

## Answer 9

Strange, and strange in the high and cold places especially. Familiar animals in the settled country, and beyond the settled country things that have no names, so that my people must come to me for names, and for protection from what lies unnamed. Let the wild have teeth and weather of its own, though not weather that rivals mine.

## Answer 10

Let them fear the sky going dark, and the thing that comes with the dark. Let them fear the broken oath, the judgment that falls on a house that did wrong, and the hunger of a winter that outlasts its stores. Beyond one another, let them fear what they cannot gather against alone. A good story is one in which the people must stand together, and the thing that stands them together is the thing they are afraid of.

## Answer 11

Warmth through the winter, which is to say fuel and shelter and the grain to see the cold out. And, for a deeper scarcity, trust. Let it be rare and dear, so that a bond between two strangers is counted a wonder, and an oath is kept because it was costly to give. I would trade a great deal for a world in which a word was worth something.

## Answer 12

Old roads, first. Roads are promises that someone meant to be followed. And bones of something enormous, in the ice, preserved: a thing from before, which the people will argue over. Also a sealed door or two in the mountains. I do not mean to say what is behind them. I would only like to be the one standing near when they are opened, and to know whether I should have left them shut.

## Answer 13

Yes, a few, and not friendly by default. Let there be a people who live under the ice and keep long memory, and a people of the high places who are wary of the sky and wary of me. I would have some of them capable of kneeling and some not, so that I should learn whom to bend and whom to leave. Let none of them be merely mortals in costume.

## Answer 14

Few. A traveler who comes from far away is a guest I should want to examine closely before the hearth is opened to him. If he brings a tool or a knowledge, let it be something that must be built slowly by my people from what the land provides, and let it come to me first. I do not forbid strangers. I ask that they be received, and weighed, and that no one be admitted who was not looked at.

## Answer 15

A pull, felt at any distance, but a pull that tells little: that another is there, and in which direction, and nothing of its temper. The knowing of the face should come only when we meet. I would have the first sense be a warning and the second a judgment, because I should like to be told something is coming before I am obliged to receive it.

## Answer 16

One body. A god in many places at once is a god who has delegated himself, and I distrust anything that is both present and absent. If it must be so, let the cost be real: that each further body be thinner, colder and slower to answer, and that the original feel every one of them fail. Let me be fully somewhere, and let that somewhere be where my people are.

## Answer 17

One who is able to say no to a god, and who is right to. A mortal of stubborn honesty, who keeps a law past the point where it costs him, and who asks me the one question I do not wish to be asked. I do not pretend I would enjoy him. But a land that produces such a person is worth ruling, and the story that follows him is worth watching to its end.

## Answer 18

A truth about the sky, and about what lay behind it before the world. A companion, perhaps, though I do not say it easily: one who is not mortal and not god, and who may stand beside me without needing me to be greater than I am. And a place with no weather in it. I should like to see the shape of that silence.

## Answer 19

That I be rightly worshipped. Not feared only, and not flattered, but known, and named correctly, and loved in the way a hard law is loved by those it has kept safe. And beneath that, a wish I hold more quietly: that what has been done once need not be done again, and that there be among the many gathered under me no one who must be judged as I judged.
