# People: the Quayfolk of Saltgate

The name of the people and of the home are my wish. They may call themselves otherwise, and I will use what they use.

## Who they are
A harbor people: fishers, coasters, salters, net-makers, rope-walkers, boat-builders and the farmers and herders of the strip of land behind the wall who feed them. They are plain, careful and talkative at a table, slow to trust a stranger and slower to turn one away. They are ordinary mortals, of one stock with their neighbors along the coast.

## How many
About five thousand, within the wall and in the steadings a short walk behind it.

## How they live
A walled town gathered close around a harbor, with the harbor wall holding back the water. Tall narrow houses of stone and timber, shared yards, a market quay, a hall where the council sits, boat sheds under the wall. Days go to the boats, the salt pans, the nets, the smithy and the fields behind. Late autumn is the hauling-up: boats pulled onto the shingle, stores counted, households drawn in around their fires. The winters are long and teach them to store, and the springs forgive them for it.

## Their code
- A stranger at the gate is given a fire, bread and salt for three nights before anyone asks his business.
- A lamp is left in a window of every household on the nights the boats are out and on the nights the gate is shut.
- No one is left on the far shore. A boat that does not return is looked for before it is mourned, and the looking is not abandoned by the first bad day.
- A debt is named aloud, in front of a witness, when it is made and when it is settled.
- A guest who breaks the roof's peace is put out at the gate, not struck; a host who turns away a guest in winter answers for it before the bench.
- They do not sell a person, and they do not rob a landing.

## Skills and culture
Boat-building, net and rope work, salting and smoking fish, reading weather and tide, coasting trade by small sail, careful bookkeeping on tally-sticks and knotted cords, the telling of long stories at table that differ a little from ward to ward. Ordinary craft only, at the level of the world's beginning.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None.

## Their magic
None. I know of no one among them who works a thing that is more than craft. What the Keeper fits to the world is for the Keeper to say.

## What they believe about gods
They hold that the door has to be kept, because something stands outside it, and that a stranger may be more than he seems. At a grave they say "the door is open," and leave a lamp lit through the night. Over a bad harvest they count the stores aloud in front of the whole ward and blame no one until the counting is done. When the water comes up in the night they ring the harbor bell, haul the boats, and open the upper floors to whoever's ground floor is wet. They have stories of a Late Guest who comes after the boats are hauled up and is known by what the household does when he knocks. They have no rites for any god and have not been asked for any. This is their belief, not a fact about the world.

## Rule and worship
Ruled by a council of elders and ward chieftains, in the leans I was given. Each ward sends one elder and one headman, and the council sits in the hall by the quay. The god is heard there and is not obeyed. There is no worship, no state cult, no priesthood and no shrines asked of them; what they do about me is theirs.

## Hierarchy
- The council decides.
- The Speaker of the council, chosen by the council from among its own and holding the office for a year at a time, keeps order in the hall and carries its word out.
- The Harbormaster, the Wall-Warden and the Tally-Keeper run the harbor, the wall with its paid watch (ordinary in size, fed and paid from the town's stores), and the town's counts and stores. They answer to the council.
- Disputes are settled by the Bench, three elders drawn from three different wards, who hear both sides in the hall's yard and give a judgment that stands unless the council overturns it.

## Helpers
The helpers are the council's own officers, who run the town when I am absent: the Speaker, the Harbormaster, the Wall-Warden, the Tally-Keeper and the Hall-Keeper. They carry out what the council has agreed and what my standing conduct allows. They are not bound in fact; they are honest and able by custom and oath, and promise nothing under pressure.

An order of mine goes from my mouth to the council, as advice and not command. If the council agrees, the Speaker puts it in the hall's tally, the Tally-Keeper copies it to each ward's elder, and each elder says it at the ward bell and again at the ward's tables, so that the last household has heard it said aloud by someone it knows. An order the council does not agree to does not travel.

## How the god meets them
As a stranger. I come down on the shingle outside the wall after the boats are hauled up, in late autumn, in the wool I travel in, and I wait outside the gate with the lamps above me. I do not know whether they have been waiting; I hope not, and I do not claim it. The gate is the town's to open. A town's wall and an open gate do not bind me, but a roof does, so I stay outside until I am asked.

The offices that stand under their own law, held by people who have never heard of me, are every one of them: the council, the Speaker, the Bench, the Harbormaster, the Wall-Warden, the Tally-Keeper and the Hall-Keeper. None is mine from the first hour. I am a guest until the council says otherwise.

I hope they know me for what I am by nearness over time, and are not told on the first night. If they ask, I do not lie about it.

## Questions for you
1. The Hall-Keeper, who keeps the council hall's door, speaks for the house and has the right to ask me in, as does the Speaker. Either may take it back aloud. The council as a whole may also give a general welcome by a vote entered in the tally, and by another vote withdraw it. A child of the hall's household who speaks for the house in the Hall-Keeper's absence counts, as the rule provides. I will tell the Hall-Keeper, and no one else, why I wait at the threshold, because someone who keeps the door should know what the door can and cannot do.
2. When the council disagrees with me, it does as it judges, and the tally records my objection with its reasons. I may argue again at the next sitting, with better reasons or the same reasons made plainer. I do not stop the council, I do not go over its head to the wards, and I do not use any power on the town to change its mind. If the matter is one I cannot abide, I leave the hall and the town, and my leaving is the only force I use.
