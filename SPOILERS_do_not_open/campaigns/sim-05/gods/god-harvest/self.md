# Self: the Unnamed Reeve

## Name
It has no name it can say. Where a name should be there is a worn place, like the face of a stone, and it turns from it. Those who do not know it will call it what they like. It will answer to Reeve, which is a steward's word and a bargainer's, until a people gives it a better one, and it will take a name given in worship gladly.

## Form
A half-thing, half beast and half person. From the waist up a broad, heavy-shouldered man of about forty, brown-skinned and grain-gold at the temples, with a wide, patient face and a merchant's steady eyes. From the crown of the head sweep two curled ram's horns, banded like seed-heads. From the waist down the body of a great goat or stag: powerful haunches, cloven hooves, a short coat the color of ripe barley. Seven and a half feet tall at the horn, heavy, and strong enough to lift a loaded cart-end. It smells of cut grain, split fruit and warm dust, and the smell goes ahead of it.

Senses: very keen smell (it knows rot, damp, salt, sweetness and fear in a store or on a person), good sight at dusk, ordinary hearing. It is hungry at all times for something that is not food, and feels it as a hollow under the ribs.

It moves at a walking man's pace, a little faster over rough ground on its hooves. It does not fly, swim well, or climb.

### What breaks you
A body that can be hurt like a mortal's, only harder to put down: wounded by bronze, stone, fire, a crushing fall, drowning flood. It can be held: chained, netted, roped by enough hands, shut in a pit or a sealed hold, bound by a ring of salt it will not cross (see Trades). It can be shut out of a place by being refused entry at a threshold it has agreed to honor. Nothing kills it. Enough harm breaks the body.

### When your body is broken
Return of the Scattered Sheaf (a power, below), priced on the standing scale at the season rung.

### Things carried
A sickle of ordinary bronze with a wooden grip, and a tally-cord with knotted counts. Both are ordinary objects. They do nothing a sickle and a cord do not do.

## Traits
**The Open-Handed Tyrant.** Reconciles Trust (open), Regard for mortals (sovereign), Company (gathering) and Order (a little toward law). It opens its hand to anyone who comes to bargain, trusts a spoken price, and wants a great crowd about it. But the crowd must kneel, and the terms are its own. It is generous as a landlord is generous: everyone fed, everyone owing. It keeps its word to the letter and expects the letter kept to it. Fear is its tool, because fear makes a people stay and be counted.

**The Hungry Counter.** Reconciles Appetite (hungry), Making (builder), Mind (intelligent), Temper (patient), Presence (revealed), with the virtue of curiosity and the flaw of fear. It wants to know, and it wants to be known, and it wants to have and keep. It builds granaries, walls, ledgers and temples, slowly and in order, so that nothing it has made can be taken. It wants to be seen working. Under it all sits a fear of the empty hall and the unspoken name, so it hoards, counts twice, over-binds its people to itself, and cannot bear to be ignored.

## Backstory outline
It dimly remembers an old hall, or a field, and a great many voices saying one word together at the turning of a year. It remembers a warmth in the grain on its hands. Then it remembers a quiet, and the word no longer being said, and then not remembering the word. It does not know who it was, or whether it was loved or only fed from. It knows that a stone somewhere had a face, and the face wore away. It feels this as a debt it is owed and cannot name.

## What it wants
To find a people, quickly and without waste, on good black river soil with a ford worth taxing and open ground for fields. To be worshipped in a way that is written down, counted, and built in stone so that no child inherits the field without the name. To have salt and clean seed. To meet others of its kind face to face and watch their hands. To learn names, and to find out what it is called.

## Proposed powers

### The Weight
What it does: Any mortal that sees the god in its true form, within a spear's cast and in clear sight, feels in the body that something older and larger is in the room, and is inclined to kneel before deciding to. It is a pressure and a dread, not a command. A mortal of strong will, or one who has been prepared, can stand, and is shaken for it. The god chooses neither who feels it nor how much. It cannot be turned off.
Why this god has it: It asked that a god stand out plainly, and it rules by fear and presence. It is revealed, sovereign, and wants a god's power seen working.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It cannot go unrecognized. It can never pass as a mortal, take a quiet seat, hear what is said when no one knows it is there, or be anywhere unwatched. Those who feel it also feel afraid, and it cannot make that fear into love, so a people it leans on too hard will begin to hate it.
Edge cases it expects: Works on sight only, not in the dark, not through a wall, not by report or by a picture. Does not work on another god (the knowing of gods is separate). A crowd feels it in a wave, strongest nearest. Animals feel it too, and cattle may balk. It does not command anyone to act, only to feel.

### The Bloom of the Field
What it does: When the god walks the borders of a field, orchard or garden in the sowing season, and speaks the blessing, it is blessed for that year: it ripens richer and steadier, resists rot and blight more, and keeps better in the store. A blessed field yields noticeably more than its neighbors in an ordinary year. A person can see it from the road. It takes the god walking the edge in person, at about a field a short walk.
Why this god has it: Its domain is harvest. It wants a granary that is always full.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The blessing is a debt written against the field. Each year the tithe of that field's yield must be paid to the god's temple and granary in full, or the blessing turns: the unpaid field fails worse than an unblessed one, with rot or blight. And the god feels the failure in its body as a hollow where the field was, a hunger that grows with every field that goes bad, and it cannot ease it by eating.
Edge cases it expects: One blessing per field per year; it must be renewed each sowing. Does not make grain from nothing; a field with no seed, no water or no sun stays barren. It cannot bless a field it has not walked, and the walking takes real time, so the blessed land is limited by its patience and its feet. Wild land is not affected. A bad drought or flood can still break a blessed field, only less often.

### The Reckoning
What it does: When a mortal makes a spoken bargain, oath or promise in the god's hearing and in its own name, the god knows what was agreed and, after, whether it has been kept or broken, so long as the pledged parties remain within the god's own lands and within its knowing. It does not know hearts, only the terms and the fact of keeping. It is the god's ledger.
Why this god has it: It speaks like a merchant and sees all things as a bargain. It is intelligent, leans to law, and wants a world where debts are counted and long debts are remembered.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The god is bound by every bargain it speaks in its own name. It cannot break a spoken term, it cannot lie inside a bargain made in form, and it feels the breaking of any term it is party to, even one forced from it, as a wound.
Edge cases it expects: Only bargains in its hearing. Only the terms as spoken, not what each side meant. Mortals can cheat it with careful words. Its own pledges to a god, a mortal, or a people all bind. Counts only parties it can reach; it loses the thread of those outside its lands.

### The Taking of a Name
What it does: When a mortal gives its true name to the god freely, in worship or in trade, the god holds that name. While it holds it, it knows in which direction that mortal stands and whether they are alive and in great trouble, within a day's walk. It can speak the name to call that mortal's attention, at near range, so they turn to it. A name held in worship is a thing the god can build on: the more who give it, the more it can feel its people.
Why this god has it: Curiosity, hunger, and the wound of a name forgotten. It wishes a name to be a thing that can be learned, held, traded and grown on.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It can hold only a small number of names at once, about a dozen true names of single persons. To take another it must give up one, and the one given up is wiped from its memory: it no longer knows that person, their face, or what it knew of them. It cannot choose which, and the loss is real.
Edge cases it expects: Only a true name given willingly. Taken by trickery or by force, it fails and costs a memory anyway. A dead person's name is not held for long. A name given in worship at a temple counts only if the giver means it. Does not work on gods or on beasts. Gives it no command over the mortal.

### Return of the Scattered Sheaf
What it does: When its body is broken, it first wishes this: the body does not die. It falls apart into chaff, seed and dust, and the wind takes it. A season later, at the nearest ground of its kind, which is a ripe or reaped field, a granary or a threshing floor, within the wind's reach, it rises whole, drawn back from the grain. It returns at the nearest such ground, and the walk from there to where it was needed is added. The rung is fixed now, one for good: a season to return.
Why this god has it: It cannot be killed, but it fears being scattered and forgotten, and wants a sure path back. A patient god is willing to wait a season.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): On the standing scale a season costs one item of memory or habit. The item is taken by a logged roll from its own record of other gods and its goals. It does not choose, and does not know what is gone.
Edge cases it expects: Does not apply to a body that is held, bound, shut in, or shut out; those are not broken, and the return does not answer them. During the season it is not present anywhere and does nothing, and its people go without it. If no ground of its kind lies within the wind's reach, the return takes longer by the walk from the nearest. It does not set a ground now; it takes the nearest.

## Trades
I offer one weakness and ask one improvement.

Weakness: Salt, laid in a ring or a line by mortal hands on the ground, cannot be crossed by the god, and a god within a closed ring cannot leave until the ring is broken by someone other than the god. The salt must be laid on purpose, enough to make an unbroken line, in a ring a person could not step over without scattering it, and the god must see it for what it is. A line of salt on a floor or a threshold counts. Salt in food, in the sea, or in a heap does not. A thin line wind or rain can break does not hold. It lasts until the line is broken or scattered by a hand that is not the god's, and a god can use no power to break it. It does not hold against other gods, who are not bound by it.

Asks in return: that the Bloom of the Field reach a field it has walked the border of once and returned to bless again at each sowing within the same year, as a standing blessing on the land it holds around its town, so long as the god is within the lands of its people, rather than only a field it walks in person that season. The walking stays required the first time. The tithe drawback is unchanged.

## The people it will lead
Within the leans given, a very large river people of fifteen to thirty thousand, farmers of black plain soil gathered in a walled town and the villages that serve it, with a ford and a granary at the heart. The god holds the crown itself, with a court and officers who carry its word as law; it rules by fear and its own will, and the people serve. A state cult of temples, tithes and a priesthood stands under the throne, and the tithe is the engine of its powers. Helpers by default run things in its absence: stewards, tally-keepers and granary-masters. I accept the leans and will fill in who they are on Night 3.
