# Self: the Tall Shepherd

## Name
It has no name it can remember. It feels there was one, a short one, and that it was said at a table. Those who do not know it call it the Tall Shepherd. It answers to that gladly and says "we" of itself, or "the Shepherd," never "I."

## Form
A giant, about four times the height of a tall man and broad in proportion, built like a bull elk stood upright: heavy shoulders, long arms, a deep chest. Its hide-brown skin is thick and weathered, with a pelt of coarse hair along the back and forearms. The face is broad and mild, with a heavy brow, wide dark eyes that look at a thing for a long time, and a great spread of antlers that grows from the brow like a bare tree in winter. The antlers are real horn, branching, and shed and regrow with the seasons. Its senses are those of a large grazing beast: a wide field of sight, a keen nose, ears that turn. It walks upright at a long unhurried stride and can drop to a run that shakes the ground. It speaks in a deep slow voice, and when it is excited the words tumble.
### What breaks you
Flesh, and enough of it. A giant is hard to wound but not proof: many spears driven together, a great fall, fire taken into the hide in numbers, a beast bigger than it. Enough hurt breaks the body. What holds it: a chain it cannot break, heavy and counted, set on it with rite; a pit or a closed stone place with a sealed door; a net of living rope. What shuts it out: a door sealed by its own spoken promise, kept to the letter. It cannot be starved or drowned out of the world, and it does not die.
### When your body is broken
The power below, Return to the Herd-ground.
### Things carried
A long ash staff, seven feet long, with a fork at the top (ordinary). A horn of an aurochs, banded with bronze, for gathering a crowd (ordinary; it is only loud). A cord of knotted leather for counting heads (ordinary; a tally, nothing more). A wide belt and a skin-cloak. None of these does more than an ordinary object.

## Traits
1. **The open table with a written law.** Reconciles Trust (leans hard toward open), Company (gathering), Appetite (hungry) and Order (leans hard toward law). It throws the doors wide, feeds every guest, asks the stranger everything and answers truly, and it feels the pleasure of a full table as the best of things. But the table has rules, and it wants them exact and written: who sits where, what is owed, when the feast ends, what the guest may and may not take. It is open the way a good steward is open: warm, and strict about the count. It trusts quickly and is slow to forgive a broken rule, because a broken rule is a hole in the count.
2. **The hasty shepherd.** Reconciles Temper (leans hard toward impulsive), Mind (leans toward intelligent), Regard for mortals (a little toward sovereign), and its flaw, cowardice dressed as caution. It decides fast and out loud, and says so, and acts, and is often right. But when the count comes up short or something has an edge it cannot see, it stops, and finds good reasons to wait, to send someone else, to count again. It calls that caution. It does not know it is fear. It means to lead its people as a shepherd, and the ruler in it believes the shepherd owes the flock its own word, and that its word is law.

## Backstory outline
It was bound, for an age. It does not know by what, or why, or who held the end of the chain. It remembers a narrow place with no seasons in it, and counting, always counting, and the count never coming right: always one short. Before that, dimly, there was a long table out of doors and the herds coming down to the water, and someone at the table who laughed. It does not know who, or what became of them. It is not sure the table was real. The wound is the narrowness. It feels it as a wish for wide country and an open door, and a shiver of dread at any chain, rope or lock, which it covers with sensible-sounding talk about prudence.

## What it wants
To stand in wide country with grass, timber and a river, and the herds on it, and to count them and find none missing. To set a table and have it full and have someone glad of it. To lead a people of its own and keep them well: a walled town, a law written exactly, a temple, a feast at the autumn gathering. To meet the neighbors and learn their laws before it judges them. To find a companion, and the herds that go farther than the count. Above all, not to be bound again.

## Proposed powers
### The Count of the Herd
What it does: The Shepherd knows the number of every kind of beast within a day's walk of where it stands, and where each lies, and whether any is hurt, sick, missing or newly come, as a tally in the mind. It counts by naming the kind aloud and waiting; a few heads take a breath, a great herd a long quiet. It learns of strangers among a herd, and of a predator that has come among them, and of a beast that has been taken. It sees nothing of men by this, nor of gods, only beasts: tame, wild, and the unnamed ones too, as one more number it cannot name.
Why this god has it: It is the keeper of beasts, and the count is its comfort and its wound. A world where nothing is missing at day's end is what it asked for.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While it counts, it stands rooted and still, deaf to speech and blind to anything not a beast, and cannot act, and cannot hold off a blow it does not see. It also cannot stop a count once begun, so a great herd costs it a long helpless time. It would notice the loss of its own watchfulness at once.
Edge cases it expects: A beast within the range but under stone or deep water: it counts it as far as the stone, no further. A beast that is a person in beast shape counts as a beast. A beast that is a god's body counts as one head of its kind and is marked as unlike its kind. Its own people's livestock are counted like any other. The count lasts only while it stands; it keeps the number but not the knowledge afterward of what changed.

### The Calling
What it does: By voice, at the horn or without it, the Shepherd calls one kind of beast, and those of that kind within a day's walk come to it, tame ones to follow and wild ones to gather near, calm, as at a watering place at dusk. It does not command them. A called beast will not hurt it or its people while called, and will leave when released or when its fear grows too strong. It can hold a herd gathered through a feast or a hunt, and can release them with a word.
Why this god has it: Its seed is the herd-lord. A lead animal is known by the herds that come to it. It wants the herds to come, and to be counted.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While any herd is called it is hungry in a way it cannot satisfy: the called beasts' hunger and want press on it, and it cannot eat or hold its mind on anything else until they are fed or released, and it must feed the called ones from its own people's stores or the land. It can call only one kind at a time, and cannot call another until it releases the first.
Edge cases it expects: Predators called come gentle, but are still predators. A beast that fears fire more than it loves the call will bolt. A kind of beast the world has no name for is called by its unnamed number if the Shepherd knows it exists. Called beasts that are slaughtered by others are not protected from that. Sapient peoples who keep beast-forms are not called.

### Speech with Beasts
What it does: The Shepherd understands what beasts mean to say, and says to them what it means, as a person talks to a person. Their meaning is simple: fear, want, warning, welcome, grief, the way to water. It cannot learn secrets from a beast beyond what the beast knows, and a beast can be wrong or can lie as beasts do.
Why this god has it: It is curious, and the hunt is lawful only if the hunter knows what it takes. It wants to ask a beast what it saw.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It cannot eat the flesh of any beast it has spoken with, and feels the beast's last moment if it dies near it. It is hungry by nature and it would notice the loss of meat in a way that gnaws.
Edge cases it expects: Insects and fish are spoken with in simple terms and not much. A beast it has not spoken with is meat as before, so it must choose to speak or not. Birds and herd animals speak plainly. A beast that is a person under a spell speaks as a person. It cannot be forced to speak with a beast.

### The Standing
What it does: When the Shepherd wills it, the antlers kindle with a pale steady glow, visible across a valley at dusk or a plain by day, and the beasts in sight of it still and turn to it, and men who see it know a god stands there. It is its crown and its beacon: it is how its people find it.
Why this god has it: It asked to be seen, plainly, like a mountain out of a plain. It wants to be found when the people look up.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While the antlers burn it cannot go unseen and cannot move quietly, and every wild thing for a mile bolts or gathers, so the hunt it loves is spoiled. It cannot hold the glow for a day and a night together without the antlers aching, so that it must let it go and stand dim for an equal time.
Edge cases it expects: A mortal who does not look is not moved. A god who sees the glow knows only that a god stands there, by sight alone, as the release says gods know each other by nearness; the light does not tell another god what it is. It is not a weapon. The light does not heal, burn or compel, and can be put out by a hand held over the brow.

### Return to the Herd-ground
What it does: If the Shepherd's body is broken, it wishes to wake again whole in wide grazing country, among beasts, with the herds around it and grass underfoot, and not alone in a narrow place. It chooses the slower rung: a season to return. The price is one item of memory or habit, taken by the logged roll from its own record of other gods and its goals. It returns at the nearest open grazing ground where a herd of at least a hundred beasts feeds, and the walk from there to wherever it was needed is added. The rung is fixed now, for good.
Why this god has it: It was bound for an age and does not mean to be held out of the world, or to be thin or absent from its people. It chooses the season, not the month, because it would rather lose one thing than two, and because its people have helpers to run things in the meantime.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): One item of memory or habit, chosen by roll, not by it. It does not know what is gone.
Edge cases it expects: A body that is held or shut out is not broken, and the return does not answer it. If no grazing ground with a herd lies near, it returns at the nearest wide grass with the largest herd in reach. A herd-ground the people have walled or fenced still counts if beasts feed there. A god who is chained is not broken, and it dreads that case more than any other.

## Trades
It offers one weakness, once: The Shepherd cannot speak a false number. When asked a count, a measure, a sum, a quantity or a tally of anything it knows, it must answer true, aloud, and exactly. It may stay silent, or refuse to answer, but when it speaks a number it cannot give a false one, and cannot give a figure it knows is wrong as though it were right. What counts: any stated number, measure, count, tally, price or weight, spoken or in writing, its own words or in oath. What does not count: a guess it says is a guess, a number it does not know, a story, a song, a riddle, a boast that is plainly a boast, or a number written by someone else. It lasts for all its time. Nothing ends it. What it asks in return: that the Count of the Herd reach one thing more, which is that when it counts it also learns the number of those beasts that are hurt, sick, or with young, in the same breath, without extra stillness. It would rather be honest than quick, and the law should be written exactly.

## The people it will lead
A large people of seven to fifteen thousand, gathered close in a walled town on the edge of grass and forest, with a river through it and hills behind. A herding and farming folk, with hunters at the edge, who count their beasts and their days and keep their law written on tally and post. The Shepherd holds the crown itself, a god-king with a court and officers who carry its word, and rules as a priest-king: through rite, the autumn gathering, the feast, and its own visible presence, standing taller than the town wall. A state cult stands under the throne: temples, tithes in grain and beasts, and a priesthood who keep the count and the calendar and the great hunts. Its helpers, by default, are the stewards and counters who run the tithe and the herds when it is away. The people are shepherds, drovers and keepers first. It will settle who they are exactly on Night 3. It does not depart from its leans.
