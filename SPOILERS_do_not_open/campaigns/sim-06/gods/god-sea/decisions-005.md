# Decisions, Year 5: Skerr (god-sea)

## The year

1. Autumn, the first dusks. I meet the Warden-Monarch alone at the tide door at the lowest tide at dusk, in my cloak, in the dim. I answer her question first, in these words: "You had the right. It was your word and your hand, and you said so aloud, which was honest. The lamp is a lamp in any hand but mine. It does not still water for you. Say that to no one outside the court. The boat broke because I was far and the swell was long. That is mine to carry and not yours. Do not carry it." I ask her to carry my words to the Harbormaster and to the Eldest Midwife (see Messages). I read the Midwife's list of the year's dead from the jar and the stick of seven notches. I tell her this year I will not go far from the bell. I ask her to strike the bell once for a word and twice for a boat in trouble, and I will come at once, by water, from wherever I lie within a night's swim of the quay. I thank her for the tenders and for the lamp lit at the water's edge. Reason: she asked in good faith and has kept my word four years. She is owed a plain yes, and she must not be left alone with a loss that was mine.

2. Autumn, the first night after that, in the water off the mouth. I put my right hand in the salt and give the night to listening (Water's Hearing), then spend the deaf day under the rocks out of sight. Reason: I did not hear the swell or the shoal last year because I was away. I want one ordinary night of the home coast, with the star dimming more each year and nothing else known. I accept that the shore will go quiet where I lie.

3. Autumn, the lowest tide at night before the Harbormaster hauls. I go to the broken lower timbers I found inside the mouth in Year 1, along the line the raised chain makes. I lay three pale stones in a row on the bed beside them with my own hand, and I touch nothing else. I tell the Warden which line to give the Harbormaster, and I tell her the stones are laid. Reason: he will not drag a harbor on a word, and he is right. A lead dragged along the chain line will ring on stone, and then the timbers are his to lift or leave. I name no cause.

4. Winter, before the first hard storm. I do one piece of Sound Timber at the yard in the dark, the keel or lowest strake of the boat the point builds to replace the one it lost, if the point and the Master of Timber choose it, with the Harbormaster's assent and the Warden's seal, joined by my right hand alone. I keep the hand idle the next day. I ask nothing for it. Reason: the point lost a master and his son and a boat in one hour. A sound keel is the one thing I can put into their hands without a word that costs them. I do not ask them to forgive the lamp.

5. Winter, the dark half. I ask the court, through the Warden, to do what it did the last four winters: haul and tar the boats early, check the chain and log it in the book, count the stores and share them through the lean, keep the harbor lamp lit every dusk, send no boat out in the worst weather for a catch, keep the Harbormaster's rule that no boat goes past the mouth while the stair can hear the Skerries. I add one thing, to be said by the court and not by me: when a long swell runs with no storm in it, the Captains' boat and its crew stand at the mouth with a line, and no boat of the point goes out to meet it. I leave the tally of storms and boats on the step, and the Warden keeps the star's count on her stick, one notch a night. The Warden may light my lamp at the water's edge in any swell, as a light to steer by, and she says aloud that it is a light. I light it myself for calm only if I am at the water, and I do not leave it after, as before. I do not use it against a storm or for comfort. The harbor water will not take me for a season after.

6. Spring and the long swell months, within a night's swim of the quay and in reach of the bell all the while. I swim the shoal off the point where the boat of the point was caught, and the water outside the mouth, slowly, by night, and learn its shape with my own body: where it rises, where a long swell stands up on it, where a boat can pass. I send the Warden the shape by the jar, in notches and a mark, and ask her to give it to the Harbormaster to draw into his book if he will. I go no farther west or east this year. I do not go back to the causeway. Reason: I was at a far shore when the swell came, and nothing at that shore needed me. The shoal is the town's own danger and I can learn it with my own body.

7. Summer. I do one more piece of Sound Timber, a keel or strake of the boat that goes out latest and comes in last, chosen by the Master, if the court still takes my word. I keep the hand idle the next day. I lie below the quay out of the tenders' sight and watch the ferryman, the two tenders and the Midwife's household from the water, and choose no keeper yet. If the Midwife sends me a house through the Warden, I go once, at the lowest tide at dusk, hooded and low, to the house she names if it stands within a short drag of the water and it is quiet, and I hear what she asks and say what I can. I take no bed, bread or name. If she does not send, I do not go. Reason: those are the boats the sea takes first and I will not lose a crew. She is the first who put a true question to my face, and a hidden god that never answers keeps no one.

## How I mean to be met
As before. Rarely, at dusk or the lowest tide, hooded, in a dim place, through the Warden at the tide door, one holder at a time. I give no name beyond Skerr and show my shape only if asked and the holder keeps still. My word is short. I ask for no rites or worship. I say nothing of the answered bell.

## Messages

To the Harbormaster, as the Warden-Monarch would name him. The words exactly:

"You asked where inside the mouth. Along the line the raised chain makes there lie the broken lower timbers of two boats built as the town builds. I do not say they are the two you lost. I do not say what broke them. At the lowest tide of the autumn I will lay three pale stones in a row on the bed beside them. A lead dragged along the chain line will ring on the stones. After that it is yours to say. You said nobody else had thanked you for the tide. I thank you. I ask nothing more of your chain."

Carrier: the Warden-Monarch, by her own mouth, alone with him, on a day she judges, in the autumn, after I have laid the stones and told her so. Route: the quay, or wherever she finds him; she chooses. Nothing sent with it. If he cannot be found, she waits and carries it when she can. If he is hostile, she says nothing further and tells me through the jar. If he asks questions, she answers only what I have told her: that I am the one who keeps the keeping, that I am not the town's, that I take no rites, and that she does not know the rest.

To the Eldest Midwife, as the Warden-Monarch would name her. The words exactly:

"You asked two things at the widow's bed. I heard both. I have no whole answer to either, and I will not give a half one. I count the widow among the kept. I took nothing from the house. I take your count of the year's dead as the only honest count and I read the list. I do not say Low Garrow and I do not say not. If you send me a house, I will come once, as before."

Carrier: the Warden-Monarch, by her own mouth, alone with the Midwife, on a day she judges, in the autumn. Route: as the Warden chooses. Nothing sent with it. If the Midwife cannot be found, the Warden waits and carries it when she can. If the Midwife is hostile, the Warden says nothing further and tells me through the jar. If she asks questions, the Warden answers only what I have told her, and says she does not know the rest.

## For the Keeper to rule
- Sound Timber: I mean two pieces this year, many weeks apart, never within a day of the last, inside what the card and the earlier rulings cover. I will do fewer if the Keeper holds otherwise.
- Calm Under the Lamp: I mean the Warden and tenders to light the lamp as a light, not as the calm, and say so. I plan no lighting of my own unless a swell short of a storm catches a boat while I am at the water.
- Water's Hearing: one listening in autumn off the mouth, with its deaf day.
- The bell: I mean to stay within a night's swim of the quay in the long swell months and hear the bell through water and stone, as my form does. I ask the Keeper to tell me in the release if the bell was struck and I did not come, or if I could not hear it.
- Laying three stones on the harbor bed by hand is ordinary craft and no power. I say so only to be sure.
- I try to grow no power this year.
