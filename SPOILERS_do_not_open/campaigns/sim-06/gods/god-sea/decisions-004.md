# Decisions, Year 4: Skerr (god-sea)

## The year

1. Autumn, the first dusks. I meet the Warden-Monarch alone at the tide door at the lowest tide at dusk, in my cloak, in the dim. I say my answers to the Harbormaster and to the Eldest Midwife in front of her, once, and ask her to carry them (see Messages). I ask for the clerks' list of the autumn's dead from the jar and I read it. I tell her I will be gone from the water by the town for some weeks in spring, and that the lamp stays in her keeping. Reason: two questions have stood unanswered a year and each was put in good faith. A plain word is owed. The Warden is the one holder who has heard me and taken my word.

2. Autumn, the first night after that, in the water at the point. I put my right hand in the salt and give the night to listening (Water's Hearing), then spend the deaf day under the rocks out of sight. Reason: I want to hear whether anything has come along this coast that is not tide, now that the star dims more each year. I accept that the shore will go quiet where I lie.

3. Winter, before the first hard storm. I do one piece of Sound Timber at the quay in the dark, a pile, a step or a strake the Master of Timber has named with the Harbormaster's assent and the Warden's seal, joined by my right hand alone, and I keep the hand idle the next day. Reason: the winter storms test the quay first, and the Master has named the pieces well.

4. Winter, the dark half. I ask the court, through the Warden, to do what it did the last three winters: haul and tar the boats early, check the chain and log it in the book, count the stores and share them through the lean, keep the harbor lamp lit every dusk, send no boat out in the worst weather for a catch. I leave the tally of storms and boats on the step, and the Warden keeps the star's count on her stick, one notch a night, and leaves it in the jar at the turn. The lamp stays in the Warden's keeping. If a boat is caught in a swell short of a storm, I light it myself at the water's edge by the harbor mouth, and the two tenders, the old woman at the east lamp and the boy at the quay's west end, refill and trim it up to a night and a day. I do not use it for comfort or against a storm. The harbor water will not take me for a season after, and I will lie off the point.

5. Spring, the lean time, after the Warden has the word that the court is holding its stores. I go under the water by night west along the shore to the old causeway's shore end, 66.5 miles, and lie there one night with my right hand in the salt and listen (Water's Hearing, one listening, with its deaf day after, taken far from the town). I mark whether anything has moved among the footings or the cut stones since I swam there, and whether any hull comes near. If I find something kept by someone, I do not go near it. I come back and decide. I send no mortal into a bad place. I tell the town nothing of the place until I know what it is. Reason: it is the one old place I have found, I cannot say whether it is Low Garrow, and the sea may carry a sound from there that it did not carry from the harbor.

6. Summer. I do one more piece of Sound Timber, a keel or strake of the boat that goes out latest and comes in last, chosen by the Master, if the court still takes my word, and I keep the hand idle the next day. I keep to the water and the town's far edge at dusk and lie below the quay out of the tenders' sight. I watch the ferryman, the two tenders and the Midwife's household from the water and choose no keeper yet. Reason: those are the boats the sea takes first, and I will not lose a crew this time.

7. Summer, if the Midwife sends me a house through the Warden. I go once more, at the lowest tide at dusk, hooded and low, to the house she names if it stands within a short drag of the water and it is quiet, and I say what I can and hear what she asks. I take no bed, bread or name. If she does not send, I do not go. Reason: she is the first who asked me a true question to my face, and a hidden god that never answers keeps no one.

## How I mean to be met
As before. Rarely, at dusk or the lowest tide, hooded, in a dim place, through the Warden at the tide door, one holder at a time. I give no name beyond Skerr and show my shape only if asked and the holder keeps still. My word is short. I ask for no rites or worship. I say nothing of the answered bell.

## Messages

To the Harbormaster, as the Warden-Monarch would name him. The words exactly:

"I asked about the two boats because I found their broken lower timbers inside the mouth, and I wanted to know if it was a storm, the chain, or something else. I do not know whether the chain was up. I heard no hull in your waters that was not yours. I do not take the book's word as wrong, and I do not take it as the whole. You took a lead and grapnel to the line and found no hull. I thank you for that. I ask nothing more of your chain."

Carrier: the Warden-Monarch, by her own mouth, alone with him, on a day she judges, in the autumn. Route: the lamp house to the quay, or wherever she finds him; she chooses. Nothing sent with it. If he cannot be found, she waits and carries it when she can. If he is hostile, she says nothing further and tells me through the jar. If he asks questions, she answers only what I have told her: that I am the one who keeps the keeping, that I am not the town's, that I take no rites, and that she does not know the rest.

To the Eldest Midwife, as the Warden-Monarch would name her. The words exactly:

"You asked which people I did not keep, and whether it was Low Garrow. I do not remember which. I remember that there was a people and that the water came up and I was not enough. I do not know that it was Low Garrow. I will not say it was and I will not say it was not. You asked whether I count the dead as kept or as lost. I count them as kept. I count the ones I did not keep by the same hand. I take your list each autumn and I read it. You asked what I want of the town. I want the boats home. I want no crew lost. I want nothing else of it, and I do not want to be asked for more than that. If you send me a house, I will come once, as before."

Carrier: the Warden-Monarch, by her own mouth, alone with the Midwife, on a day she judges, in the autumn. Route: as the Warden chooses. Nothing sent with it. If the Midwife cannot be found, the Warden waits and carries it when she can. If the Midwife is hostile, the Warden says nothing further and tells me through the jar. If she asks questions, the Warden answers only what I have told her, and says she does not know the rest.

## For the Keeper to rule
- Sound Timber: I mean two pieces this year, many weeks apart, never within a day of the last, inside what the card and the earlier rulings cover. I will do fewer if the Keeper holds otherwise.
- Calm Under the Lamp: I mean the tenders to refill and tend it for up to a night and a day as before, with me away from the water after I light it, as the earlier ruling gives. I plan no lighting unless a swell short of a storm catches a boat.
- Water's Hearing: two listenings in the year, one at the point in autumn and one at the causeway's far shore in spring, each with its deaf day. The spring one is far outside twenty miles of the town, and the card measures the twenty miles from where I listen. I ask the Keeper to rule that this holds.
- I try to grow no power this year.
