# People: unnamed

I leave the people and their home unnamed. A mortal will name them in play.

## Who they are
Mortal folk of the common kind, small and weathered, a people of one harbor town and the shore around it. They are fishers, boatwrights, salt-boilers and ferrymen first, farmers a poor second. They are not a conquering or wandering people. They have held this coast a long time and have lost a good deal of it to the sea. This is a wish: I do not say how long.

## How many
About two thousand, inside and just outside the walls. I take a middle of the small range and ask for no more, because a harbor on thin soil feeds few.

## How they live
In a walled harbor town of grey stone below and old timber above, on a cold broken shore, with small fields and goat and sheep pasture on the headland's lee side. Boats go out in the short fair days and come in before the weather. Autumn is the busy season: the last catch is salted and smoked, the boats are hauled and caulked, and the stores are counted against the winter. Winter is mending, boiling salt, spinning, and waiting. Spring is lean. Summer is earned. They keep a count of storms by the year, not only of days. They keep a lamp at the harbor mouth, lit every dusk through the dark half of the year.

## Their code
They honor the one who stays: the keeper, the ferryman who goes back out for a late boat, the midwife called at any hour, the lamp-warden who does not leave the lamp. They honor an oath to the harbor. The worst of them would still not leave a boat's crew in the water while a hand could be lifted, and would not put out the harbor lamp while a boat was unaccounted for. These are customs and oaths. They are temper and honor, and they can fail.

## Skills and culture
Ordinary and free: clinker-built open boats and small decked ones, nets, long lines, weirs, salting, smoking, drying on racks, boiling brine for salt, tarring, rope from hemp and from spun weed-fibre, sail from coarse wool and linen, a stone and timber quay, and a small bronze and iron smithy for nails, hooks and tools (no steel). They read weather by cloud, swell and gull. They keep a tally on notched sticks, and the court's clerks keep a harbor-book of boats out and boats back. They bury the drowned who are found, and set an empty cairn for the ones who are not.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I ask for no edge, and offer no weakness for one. Their patience and their care for a late boat are a habit and a pride, taken as temper, able to fail.

## Their magic
None that I will name. I ask for no one among them who works a thing that cannot be done by hand. If the Keeper's fitting of mortal magic puts such a one among them, I ask only that I be told in the release, and that they be ordinary about it.

## What they believe about gods
On the morning before I come: that the sea is not a god but is old and keeps what it keeps. A few old people say that someone keeps the keeping, a patient thing that walks the bottom and does not answer. Most doubt it. They do not pray to it. Fishers leave a heel of bread or a fish-head on the quay's lowest step and do not say why. The line that holds the throne is called sacred for an old promise, the way the line of lamp-wardens has always been, and the people know the promise but not who made it. This is their belief and a wish about their past; the world decides.

## Rule and worship
Within my leans. A mortal line holds the throne in my name and is held sacred for it, and a court stands between the throne and the people, and I am reached only through it. The line is the old line of lamp-wardens, the family that has kept the harbor lamp. It is sacred for the lamp and the promise, and a monarch of it keeps the lamp and the harbor-book and says the court's word. No worship. They ask nothing of me in rites, and I ask nothing of them. What they do about me is theirs.

## Hierarchy
The Warden-Monarch, from the line, sits the throne, keeps the lamp and carries the court's word to the people. The court under the throne: the Harbormaster, who rules the quay, boats and tides; the Master of Timber, who rules the yard and the boatwrights; the Reeve of the Fields, who rules the headland farms and stores; the Eldest Midwife, who speaks for births, sickness and the dead; and two old Captains, who speak for the boats and for the spear. Disputes of death, boundary and marriage are settled first by the household heads and the street's elder, then by the Harbormaster in the harbor court, which sits at low water on the quay steps, and last by the Warden-Monarch, who does not overrule the court without the Eldest Midwife's assent. This is who they are in office, never what they will do. Each can fail me.

## Helpers
The court is the helper body. The Harbormaster keeps the town's order and the harbor-book. The Master of Timber keeps the yard and sees which pieces I have joined. The Eldest Midwife keeps the town's peace between households. The Warden-Monarch keeps the lamp. The two Captains hold the muster. About one hundred and fifty can carry a spear when it comes to that, mostly fishers with a boat-hook and a short spear, drilled by the Captains each spring, and the walls and harbor chain are their post. When I am away, the court runs the town by its offices and its books, and the Warden-Monarch decides what the court cannot. A word of mine reaches the far end of my people through the court's clerks, who read it to the Harbormaster, who gives it to the boat-captains and the street-elders. I say little, and my word is short.

## How the god meets them
Rarely, and from the edge. I come in at dusk by the harbor mouth, or in the dark of the lowest tide, in my cloak, and I meet the court through the lamp-warden, at the tide-door, not in the open. I am not shown to the town. I let a few doubt. The warden and the Eldest Midwife may be given my shape in a dim room, once they have proved they will keep it. I will hear the people's own complaint, and I will not be called on to settle it. This is my wish for how I meet them, and the world decides.

## Questions for you
1. Who tends lamps, and would they know what they were tending? The lamp-wardens tend lamps, the line and the apprentices of the line, and a harbor lamp is kept by a boy and an old woman at each of the two ends of the quay. They know the harbor lamp is a lamp. They do not know that my lamp is the instrument of the calm. I tell the Warden-Monarch alone, once, when the Warden-Monarch has been proved, that my lamp is not to be lent or snuffed, and the Warden-Monarch decides whom to tell. They would know a lamp that was lit, and that the water lay quiet, and would call it luck.
2. How does a word of mine get to the court, and theirs to me, when I lie in the water? A bell and a tally. At the foot of the quay's lowest step hangs a small bronze bell on a chain below low water, which rings when struck from the step, and I hear it through water and stone. A bell struck once means a word is left. The court leaves the word on notched sticks in a sealed jar on the lowest step, and I read the notches. I answer by leaving a stick on the step before dawn, with a mark the Warden-Monarch has been given and no one else. In a storm the bell is not heard, and the court knows to wait.
3. Whose boats and quays get Sound Timber first, and who decides? The harbor first: the quay's piles and the lowest steps, then the keels of the two boats that go out latest and come back last, because they are the ones the sea will take. The Master of Timber decides which boats and piles, with the Harbormaster's assent and the Warden-Monarch's seal, and I join the piece with my right hand and say nothing about why. I take one piece at a time and cannot hold a hammer for a day after, so the Master of Timber is told which pieces are mine, and the town thinks them well made.
