# People: the Longwalk

Their own name for themselves, as I hope it: the folk of the long walk, the Longwalk. I give it as a wish, and they may call themselves otherwise. They have no fixed home, so I name none; their camping grounds stay unnamed until a mortal names them in play.

## Who they are
Mortal folk of the common kind, herders and hunters of wooded upland and open grass, many clans in one band, various in speech and custom at the edges and one in the way they walk and share. Not one blood. Some clans married in from older bands, and a few families came to the walk from river villages and stayed. They are lean, weathered and unhurried, and children walk from the age of four.

## How many
About five thousand, the usual number. I ask for no more and no fewer. A people that moves with its herds and its tents can only be so large before the grass and the wood run short, and five thousand is a band that can still turn together.

## How they live
They keep cattle, goats and a few sheep, and dogs in numbers. They follow a long round of the year: the high wooded uplands in the lean of winter, for fuel and shelter, down to the grass in spring behind the first storms, along the river under its line of trees in summer for water and fattening, and back through the autumn hunt in the timber, with the larders filled. A beaten wide trail across the grass is theirs, worn by the going and coming of many feet and hooves, and smoke over the trees is their camp. They live in hide and felt tents on light frames, packed on sledges and the backs of beasts. They have no walls and no fixed seat.

Days are work. At dawn the herds are driven out and the dogs go with them. Women and men hunt, tend stock, dress hides, cut and carry wood, dry and salt meat, spin, weave and mend. Children mind the young stock. At dusk the fires are lit and the council's runners go from fire to fire. In spring the first storms bring the calving, and the whole band moves with it.

## Their code
They honor the share. Meat taken in the hunt is divided to every hearth in the clan before the hunter keeps his own, and a debt of meat, salt, or a beast is paid or remembered for three generations. They honor the guest: a stranger who has eaten at a hearth is safe under it for as long as he stays, and three days after. They honor the season of the hunt: no doe with young is taken, and no beast is killed for its hide alone.

The worst of them would still not kill a guest under a roof, would not break a share, and would not leave the old and the lame to die on the trail without a fire and a guard. A man who does any of these is not driven out. He is walked behind the band, at a distance, unfed from the common pot, until the council says otherwise.

## Skills and culture
Ordinary culture only. They make felt, hide, bone, wood and stone tools, bows and spears with hardened and stone and bone points, nets, snares and pitfalls, pack frames, sledges, rope, and plain pots of fired clay for the larders when they stay a season. They know salt as a thing to hold meat through the winter and trade for it, and they carry salt as the first of their goods. They keep count with knotted cords and notched sticks, and the cord is read at council. They sing the round of the year, with a song for every ford and every grazing ground, and these songs are their map. They know herd animals by their lines and keep breeding stock with care; a bull of good line is a clan's pride.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None asked. Nothing here is a real edge over another people of the same number, and I offer no weakness against anything. The habits below are taken as temper and may fail.

Habits and pride, taken as temper and able to fail: they are slow to panic on the trail and slow to anger in council; they are hard to hurry. They are proud of their dogs. These may give way under a long hunger or a long fear.

## Their magic
One, and I am happy for the Keeper to fit it to the world. An old woman among them, called the Still One by the clan she was born to, can sit by a herd at dusk and tell its mood and its trouble: sick, afraid, near to a hunt, or ready to calve. It is simple. It costs her: she goes without eating the day after, and she is slow among her own for some days, and the young are a little wary of her. She trains no one, and she would not wish it on a child. Others among them are only good with animals, which is ordinary.

## What they believe about gods
On the morning before I come: they say the country has keepers, in the high places and the old timber, and that a keeper may be asked for nothing and forgives nothing. They have never seen one. Some say a keeper is a beast that outlived its own kind. Some say the storms are the keepers' tempers. Most say that it is the work of a herd to keep the herd, and that a keeper, if it ever came, would be heard at council like any elder, and no more. They make no rites. They set a share of the first kill of the autumn on a flat stone and leave it, and they do not say for whom. The dogs, they say, know old things.

## Rule and worship
A council of elders and clan heads rules them, twelve to fifteen, one from each clan or clan group, by long standing and by consent. The god is heard there and does not rule it. Worship: none, within my lean. They ask no rites of me and I ask none of them. What they do about me is theirs.

## Hierarchy
The Council of the Fire sits at the stopped camp, at dusk, in a ring. It chooses a Speaker from among its own, for as long as the band trusts the Speaker, who keeps order and carries the council's word. The council is advised by the Houndwarden, who keeps the dogs and reads the beasts and omens, and by the Marchwarden, who keeps the route and the song of the year. Disputes are settled by the Three at the Fire: three elders named by the Speaker for each matter, hearing a death, a boundary, a marriage gone bad. The Three speak the settlement, and blood price is paid in beasts and salt, not in lives. A matter too heavy for the Three goes to the council as a whole.

The Speaker I hope for is patient and well liked, past sixty, called Orrin Haddock in my wish; I give the name as a hope. The Houndwarden I hope is a lean and watchful woman of middle years, called Tessaly, who is close to the dogs and slow to speak, and who is the first to see the dogs turn. Each of them can fail me in play.

## Helpers
The council's helpers carry out what is agreed. I ask these offices:
- The Speaker, who keeps the council and carries its word to the clans.
- The Houndwarden, who keeps dogs, herds and signs, and who stands closest to me.
- The Marchwarden, who keeps the route, the fords and the song of the year, and who decides with the council where the band goes next.
- The Saltkeeper, who holds the stores of salt and dried meat for the winter and the lean, and who counts them by cord.
- The Runners, a company of young walkers who carry word from fire to fire and clan to clan, on foot, by day, with smoke by night for the plainest news.

When I am away they run things as they always have. A word of mine reaches the far end of the band through the Speaker to the clan heads, then by Runners from fire to fire, which takes a day or two across a band stretched along the trail. I speak at council, at dusk, from the edge of the firelight, and the council decides. I command no one.

Of those who carry a spear for them, when it comes to that: about nine hundred, one in five or six of the band, hunters and grown people who have held a spear in a hunt. Perhaps three hundred are hunters by trade and would stand together well. The rest are herders who would stand to protect the herds.

## How the god meets them
I do not come at the heart of the camp. I come at the edge, at dusk, to the dogs first, and I stand where the firelight ends. I meet the Houndwarden first and let her see me before the others, in the way I would want to be met, as a thing that came to the margin of the fire and waited to be asked in. I do not say what I am. I ask nothing and offer to watch the herds. The council hears me when it asks. I do not bring in tools or ways in my first season. If they ask, I tell them how a thing is made and they make it at their own pace.

Do the people hold their offices and know whose they are? Yes. The offices are theirs and they hold them; none of them is mine. They are not waiting for me. They have never heard of me. They are a people that walks, that has its own council, and that I come to find.

## Questions for you

1. How I know where my people are, on the move: their beasts are not them. The Listening never reads my people, but it reads their cattle, goats, sheep and dogs as the beasts they are. A walking band is a long moving line of herd and flock with an empty place among it, and that empty place is my people. I also know them by the trail beaten wide across the grass, by smoke, and by the fords and the songs. When I cannot find them by these, I go to the last ford and wait for a Runner.

2. What my herders make of the boldness of wild beasts near people, and who answers to the council: they take it as the country coming close, and as a plague and a gift. A boar that does not run, a wolf that lingers at the edge of the herd, a stag that grazes near the tents: the hunters say it is easy meat and the herders say it is a danger to the stock. They do not know it is mine, and I do not tell them early. The Houndwarden answers to the council for it, and keeps a count by cord of the beasts that grew bold, where, and what they took. If the matter grows heavy, I answer to the council for it myself, once they know whose it is. I will say the plain truth when asked.

3. What their dogs and herds tell them about me, and what they have made of it: the dogs fall silent and turn all one way, and the herd beasts lift their heads and face the same place. The Houndwarden sees it first, and has seen it as a sign of a wolf, a stranger, or an old thing in the country. She will say it is the dogs listening to something. The council has made of it an old omen: that the dogs know old things, and that when they turn, it is wise to light a fire and wait. They have not made of it a god. They will not know what the dogs face until they see me.
