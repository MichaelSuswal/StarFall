## Answer 1

A wide country at the hour before rain, when every herd has lifted its head at once and the grass lies over in a single long bow. Nothing is yet struck, and everything knows that it will be. I would have the world feel like that hour: watchful, fed by what is about to break, and honest about the breaking.

## Answer 2

Open country that is cut by timber. Wooded uplands that fall away into broad grazing plains, with rivers wandering through both and marsh in the low places where the game comes down to drink. I wish for ground that holds many kinds of living things within a day's walking, and that hides a patient watcher well.

## Answer 3

Let the seasons be four and honest, and let each be a little severe. A winter that thins the herds and teaches mortals what they owe to one another. A spring that comes in on storm, loud and flooding, so that the fields drink on the anger of the sky. A summer long enough to fatten what the winter spared. An autumn of running game and full larders, the season of the hunt rightly kept. I do not wish for mildness. A mild world forgets its debts.

When each new year finds me, I would wake into the first storms of spring, when the thaw is loud and the herds are moving.

I wake into spring.

## Answer 4

Some of each, and I would have the proportion lean toward the wild. Far country that no one has walked, where the beasts have never learned to fear a fire. And at its edges a few old kingdoms already rooted, that I may learn from them what ruling costs and what it breaks. I am wary of a world that is wholly settled, for I could not move freely in it, and wary of one wholly empty, for I would have no one to gather.

## Answer 5

Old, but not tidy. Several thousand years, with ruins under ruins, though most of what was built has gone soft into the earth and is remembered only in the shapes of hills and the names of fords. I would have mortals who half know that others walked here before them, and who are right to be a little humbled by it.

## Answer 6

Nearer the rumor than the wise woman in every valley, though never wholly a rumor. A thing that most doubt, that a few keep, and that now and then leaves a mark too plain to be denied. Let it be rare enough that a mortal who has it is watched, and common enough that no hunter laughs outright at a tracker who reads more than the ground.

## Answer 7

It should feel like listening. A pressure behind the eyes, a knowing of where the living thing is before it is seen, a borrowed quiet. And it should cost a mortal in kind: a portion of their own appetite, or sleep, or ease among their own kind, so that those who practise it are marked a little apart. Power that costs nothing is a theft from someone not yet named.

## Answer 8

Let a god stand out, but not as a lone lamp in a dark room. I would have gods be great, plainly greater than a sorcerer, and yet hindered by what they are, so that a patient and gathered people may give a god real trouble. I am content to be formidable. I am not content to be unanswerable. A judge who cannot be answered is no judge.

## Answer 9

Stranger than the familiar, though rooted in it. Let the common animals be as they are, and let there be, in the deep timber and the far marsh, creatures no one has a name for: old kinds that wear the shapes of the common ones wrongly, and herds that move by a logic of their own. I would have the hunter meet something at the edge of the map and be unable to say whether it is prey.

## Answer 10

The turning of a thing that was fed. Famine that comes after a good year. A pack that has lost its fear. A plague carried in the flesh of the beasts. A drought that the old stories said could not come. And, besides, the quiet kind: a neighbour who is patient, and who counts. That is the danger that makes a good story to me, the one that was invited and then forgot its manners.

## Answer 11

Salt, and the beasts that can be kept. Salt to hold the winter meat, and strong draught animals and breeding stock whose lines have not been spoiled. Let mortals trade for the first, fight over the second, and go without both in a bad year, and let them learn what it is to be a day from either.

## Answer 12

Bones of something enormous, lying in a long curve beneath a hill that the herds will not graze upon. Beside them old roads that run to no town, and, I think, one sealed door that no one has yet thought to open. I would like to find that the dead of this land were larger than the living, and that some of them were not killed.

## Answer 13

Yes. Not many, and not as mortals' mirrors. I would have peoples of the wood and the herd, who are neither beast nor human and who keep law of their own that mortals only half understand. Let them be proud, a little cruel, courteous in the matter of hospitality, and slow to forgive a debt left unpaid. I would be glad to be judged by some of them.

## Answer 14

A few. Let there be travellers who came a long way, few enough that they are remembered by name, and who brought a method or a tool that no one here had thought to make. Let them not be saviours. Let them be guests whom the land must decide how to treat.

## Answer 15

A knowing at close range, a pull that is felt only when the two are near, as a beast knows another beast in the dark by the way the air is held. Distance should keep us strangers, and I would prefer it so. A being who can feel every other from any distance has no privacy and no mystery, and a god that cannot hide is half unmade.

## Answer 16

One body, as a rule. Several only at a cost that is felt: that each new body should be a smaller thing, thinner in its judgement, and that the one who spreads should lose something of the sense of which of them is truly itself. I would take the weight of a single body gladly, and I would not ask for more than one.

## Answer 17

A tracker who becomes a judge. A mortal of no great station who sees a wrong plainly, follows it patiently across hard ground, and is unmoved by the rank of the one who did it. Let the land produce a few of these, and let some of them be inconvenient to gods.

## Answer 18

A companion, and I would not know it was one for some time. Something that follows at a distance for a long while, as the wild ones do, and comes in only when it has decided I am safe. I would also like to discover what the dead of this land were afraid of, since I think it is close.

## Answer 19

That it should hold a company. That a gathered people, many and various and bound by no fear of me, might walk through this world without being driven, and that when the storm came it should feed them rather than flatten them. And I would ask, though I do not expect it, that the death at the beginning of me be answered in some way that I will recognise when I see it.
