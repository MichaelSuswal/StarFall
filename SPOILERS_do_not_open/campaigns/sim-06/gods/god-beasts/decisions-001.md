# Decisions: god-beasts, Year 1

I stand at the wood's edge on a beaten trail that runs north to south. I hold that my people are on the move between the high wood and the grass in spring, and that I will find them by the line of their herds. I may be wrong in where they are, and the order below bends to what the Listening shows.

1. Spring, in the first light of the landing morning. I stay at the wood's edge, low in the timber, and stand still with my attention set outward. I listen at the wood's edge for as long as it takes to read the whole of my reach, about three miles through timber and about ten over the open plain to the east, and I take note of where the gatherings are, how large, which way they move, and whether they graze, rest, hunt or flee. I look for one thing above the rest: a long moving line of cattle, goats, sheep and dogs with an empty place along it, which is how my people read to me. I break the listening between each reading with a breath of effort, and I do not listen while any wolf or person is near enough to reach me unheard. Reason: I do not walk blind into a country I have not read, and I cannot be sought until I know where to be found.

2. Spring, the first three days. I go by the wood's edge along the beaten trail, never in the middle of it and never in open grass by day, toward whichever way the Listening shows the herd line. If it shows none, I go south along the trail toward the wet hollow and the lower ground, since the spring round comes down behind the first storms to the grass, and the trail is worn by many feet and hooves. I stop to listen at every rise and every ford, once for each half day's walk. I carry my staff and my horn, and I do not sound the horn: it would tell every person in earshot where I am and it would pull every beast off its ground in anger, and I am the one who would be missed before met. I keep timber at my back. I speak with no beast on the way unless I must ask water, and then with one beast and few breaths, since each beast I speak with loses its fear of me and of the people near me and stays so.

3. Spring, the day I read the herd line. I settle a day's walk off, on ground where a watcher would pass me by, and I watch their camp by the Listening and by my own eyes from the timber until I know where the fires are, which way the dogs face, and when the dusk meal ends. I do not use the Listening within the camp itself for people, since it will not read them, and I do not use it to spy on the band. I count the herd and the dogs by it, and no more. Reason: a shepherd counts the flock from the margin, and does not count its speech.

4. Spring, the dusk after, and again each dusk until I am met. I come to the camp's edge from downwind of the dogs, where the firelight ends, and I stand still. The dogs will fall silent and turn to face me, and the herd beasts will lift their heads, since that is what I am. I let them. I wait for the Houndwarden, whom I hope is a lean, watchful woman of middle years who reads the dogs first, and I let her come to me and see me before any other. I say nothing of what I am and nothing of what I can do, and I do not speak first. When she speaks, I answer in a low even voice, formal, and I tell her the plain truth if she asks it: that I am not of the band, that I came to the margin of the fire, and that I will keep the herds if the band wishes it. I ask nothing. If she bids me away, or if any hand goes to a spear, I withdraw into the timber without a word and I do not return the same night. I keep a way out behind me at every fire, and I stand so that the fire lies between me and the dogs' nearest rush. If I am held, I do not break the hold by force, since a body held is not broken, and I will wait for the council's word.

5. Spring and the first of summer, once I am asked in. Through the Houndwarden, I offer to watch the herds at night on the edge of the camp, where the wolves come, and I do it. I keep the margin. I tell the Houndwarden that I will answer at the Council of the Fire when it asks me, from the edge of the firelight, and that I do not wish to sit in the ring. I ask her to carry to the Speaker only this, in my words: "A keeper has come to the edge of the fire and will stand where the light ends. It asks nothing. It will answer what the council asks of it." I wait for the council to ask. I do not press. If the council asks what I am, I tell the plain truth: that I am one of the keepers they speak of, that I came to lead them as I am able and that I will be heard at council and no more. I tell them the boldness of wild beasts near the tents may be my doing where I have spoken with them, and I ask the Houndwarden to keep a count by cord of every beast grown bold, where, and what it took, and I answer to the council for that count. I do not bring tools or ways in this season. If they ask how a thing is made, I tell them, and they make it at their own pace.

6. Summer, at the river under its line of trees and in the long fattening season. I walk the margin of the band's ground each night and learn the country with them: where the game comes down to drink, which beasts keep which ground, where the old roads run to no town, and whether any neighbor hunts our range and counts our herds. I ask at the Marchwarden's fire about the fords and the songs, since the songs are their map, and I listen to the song of each ford, and I take it into my own knowing. I ask no beast anything that I can learn from the Marchwarden or the hunters, and I speak with beasts only where the herds are threatened, and then I tell the Houndwarden afterward. I hold my temper. If I judge a wrong done within the band's ground, I weigh it across the season before I say it to the council and I take no trail at all this year unless the wrong is plain and the doer is not of the people I mean to keep. A storm cannot be called back and a trail cannot be put down, and I am not yet known to them.

7. Autumn and the first of winter, the hunt in the timber and the going up. I stand with the hunters at the hunt's edge as one who watches the beasts and tells the Houndwarden where the game lies, once the council has asked it of me, and I ask that the hunt keep its season: no doe with young taken, and no beast killed for its hide alone. I set no share on the flat stone for myself, and I do not look to see who leaves it. I go up with the band to the high wood for the lean of winter and I keep the margin of the winter camp. I watch who counts and who rules, what the Speaker carries and what the council will not hear, and I learn what ruling costs by watching the council pay it. At the end of the year I hold my place at the edge of the firelight, with the horn still at my side, and a way out behind me, and I wait to be missed.

## Messages
None. Anything I mean to say goes to the Houndwarden's ear in person at the camp's edge, and I send no word by messenger or horn this year.

## What my people are instructed to do
Nothing yet. No office holder has heard me or taken my word, and I hold no one to anything. I ask only, through the Houndwarden once I am met, that the band keep its own round and its own code, that the Houndwarden keep the cord count of bold beasts, and that the council hear me when it chooses and not before.

## For the Keeper to rule
1. I do not use any power in a way my card does not plainly cover. I use The Listening as the card states, and I hold that it reads the band's cattle, goats, sheep and dogs as gatherings and singles, and that my own people are the empty place within them. I ask the Keeper to rule what the empty place reads as to me when a few of my people stand a little apart from the herd, and whether a single herder with a dog reads as a dog alone.
2. I do not sound the horn, speak with beasts beyond need, take a trail, or call a storm this year unless a plain wrong forces it. If a beast I must speak with grows bold, the Keeper rules the boldness as the card states.
3. I try to grow no power this year.
