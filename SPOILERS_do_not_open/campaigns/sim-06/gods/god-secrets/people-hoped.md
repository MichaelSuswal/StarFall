# People: unnamed

I give them no name and their home none. I have no name of my own to give, and a name given to a people by a stranger is a thing they would have to carry. Let them name themselves, and their town, in play. What I leave unnamed stays unnamed.

## Who they are
Mortal folk of the common kind. Traders, boatmen, granary keepers, fishers, reed cutters, tanners, coopers, a few smiths in bronze, and the farmers who work the firm ground inside the river bend and bring in the harvest. They are a mixed people who came to the bend over many years from river and marsh and upland, and they hold together by trade and by the walls, not by one blood. It is an old town. They would say it has always been there.

## How many
About five thousand. Most live within the walls, gathered close. Several hundred live outside them on the marsh edges and the boats, and are counted on the council's rolls all the same.

## How they live
In a walled town on the firm ground inside a slow bend of a river, with wet ground, sedge and standing water at the edges. A wall of earth and timber, a river gate and a land gate, a market street that runs between them, and a granary that is the largest building in the town. They farm the firm ground, fish and cut reed in the marsh, and carry goods on the river. Their days are the market, the boats, the fields and the granary. In autumn they bring in the harvest and fill the store. In winter they stay in, mend, talk and keep their books. In spring they test the stores and open the river. The town talks all winter and learns what it is.

## Their code
They honor a weighed bargain and a sealed word: a thing agreed before witnesses and marked is kept, and a man who breaks it is not trusted at the market until he has made it good. They honor the guest at the door, who is fed and left unharmed for three days. They keep their own books and are not asked to show them.
The worst of them would still not sell a neighbor's child into debt, foul the common well, or burn the granary. These are oaths, and they can be broken. A people is not a guarantee, and I do not ask for one.

## Skills and culture
Ordinary, and free. Keeping count with tally sticks and with marks on clay or wax, weights and scales, river boats of every size, reed and willow work, salting and smoking fish, brewing, bronze and leather and wool, walls and ditches, a market with set days and a set peace within it. Reading is rare, and the clerks of the tally are the people who have it. They are fond of old things that nobody can fully read, and they argue about them.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None asked. I take no real edge over other peoples, so I offer no weakness against one. A habit of my people, that they are slow to be fooled in a bargain, I take as a temper: who they are, and able to fail, and it will fail them when the bargain is made by someone they like.

## Their magic
A few, in the alleys and out on the marsh edge. I say five or six hedge-witches are known by name, in a town of this size, and nobody says so at the market. What each does is small and by simple means: one eases a fever with a drink of marsh herbs and a night's sitting up, one finds a strayed beast or a lost child by walking with a thread and a bowl of water, one sets a bone, one gets a stubborn hearth to catch, one lays a breech birth right. Each pays for it, as I would have it paid: a rough patch in the voice, a season of heavy sleep, a little warmth gone out of the hands that does not wholly come back. None can do a thing that could not in the end be done by hand and patience, and none works every day. I name no one among them. The Keeper fits their magic to the world.

## What they believe about gods
On the morning before I come: that gods are far off and do not much mind the affairs of a town, and that a prudent house owes them nothing and asks nothing, and does not speak ill of them. Some leave a cup or a crust at the river in autumn, and call it manners, not worship. Some say the marsh has its own odd folk who deal by night, and that these are the nearest to gods that anyone has seen. If a god came, they say, it would be a stranger who counts, and you would know it by the way the books come right.

## Rule and worship
Rule: a council of elders and headmen, as I lean, with no change. The heads of the large families, the headmen of the boat families and the wardens of the granary and the gate sit at one long table. They number about nine, and one of them is Speaker for a year. I am heard there. I do not command and I do not have a vote, and I take that as the arrangement I want. Worship: none. They ask me nothing and I ask nothing, and what they do about me is theirs.

## Hierarchy
The council advises and rules. Under it, a small bench of three elders, one for the fields, one for the boats and one for the market, settles the rest: a boundary, a marriage gone bad, a debt, a theft. They sit on market day at the gate. A death, and any matter touching the council's own, is heard by the whole council. I advise, when I am asked, and I am usually asked last.

## Helpers
Offices that run things when I am absent: the Clerk of the Tally, who keeps the council's books and the rolls of who is entered; the Granary Warden; the Gate Warden, who commands the watch; the Boat Master, who carries word and goods along the river; and the Crier, who calls the council's decisions at the market. They carry out what the council agrees. When I am away, the Clerk of the Tally keeps my place at the table and carries only what I left in the clerks' hands, no more. Orders travel by tally stick and sealed wax, carried by runners in town and by the Boat Master's crews on the river, and cried at the market for the town. A word of mine reaches the far end of the people, the boat families on the marsh, in a few days by water.
How many carry a spear, when it comes to that: about six hundred, of whom a hundred or so are the standing watch under the Gate Warden, trained and fed from the common store. The rest are householders and boatmen who keep their own spear and are called by the Crier and the council's word.

## How the god meets them
I come on the worn track in autumn, on foot, as a stocky stranger in a plain brown coat, with a purse of scrap silver and a key at my neck, toward the smoke of the walled town, at the time of the harvest. The hinge: they hold their offices and know whose they are. They were not waiting for me, and have never heard of me. I come to a people that is already a people. The council's clerks take me for a clever trader and a guest, who is fed for three days, and I am entered as the guest. I sit at the granary and look at the books, I say true things about them, and over the first days the council learns that I am useful and that I keep my counsel. I say no more of myself than I am asked, and I am hard to cheat.

## Questions for you

1. Who counts as my own people for the blind place: those on the council's rolls, kept by the Clerk of the Tally: every household entered in the town and every boat family and marsh household that has been entered, wherever they happen to be standing. Not a guest, a traveller or a stranger staying within the walls who is not yet on the rolls. A person is mine from the day the Clerk writes them in, and not before. I prefer a line the Keeper can read in a book on any day.

2. If a kept thing touches the council's business, I say nothing of it and do nothing that would reveal it, and I do not bargain with it. I ask for the council to hear the other side first, I ask the Granary Warden or the Clerk for the figures, I ask for a night's delay, and I put the questions that would let the council reach the truth by its own work. I do not lie about the matter, and I do not vote on it, since I do not vote. If the matter cannot wait and I cannot speak, I leave the table, which is easy for me, and I know it is cowardice that I call caution. Privately, I ask the teller to release me from it, and I do not press.

3. The clerks write: "the stranger", with the day, the bench he sat on, and the thing he said or was asked. If I am asked for a name for the book, I give none, and the Clerk of the Tally draws a small empty ring in the place where a name would go, and the ring becomes my mark, and the town comes to call me by it. They may call me "friend" in the market and I let them.
