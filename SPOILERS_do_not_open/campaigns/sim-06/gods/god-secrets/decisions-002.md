# Decisions, Year 2: god-secrets

I am the stranger the town calls the Counter. I act as the man throughout and I do not change shape this year unless a thing I cannot foresee forces it. The key stays on its cord at my neck and the tally stick in my coat. I hold the stool at the foot of the long table on the stores, and I am not on the rolls.

1. Autumn, the harvest month. I go to the Granary Warden in the granary yard, with the Clerk of the Tally standing by if the Warden wishes it, and I offer him a bargain in his own terms. The count aloud last year ran a little under a twentieth short of his book. I ask nothing of him for it and I do not call it his fault. I propose that this autumn the harvest be carried in and counted aloud by his own people, before witnesses, as the first sacks go in and again when the store is closed, with a pair of lads set on the damp corner and on the rats so that if the loss is rats and damp the count shows it where it happens. I ask the Clerk to put the figures to tally stick as they are cried, and I ask the Crier to cry the closing count at market as the Granary Warden's count. I ask the Warden to take the credit. I do this because a loss he is allowed to find and mend is a loss he will keep finding, and a loss I find for him is one he will hide. I bring no figure of my own to the council on this. I carry it in the Warden's name or I do not carry it.

2. Autumn, at the first sitting of the council after the harvest is in. When someone asks again who is the man who will not give a name, I answer for myself, if I am asked at the table or if the Speaker lets me, in a sentence: "I have none. I had one once, I think, and I have not found it. Call me what you like, and I will answer to what is kindly meant." I say it plainly and with a small smile and I add nothing. I do not tell them where I came from beyond the south-west track, and I do not lie about it. I let the Clerk's ring and the scales stand as my marks. I do this because the question went unanswered last year and the Clerk answered for me, and a stranger who answers for himself once, shortly, is easier to trust than one who is always answered for.

3. Winter. I keep to the walls and talk. I sit at the granary, the gate and the market bench, and I let long conversation do what it does. I ask the Gate Warden to a cup, in the market street and in daylight, and I ask him to put any question he likes to me. I answer what I can truthfully and say so where I cannot. I offer him a count of the watch's rations from the common store, a weighing of what the standing watch eats and what it costs, since he feeds a hundred from the store and may not have seen it in one figure. I do this so that he has seen me do him a service and no harm. I do not ask him for anything in return. I hold the one kept thing as I hold it, I take no new one that I would not be glad to carry, and if a teller begins a telling I do not wish to keep I say so before it is done. I do not press anyone. I tell the Keeper at the next release how many I hold.

4. Spring, when the river opens, at the river gate. The loaded boats grounded twice last spring at the dredged gate and the lowest cellars took water again. I go to the Boat Master first, then to the Clerk, and I ask the Boat Master, as a trader asking after his own trade, what the two groundings cost in cargo and in days, and I put it into one figure. I offer the Clerk to set that figure beside what the dredging cost the town. I bring both to the council at the long table, if I am asked, and I say only what the figures say, and I say whose they are. I ask the Boat Master's crews to sound the channel with poles at the gate at low water and at high, and I ask the Clerk to put the depths on tally sticks so that the next time the council argues dredging it argues from a count. I do this because a town that talks all winter should have its figures when it talks. I do not go into the cellars and I do not use the key on any of them. The people's hidings are closed to me, and the cellars are theirs.

5. Summer. I go down the river, not up the track. I ask the Boat Master for a berth on one of his crews' boats as a weigher and counter, paid in scrap silver for my passage and my keep, and I go for the better part of a month, as the man, with the key at my neck and the tally stick in my coat. I go to see who keeps the water below the town, who trades on it, what comes up it, and whether any god, or any stranger who might be one, has a hand in it. I tell the Clerk of the Tally only that I mean to be back before the harvest and I leave nothing in his hands but that. I do not search any hidden place in any people's town that is not mine, and I do not use the key for anything on the river unless I am standing on open ground and have a reason that would pass at the table. I come back before the harvest of the third year and look first at the store. I do this to know the world around my town, and to see whether I am looked for this time.

6. Autumn again, on my return, if the Clerk will have it. I ask the Clerk, over a cup and as idle talk, whether I might sit with the old league's tallies in his house for an evening and count what can be counted of them, the notches and not the meaning, as he sets the terms. I say plainly that I do not ask for the list of names and I will not ask for it. I say that if he wishes me to leave at any hour, I leave. I do this because the figures are my trade and the old tallies are the kind of thing a counter might be asked to look at, and because a kept book that is shown by the one who keeps it is better than a kept book that is taken.

## Messages

None. I send no word to anyone out of my sight this year. I have no one to send it to, and no one who knows my name.

## For the Keeper to rule

1. The sky. The star dimmed on three nights and many in the town tied it to me. I did nothing I know of on those nights. If the town asks me, I say I do not know, which is true. I ask for no ruling and I note it so the Keeper knows what I will say.

I do not try to grow any power this year.
