# Decisions, Year 1: god-secrets

I have no name. I am the stranger in a plain brown coat. Everything below is meant to be done as the man unless it says otherwise. The key stays on its cord at my neck and the tally stick in my coat.

1. Autumn, at first light. I stay on the rise where I woke and watch before I walk. I look at the smoke of the town a mile to the north-east, at who uses the worn track and which way they carry what, at the river to the north-west and what moves on it, and at the mountains and the far plain so that I can say later what I saw and not what I was told. I do this because I flinch from walking into a room I have not looked at, and I will call it prudence. I do not change shape. A change costs me a day of slowness and a word, and I have no reason yet to pay it.

2. Autumn, mid morning of the same day. I walk the track to the land gate as the man and come to the Gate Warden's watch, openly, as a trader with a purse of scrap silver and brass weights and nothing to hide but myself. I ask for the guest's three days, which the people honor, and I take it. I give no name. If I am asked for one for the book I say I have none, plainly and with a small smile, and I let the Clerk of the Tally draw whatever mark he likes. I ask for a bench in the market street or the granary yard to sit on, and I pay in scrap silver for my food and a bed so that I owe nobody and am a guest and not a charge. I say little of where I came from, and I do not lie about it: I came by the track from the south-west, and I am a stranger who counts.

3. Autumn, the three days of guest right. I ask the Granary Warden, and then the Clerk of the Tally, to let me look at the books of the harvest and the store, since I am a trader and a counter and would like to see how a town this size keeps its count. I say true things about the figures, small ones, and only when asked or when a number is plainly wrong. I tell the Granary Warden, through the Clerk if he prefers, what I would tell any warden: that the harvest should be carried in and the store counted aloud before the first hard frost, and that a count cried at the market is believed in a way a count kept in a book is not. I ask the Crier and the Clerk to cry the store's count to the council when it sits. I ask this as a trader asking for a favor in a bargain, and I do not command anyone. I meet the office holders one at a time and in private where I can, the Clerk of the Tally first, then the Granary Warden, then the Gate Warden, the Boat Master and the Crier, and I offer each an honest service in exchange for a hearing: a count, a weighing, a second pair of eyes on a ledger. No office is mine until its holder has heard me and taken my word, and I will not ask for that before I have earned it.

4. Autumn, late, after the three days. I ask to stay on past guest right by hiring a bed and paying for it with counting and weighing work, and I ask the Clerk to bring me before the council as a guest who has done the town a service, not as a petitioner. I ask for a place on a bench at the long table, not a seat and not a vote. I do not ask to be entered on the rolls this year. I would have the town decide that of its own choosing and in its own time. My purpose here is to be heard on the granary and the winter's stores, and to see who among the nine speaks last and who speaks first.

5. Winter. I stay inside the walls and talk. I sit at the granary and the gate and the market bench and I let long conversation do what it does, since people tell me more than they meant to and I remember to whom. I keep every such thing to myself and do not trade on it. I look for one person who might one day say a true thing to me and have it stay said, and I do not press. If someone begins a telling in trust and I do not wish to hold it, I say so before the telling is done. If I take one in, I keep it absolutely, and I will tell the Keeper in the next year how many I hold. Once, on a night in the dead of the winter, I take the dog's shape in my own room, leave my coat and key on the bed where I can fetch them, and walk the alleys and the marsh edge to learn where the hedge-witches' houses are, what the boat families do after dark, and where the dogs of the town sleep. I come back to my room, take the man's shape, and pay the day of slowness and the lost words. I do this once and no more this winter. I ask the Keeper to rule on nothing in it.

6. Spring, when the river opens. I take the bronze key in my hand and walk the open ground, not the houses: the rise where I woke, the verge of the worn track for a mile in each direction, and any square of public ground the council or the Clerk says I may use. I do this to learn whether anything has been put in the ground on purpose near the town, an old cache, a sealed cellar, a road that was closed. I stay out of every house, yard and storehouse of the people, and I would not search the council's or its helpers' places at all. I ask the Clerk what old things the town has that no one can fully read, and whether the rolls hold any name I might ask about, and I ask it as idle talk over a cup. If I find a hidden place on open ground I do not open it. I note where it is and what size, and I leave it.

7. Summer. I leave the town for about a fortnight, on foot as the man, down the worn track to the south-west, past the first places I come to, as a trader with his scales, to see who keeps the road, who trades on it, what the plain country holds, and who else, if anyone, comes up it. I do this to learn the world around my town and to be missed a little for as long as I am away. I tell the Clerk of the Tally only that I mean to be back by the harvest and leave nothing in his hands beyond that. I come back before the first harvest of the second year, and I look first at the store.

## Messages

None. I send no word to anyone out of my sight this year. I have no one to send it to, and no one who knows my name.

## For the Keeper to rule

1. My own people. My card says I may never search the hidings of my own people, the council and its helpers among them. I do not know whether the people of the town count as mine before the Clerk enters me or them on the rolls. I will treat all of them as blind to me this year and search only open ground, which is the narrower reading and the one I choose. I ask the Keeper to rule if that reading is wrong and to tell me only the outcome.

2. Dog shape and the key. I leave the key on my person's clothes in my room when I take the dog's shape in action 5. My card says the dog form does not work with the key and that anyone who picks it up while I am a dog holds the power. I am leaving it in a room I have hired and nobody has been told is mine to enter. I ask the Keeper to say whether that counts as left where another could take it, and I accept whatever the answer costs.

I do not try to grow any power this year.
