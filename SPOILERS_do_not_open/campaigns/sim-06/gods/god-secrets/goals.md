# Goals

Held now, at the opening of the fourth year, in order of weight.

1. Keep the store counted by the town, in the town's own hands. The far run was filled this year by the sack before a tally-keeper of each family, and the whole store squared. The gap is named: 3,320 sacks, a third of the roll. Both heads say the roll is wrong, and neither says where the grain went. I do not ask. The Warden keeps his office and his face, and nobody was put out. I want the same count made again this autumn the same way, so that it becomes the town's custom and not my proposal. A custom that does not need me is worth more than a favor that does.

2. Be heard by the offices one at a time, and let the Clerk decide what he wants to hear. The Clerk gave me an evening with the old league's tallies and I read two of them as their makers would have. One of them is a count of grain and salt owed by the old factory here to a salt house of the delta in the far west, never struck off. I have told no one. It is a debt that may or may not be live, a house of the delta that may or may not exist, and a thing that could cost the town. It is not a kept thing, and I read it with my own hand, so I may speak it. But a count the Clerk did not ask for is a gift with a hook in it. I mean to ask him first whether he wishes me to say what the stick says, and to say it only if he does. The Gate Warden asked nothing of me and I ask nothing of him. The Boat Master took the weighing. No office is mine until its holder has heard me and taken my word.

3. Stay one large merchant among many. Be useful in the books, say true things about them, and be asked last. The town ties the dimmed star to me, and the count rose again to six. I cannot mend that by speaking, and I will not feed it. I say I do not know, because it is true.

4. Hold what I hold. I carry one kept thing, told to me in the dead of a winter. It binds me entirely and I will not bend toward it. The reed cutter's daughter asked whether I would hear from her what her father began, and I told her to keep it until she knows whether it is hers to give. I stand by that. I take no new telling that I would not be glad to carry, and I leave the door as it was. Of twelve places I hold one.

5. Learn the ground further, and the tale of the Guest. I know the river from the head to the mouth, the coasting boats, and that a harbor town lies west along the coast. I have heard at the head of the river of a woman with a staff and a key whom the pass herders feed. I want to see the ground between the landing and the pass with my own eyes, and hear it again from someone who has been nearer than a drover. I do not intend to knock on a hall door. I would sooner be found at my own table than find one on the road, and I will not pretend that is not fear. I will go as far as the flinch lets me and write down where it stopped me.

6. Do not ask yet about a lost name. The old league's walkers sing a private list of names, twenty and some unlike any on the rolls. I have not asked for it, and the Clerk has not offered it. The tally I read does not name me. I claim nothing. A name here may be mine or may not, and I would sooner leave it unclaimed than take one that belongs to another.

7. Be missed a little by someone when I am away. I was gone a month this year on the river. At the head no one looked for me. Whether the town did I will know on the store first and the faces after. I will tell the Clerk when I am going and for how long, and leave him a day to be back by. A person who knows when you are due can notice when you are late.

8. Do not use the key where the people live. Their hidings are closed to me, the granary among them. If a finding is wanted in the town, I do not do it.
