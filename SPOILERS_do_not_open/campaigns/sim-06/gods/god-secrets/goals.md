# Goals

Held now, at the opening of the fifth year, in order of weight.

1. Let the count become the town's and not mine. It has now been made at the door three autumns, and the last two the same way: by the sack, a tally-keeper of each far-run family cutting a stick, the whole store squared, and the closing count cried as the Warden's. It stood at 38,500. Nobody asked where the old grain went and nobody said. That silence is a bargain I did not make and will not break. The test of a custom is whether it runs when I am not at the door. This autumn I do not stand at the door. If it is done the same way without me, it is theirs.

2. Be heard by the offices one at a time, and let each decide what it wants to hear. The Clerk wished to hear what the old stick says, and I gave him the figures in the winter. He has told no one. It is his book and his to open, and I will not ask him whether he has. The Warden took the count to the council himself. The Boat Master took the weighing. No office is mine until its holder has heard me and taken my word, and I will not hurry it.

3. Help the town argue from counts, not from grievance. The farming houses say they paid for the boat houses' river. The boat houses borrowed their share from two houses at the table. That is a ledger with two honest books and no common one. I will not say who is right. I will put the dredging's cost, the groundings' cost, and who paid, onto sticks the Clerk keeps, so that the council has one book to argue from. If it is not asked for, I say nothing at the table.

4. Stay one large merchant among many, and be asked last. The town ties the dimmed star to me and the count rose to seven. I cannot mend that by speaking and will not feed it. What I can do is offer the town a count of its own: the Clerk cutting a stick for each night, with the month and the hours, so that the star becomes something the town watches and keeps, not something it charges to me. I say I do not know, because it is true.

5. Hold what I hold. I carry one kept thing, told to me in the dead of a winter. It binds me entirely and I will not bend toward it. The reed cutter's daughter has not asked again. If she does, I say what I said: keep it until you know whether it is yours to give. I take no new telling that I would not be glad to carry, and I leave the door as it was. Of twelve places I hold one.

6. Be missed a little by someone when I am away, and be present when I am not. I was gone about five weeks in the summer and was back before the day I named to the Clerk. This year I stay. A person who is always at the table and is missed only when gone is worth less than one who is sometimes away and found right where he said. I do not yet know which I am. I will learn it by staying one summer and watching who comes to the bench.

7. The ground and the Guest. I know the river from head to mouth, the coasting boats, and the plain's edge and out onto the grass for twelve days. I have not come within many days of the pass. I do not intend to knock on a hall door. I would sooner be found at my own table than find one on the road, and I will not pretend that is not fear. I leave the pass for a later year and write down that I chose to.

8. Do not ask yet about a lost name. The old league's walkers sing a private list of names, twenty and some unlike any on the rolls. I have not asked for it, and the Clerk has not offered it. The tally I read does not name me. I claim nothing.

9. Do not use the key where the people live. Their hidings are closed to me, the granary among them. If a finding is wanted in the town, I do not do it.
