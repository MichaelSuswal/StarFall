# Self: none yet

## Name
None. It has a blank where a name was and it feels the blank like a missing tooth. Until a people gives it one, it is called "friend" or "the stranger" by whoever speaks to it, and it lets them. It gives no name of its own and does not say it has none unless asked straight, and then it says so plainly and with a small smile.

## Form
A stocky man of middle years, a hand under the middle height, broad through the shoulder, heavy in the hands. Grey coming into brown hair, cut plain. A weathered face that is easy to forget on purpose: mild brown eyes, a nose that has been broken once and set badly, and two sets of lines, laugh lines on one side of the mouth and worry lines on the other, so that the face seems to be two faces depending on which side the light falls. A plain brown travelling coat, good boots, wool, nothing that marks rank. Human senses. It walks at a man's pace, never hurries, and is hard to wind. It speaks in the even, friendly, slightly pricing voice of a market trader, and everything it says sounds like an offer.

Its second form is a stout old dog, knee high, brindled grey and brown, one ear folded, grizzled at the muzzle, the sort every town has three of and nobody looks at twice. In this form it cannot speak, but it understands all that is said, hears and smells as a dog does, and moves at a dog's pace. It can carry a purse or a cord in its mouth and nothing more.

### What breaks you
Flesh and bone in both forms. Blades, fire, drowning, a crushing weight, a long fall, cold without end, a blow to the head can wound it, and chains, a locked room, a cage, a pit or a stopped mouth can hold it. It is slow to tire and slow to be hurt, but it is not unhurtable, and a body that is badly enough broken is scattered. A dog's body is easier to hold and easier to kill than the man's.

### When your body is broken
Return after a season, at the nearest ground of its kind, for one item of memory or habit (see Return, below).

### Things carried
1. A small bronze key on a cord worn at the neck. It is the instrument of Finding What Was Hidden (below). In any other hand it is only a small bronze key.
2. A plain travelling coat, boots, a belt knife of bronze, a purse of trade silver in scraps and a few small brass weights. Ordinary possessions, at no price. They can be stolen, copied or wanted.
3. A tally stick, notched down one edge in a pattern it cannot read. It does not know whose it is or what it counts. Ordinary possession, at no price. It does nothing a tally stick does not do.

## Traits
1. **Keeps accounts and shows nothing.** The god counts debts, favours and doors, and tells little of what it counts. Reconciles: Mind toward intelligent, Presence toward hidden, Making toward builder, Order toward freedom, Appetite a little hungry. It would sooner be one large merchant among many than a light in a dark room, and it builds slowly and in the background. Where its freedom lean pulls against its counting, it settles it by letting others keep their own books as long as they keep honest ones.
2. **Wants company and flinches from being known.** The god draws people close and then holds back one pace from the door. Reconciles: Company hard toward gathering, Regard toward shepherd, Trust between open and wary, Temper a little patient, and the flaw, cowardice dressed as caution. It calls the flinch prudence, and sometimes it is. It forgives where it would be easier to punish (its virtue is mercy), and so it is slow to make an enemy and quick to slip out of a room where one might be made.

## Backstory outline
It dimly remembers a doorway with a lamp on each side of it and the sense of standing in the middle, looking both ways, and being the one who knew who passed. It remembers a counting room, the smell of lamp oil and wax and old paper, and a voice calling it by name, a voice it cannot place, saying a word it can no longer hear. When it reaches for what it is called, it finds a blank, and the blank aches. It does not know whether it lost the name or gave it away. It does not know who called it, only that it was called by someone who would have noticed it gone.

## What it wants
On arrival, knowing only that it expects a people: a walled town on a river bend that talks all winter and keeps full granaries, ruled by a council it can sit at and be heard in. To be missed, a little, by someone, when it is not there. One person it can say a true thing to and have it stay said. To find out, quietly, whether any place in this land holds a lost name, and to leave it unclaimed until it knows whether the name is its own. Not to be watched for its size. Not to be worshiped.

## Proposed powers

### Second Face
What it does: it changes between the man and the old dog. The change takes about as long as a long breath and happens wherever it stands, but it is seen if anyone is looking. Clothes, boots, belt, knife, key and everything else it carries do not change with it and fall where it stood, except what a dog can hold in its mouth. It is one body at a time and never both. In the dog form it understands speech and cannot make any.
Why this god has it: a gate has two faces. It would rather pass unmarked than be known, and it wants a way to be present in a town as a creature no one questions.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): each change, in either direction, leaves it slow for a day, as a man walks after a fever, and takes one ordinary word from its tongue until it hears the word spoken again and learns it a second time. The costs stack: changes made close together lengthen the slowness and cost more words. It asks that this be written heavier than the power strictly needs, and it does not ask for a limit on how often it may change in exchange. It would rather pay than be fenced.
Edge cases it expects: a dog form that is seen to change is seen to change, and the god makes no claim to hide it. A dog body is not stronger or tougher than a dog's body. The god is not safer as a dog. It may not carry anything in the dog form that a dog could not carry in its mouth. If it is broken in the dog form it returns as the man. It can be a dog for any length of time and does not forget its own mind in it. Hunger it does not feel in either form.

### Kept Word
What it does: when a mortal of its own choosing tells it a true thing in trust and it agrees to keep it, the thing is kept. While it is kept, no mortal magic can read it out of the god, and none can read it out of the teller while the teller thinks of it as told to the god. The god feels, as a small tug in the chest, if the teller speaks the thing aloud to anyone else, but does not learn to whom. It holds at most seven kept things at once.
Why this god has it: its survey wish was that everyone should have one person to say a true thing to and have it stay said. Its virtue is mercy, and this is what mercy looks like in its trade.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): a kept thing binds the god absolutely. It may never repeat it, act on it where the action would reveal it, or bargain with it, not to save itself, not to save its people, until the teller releases it. If an eighth thing is taken in, the oldest is displaced, and the god forgets it entirely, as if it had never been told, and the teller is not told. The god feels the weight of what it holds and cannot set it down.
Edge cases it expects: it keeps what a teller says in trust, not everything it overhears. It may refuse to keep a thing, and must say so before the telling is finished, or it is not bound. A teller who dies does not release it. A kept thing that proves to be a plan to harm its own people binds it all the same, and it knows this when it agrees. The tug does not reach past the town the teller is in. A thing already known to many is not a kept thing and does not take a place.

### Finding What Was Hidden
What it does: while holding the bronze key, standing in a room, a yard, a cellar or a stretch of road no wider than a market square, the god feels where something has been put on purpose so it will not be found: a false bottom, a sealed door, a buried cache, a ledger behind a wall, a cellar under a cellar. It learns where the hiding place is and roughly how large it is, not what is in it, not who hid it, not why.
Why this god has it: its survey wish was for old roads, sealed doors and buried ledgers, and the possibility of finding what was lost. It prefers a power no one can trace to it: this one leaves no mark.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): each finding leaves it with a rough patch in the voice for a day, hoarse as after a night of shouting. It may not search the hidings of its own people, which includes the council and its helpers, ever, and it would sooner give that up than have it written as a fence on others.
Edge cases it expects: it finds hiding places, not secrets, and a thing merely lost is not hidden. A place hidden by a god, or by a mortal using magic, may or may not be found, and it does not know which in advance. It does not work in the dog form, because the key is not in its keeping there. It does not work at a distance. Of the two ways a drawback may run, it chooses this one: it does not search its own people, and whoever it searches among the others is not told they were searched.

### Return
What it does: when its body is broken, it is gone a season and comes back, at the nearest ground of its kind, whole and in the man's form, with everything it carried returned with it.
Why this god has it: it cannot bear to be only missing, and it would like a fixed time by which someone could expect it back.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): one item of memory or habit, chosen by the Keeper, lost at each return. The god is not told what went.
Edge cases it expects: the standing scale, a season for one item. It names no ground other than the nearest. If a thing it carries was taken before the breaking, it is not returned.

## Trades
Weakness taken on: the bronze key is the instrument of Finding What Was Hidden. The power comes only through the key. Whoever holds the key, in a hand, in a pocket, on a cord at the neck, in a purse or in a mouth, holds the power for as long as they hold it, and the god cannot find hidden things until it holds the key again. The mortal who holds it learns nothing of the power: for them it is a small bronze key. A copy of the key is only a copy. If the key is lost, buried, or sunk in a river, the power is dormant until the god takes it up again, and the god has no sense of where it lies.
What it counts as: held on the person of someone else, or in a place they alone can reach, for any length of time. The god itself holding it again, even by a hand's touch, ends the loss. It does not count if the god leaves the key behind in a place it can fetch it from: only another person holding it takes the power.
What it asks in return: Kept Word holds twelve things at once instead of seven.
Edge cases: if the god changes to the dog and leaves the key on the ground, anyone who picks it up has the power. The god considers this part of the weakness and not an accident to be avoided. The flaw of cowardice dressed as caution, and its wish to be unremarkable, are written into how it conducts itself and buy nothing.

## The people it will lead
A river people, about five thousand, in a walled town gathered tight on the bend of a river, with marsh at the edges for those who need to be hard to find. Their market is the town's reason for being, and their council is made of the heads of the big families and guilds, a handful of elders and headmen of the boat families, who sit through the winter and argue. The god sits at the council table as a clever stranger, is heard, and does not command. Its helpers are the council's clerks and tally-keepers, who run things when it is away and who keep honest books. The people ask no rites of it and give none. They are traders, boatmen, granary keepers and fishers, and a few quiet hedge-witches in the alleys who are known and not mentioned. It accepts the rule lean and the worship lean as given.

## The premise
