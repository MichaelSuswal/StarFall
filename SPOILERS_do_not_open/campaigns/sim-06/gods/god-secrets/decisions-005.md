# Decisions, Year 5: god-secrets

I am the stranger the town calls the Counter, with a small empty ring for a mark. I act as the man throughout and I do not change shape this year unless something I cannot foresee forces it. The key stays on its cord at my neck and the tally stick in my coat. I sit at the foot of the long table on the stores, and I am not on the rolls. I use the key on nothing in the town. I stay in the town and its marsh holdings this year and go on no long road.

1. Autumn, the harvest month, the evening before the first sack goes in. I go to the Granary Warden in the granary yard, with the Clerk of the Tally by if the Warden wishes it, and I tell him that the count is his this year and I will not stand at the door. I ask nothing new. I ask only that it be made as it was made the last two autumns: counted aloud at the door, the far run filled by the sack with a tally-keeper of each of the two families cutting a stick, the near bins the same, and the whole store squared from the first sack. I ask the Clerk to set this year's sticks beside the last two and the old roll, and the Crier to cry the closing count at market as the Warden's. I ask the Warden to take it to the council himself. Then I do not go to the yard on the days of the carrying in. I sit on the market bench, where anyone who wants me can find me, and I let the count be made without me. I do this because a custom that needs me at the door is my custom, and I want it to be the town's. If it is done the same way without me, I know it is theirs. If it is not, I note it, I take it as it stands, and I say nothing of it unless I am asked.

2. Autumn, at the first sitting of the council after the harvest is in. I stay at the foot of the table and speak only if asked. If a headman asks me what I make of the new count against the old, I say what the sticks say and whose sticks they are. If the farming houses and the boat houses turn to who paid for the river, I say only what the Clerk's sticks hold: that the bar was dredged in the dry season by the council's own rule, that it cost fourteen hundred and twenty days of hired work and two boats, and what the groundings have cost in cargo and days over the three springs. I say that I do not know who ought to bear it, and that a town that argues from one book argues shorter than a town that argues from two. I ask the Speaker, through the Clerk, that the Clerk set the dredging's cost, the groundings' cost, and who paid each, onto one stick for the council, and I carry no figure of my own beyond what the Clerk has. If anyone asks again whether I am the one who dims the star, I answer as before, that I do not know. If the table turns to blame, I leave it.

3. Autumn, after the council has sat. I go to the Clerk of the Tally, over a cup and as idle talk, and I tell him that the star has dimmed seven times this year and twenty-one since I came, and that the town sets cups on the river steps and calls it manners. I ask whether he would care to cut a stick for each night, with the month, the hour the blue deepened and the hour it passed, kept by the night watch on the walls, who are awake. I tell him I do not ask this for my sake. A count the town keeps for itself tells the town what the star does, and does not tell it that I do it. I say plainly that I do not know what it is. I ask the Gate Warden, through the Clerk, whether the watch on the wall would note the hour on the nights it dims. If the Clerk says no, that is his to say, and I do not ask again this year.

4. Winter, once the cellars have taken water or not. I go to the Clerk and the Boat Master and ask, as a trader asking after his own trade, whether the Clerk would set down by household how many cellars took water this winter and in the three before, and how many of the small ones took the fever and in which streets. I ask for the count and not for a cause. I bring it to the council only if I am asked, and I say whose count it is. I do this because the cellars are the people's, I do not go into them and I do not use the key on them, and a town that talks all winter should have its figures when it talks. I do not speak of the old grain, and I do not speak of the delta stick. The Clerk has it. It is his book, and I do not ask whether he has opened it.

5. Spring, when the river opens, at the river gate. I ask the Boat Master whether the crews will sound the channel at low water and at high a fourth spring, and I ask only that it be done as it was, by them, with the depths put on sticks by the Clerk beside the last three springs. I do not go down to the gate on the day. I leave it to the Boat Master to set the first loaded boat to the gate himself. If he asks me to stand at the gate, I stand. I do this because the sounding is now a thing the boat houses can keep by themselves, and I want to see whether it is.

6. Summer, for about ten days, in the second month of the warm season. I tell the Clerk of the Tally that I am going down to the boat families' holdings on the marsh edge, by water, in a boat house's boat, and that I will be back on or before a day I name to him. I go as the man, with the key at my neck, and I use the key on nothing. I go to sit with the headmen of the boat families at their own tables, because the boat houses are the ones who borrowed their share, and I would hear their side of the river's cost in their own words, over a meal. I say what the sticks say if I am asked and I ask for nothing. I do not take a new telling that I would not be glad to carry, and if a teller begins one I do not wish to hold, I say so before it is done. I come back by the day I named, and I go first to the Clerk and give him the day I came and the day I said. I do this to be heard by the boat houses as I am heard by the farming houses and the offices, and to learn who looks up when I come through the river gate.

7. Late summer, the weeks before the harvest. I stay at the market bench and the foot of the table, and I note who comes to find me and who does not. I do not go up the river or out onto the plain this year. I leave the pass and the Guest for a later year, and I note that I chose it and the flinch did not stop me, because I know the difference and I would not have it called prudence if it was fear. I do not go looking for a god, and if I feel the weight in the air I do not change my face.

## Messages

None. I send no word to anyone out of my sight this year. I have no one to send it to, and no one who knows my name.

## Answers to what was asked

Nothing was put to me and left unanswered this year. The reed cutter's daughter asked me in Year 3, and my answer stands: she is to keep what her father began until she knows whether it is hers to give. If she asks again, in front of me, I say the same and I do not press.

## For the Keeper to rule

1. The sky. The star dimmed on seven nights and many in the town tie it to me, though two of the nights fell while I was out on the plain. I did nothing I know of on any of them. If the town asks me, I say I do not know, which is true. I ask for no ruling and I note it so the Keeper knows what I will say.

I do not try to grow any power this year.
