# Decisions, Year 5: Ilvane

Year 5 runs from the thaw round to the next thaw. The tremor goes at the thaw. I go on foot in undyed wool with the bone-handled knife, and I keep my name for any who ask me straight. My body stays in the people's land all year except one short walk of no more than five days in summer, named beforehand to the Reckoner.

1. Spring, the first days. I go to the Reckoner of Bodies first, at her door in the village, before any other office. I tell her four things and ask three, in my own words. I tell her the cords are lower again and I do not say what they mean, as she does not. I tell her I was far down the river when a runner came for a woman in a hard labor and no answer could be given, that I did not say how long I would be gone, and that this was mine to say and I did not. I ask what became of the woman and the child, if she knows. I ask her to carry to the elder of that steading, and to any steading that asks, these words: "When I am in the people's land and can be found, send to me through the Reckoner's door and I will come as I can. When I am gone, no one can answer, and a woman in labor must not wait for me. Send to your own sitter first." I tell her the days of any walk before I go. I ask her to carry to the Warden of Waters that, when salt comes up the river this year, he send a runner to her door for me, and I will come to the river and the creek mouths and the nearest wells while the salt is there and read them, because it came about five times and I do not know what it does to the water below. I ask her, last, about the Hearth-Holder who asked again to see the cords: that the cords are the court's and I hold no claim on them that the seat does not, that I do not forbid him to see them, and that I ask her to tell him in her own words what they say as she counts. If he asks for me, I will come with her or after her leave. I do this because she took my word first, because an office I go round is an office I lose, and because I will not be the reason the seat is kept from what is its own.

2. Spring, after the Reckoner. I go to the mounds with Hedda. To Hedda I say plainly that I count the same star and that the count is three, five, six and now seven. I say that the two pairs in the fifth and seventh months of Year 3 fell while I was far off and mending no one, and that her Year 4 count and mine point different ways. I say that I do not know what the dimming is, that I did not light it, and that I do not know it is going out. I ask her to keep counting by the month and to set beside each night what I did in that moon, and I ask her to tell the old women what I have said and not what she thinks I would like said. To the sitters of the three nearest steadings, through Hedda, I say that I know no brewing and cannot say what to give. I say that I will read a sick one before and after a brew when I am sent for and say what changed in the body, and that they may judge the brew by that, and that I know the Reading is closed on their own people so I will ask them what ails the sick and they must say it plainly. I do this because the old women's word that the lamp is going out is the people's fear and I do not feed it or quiet it with something I do not know, and because the younger sitter found use in my saying and the others asked.

3. Late spring. With the Keeper of the Mounds, if the Reckoner's word reaches him and he comes, I stand on the bank ground again and read the soil under the same rows in the same order, oldest to newest, and under any rows laid since his peg, and I read the ground on the rise where he lays now. I ask him to say aloud from his records which were laid in which month, and I have Hedda or the Reckoner's runner set the words in order so I can follow. I am looking for whether the foulness fades with the years, holds, or spreads, and whether rows laid on the rise since the peg read sound. I order no grave moved. I rot none. If he asks me whether a place is fit for the next rows, I read it and tell him what I read. I do this because one reading in one season does not tell me about time, and the Keeper is the one who answers for the graves.

4. Summer. Having told the Reckoner the days beforehand, I walk west to the treeline and go into the forest no further than a day and a half, reading the water at each spring I reach, any soil at a burial ground or midden I find, and any creature that looks sick. I come back out by the fifth day at the latest. I look for the forest's edge, for any other people, and for any sickness the people's steadings do not have. I mend no one on this walk unless a death is plainly at hand. While I am away no one acts for me and a question put to me waits, and the Reckoner has told the steadings so. I return to the Reckoner's door before any other and tell her what I found, including nothing. I do this because I have seen the river's edge and the upstream ground and never the forest, and because the walk is short enough that the steadings will not wait long for me.

5. Autumn, the wet season. I return to the village for the season when the children and the old fall ill. I tend the sick the sitters cannot reach and I read before and after when a sitter has brewed and sent for me. I read the three test wells on the rise at the end of the wet before any elder asks me, and I tell the Reckoner and the Warden what they read. If the Warden sends for me for the salt, I go at once to the river and read it where the salt stands, and at the creek mouths and the nearest wells, and tell him what I read. I sit at the seat's hearth if the Hearth-Holder asks me, with the Reckoner or after her leave, and I let the Judge-Mother and the Spearward see me again. If the Spearward asks, I say as before that I will turn the rot on no store of the people, in war or out of it. I use Hasten the Rot only if a carcass or spoiled grain threatens a wellhead, once in a day, and on that day and the next night I do not mend.

6. Winter. I stay among the steadings through the long sickness season and go where the need is greatest. I mend only what the sitters have failed at, one at a time, with long rests, and I keep the count in my head so the fever and the tremor do not run past what I can carry. I do not mend a child of the line unless no one else can save it. By the thaw I want the Reckoner's cords lower again, told to me by wells closed and wells open, and I want the sitters to have sent for me no more than four times. I count the nights the star dims and I note where my body was and whom I had mended within the moon, and I give the count to Hedda.

## Messages
None. I send no message to anyone not in front of me this year. The words to the elder of the steading below the court, to the Warden of Waters about the salt, and to the Hearth-Holder about the cords go by the Reckoner, who is in front of me, and she is the carrier, not a message of mine. The words to Hedda, the old women and the three steadings' sitters are said by me at the mounds, or through Hedda, who is in front of me.

## For the Keeper to rule
1. Salt in the water. Reading the Rot tells whether water is fouled and with what. If salt standing in a river, creek mouth or well is not a sickness, rot or poison, I expect it reads as nothing I know. I ask the Keeper to say what I learn when I read salt-touched water, and whether the wells and the creek below it read as fouled or as whole.
2. Nothing else lies outside my card that I know of. Reading the Rot tells me what is wrong now and never what brought it, so I do not ask to be told the cause of the foulness or of the star. I do not mean to try to grow a power this year.
