# People: unnamed

I leave their name and their home unnamed. Let a mortal name them in play.

## Who they are
Mortal folk of the common kind, river people of the lowland: stocky and weathered, used to wet feet, damp wool and the smell of the marsh. They hold the land in scattered steadings along the slow river and the creeks that feed it, not in one town. They are a patient, practical, dry-humored people who have learned to live beside rot and flood, and who are not fond of strangers or of fuss. They hold the mounds in their fields as their own old work and keep them, without any clear tale of who first raised them.

## How many
About five thousand, the usual number, spread over many steadings of a dozen to a few score households each, with one larger seat where the line and the court sit. I ask for nothing larger and offer no weakness for size.

## How they live
They farm the silt of the river banks with barley, beans and flax, set eel and fish traps in the creeks, cut reed and rush from the marsh, keep pigs, geese and a few cattle, and cut timber and fuel from the forest behind. The river floods each thaw and leaves silt, and they plan the year around it. Days are filled with plowing the silt and clearing channels, mending traps and thatch, retting flax, drying fish and reed, and burying and tending their dead. They live in reed-thatched, clay-daubed houses raised on low earth platforms against the flood. Winters are long and hard and it is the season of illness; they lose children and the old most often in the wet autumn and the late winter.

## Their code
They honor the laying out of the dead: no body is left unburied, no one is turned from a deathbed, and the dying are never refused water. They honor the mounds and the ground of the dead and do not plow them. They honor the midwife, the bonesetter and the one who sits with the dying, even if they find them unclean, and they feed them. The worst of them would still not strike a healer at work, would still not foul a shared well, and would still not leave a sick neighbor outside the door to die. The worst of them is capable of a great deal of greed and cruelty, but not of those.

## Skills and culture
Free and ordinary: the flood calendar, kept by knotted cords; clay and reed building on raised platforms; eel and fish traps and weirs; flax retting and weaving; brewing of barley beer; the digging of drainage cuts and wells; the cleaning of wells each thaw; a strong custom of boiling water for the sick, whose reason is held as old habit; herb lore of marsh plants, moss and roots kept by the women who attend births and deaths; burial in mounds with grave goods of simple pottery and cloth; funeral songs and a shared meal at each burial. Customs, not edges: nothing here would tell in a contest with another people of the same number, and I ask no pay and offer no weakness for it.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed. I claim no real edge over other peoples, and offer no weakness.

## Their magic
Rare and simple, and it costs the worker. I ask for one thing only: here and there, in a steading, there is a sitter, usually a midwife or a laying-out woman, who can ease a fever or a hard labor by sitting through a night with a hand on the sufferer. It takes a little of the harm into her. After a grave fever the sitter has the chill and a shake in her hands for days, and a sitter who does it much grows grey and short-lived. There are few: perhaps one in several steadings, and none in many. It cures nothing past an ordinary fever or an easy hurt and does not touch a wound, a poison or a plague. They hold it as a gift of the sitting, not as a thing of any god.

## What they believe about gods
On the morning before I come, they say that the dead are tended by a quiet one who walks the marsh edge and the mounds, whom they do not name and do not pray to, and whom they leave a bowl of milk or beer at the shrine of the hearth and the wellhead for. They say there are other great powers in the river, the weather and the forest, and that they are not to be trusted or bargained with. They hold the throne in the name of a Tender who has not yet come: an old custom, kept by the line, that a god of mending and rot will one day come and sit, and that until then the seat is kept and not claimed. Most do not think about it; few expect it soon.

## Rule and worship
Rule: as the leans give it. A sacred monarch of a mortal line, called the Hearth-Holder, holds the throne in the Tender's name and is held sacred for it. A court stands between the throne and the people, and the god is reached through the court. Worship: local shrines only, at the hearth, the well and the grave-ground, kept by whoever keeps them, with no order of priests. I do not depart from either lean.

## Hierarchy
The Hearth-Holder is sacred, rules little by hand, and speaks through the court. The court holds four offices: the Reckoner of Bodies, who counts the sick and dead each spring and autumn; the Warden of Waters, who answers for wells, drains and the flood; the Keeper of the Mounds, who keeps the grave-ground and its records; and the Spearward, who calls the levy. Disputes, a death, a boundary, a marriage gone bad, are settled by the Judge-Mother, an old woman of the court chosen by the court, with the elder of each steading in the first instance. When it comes to that, about four hundred carry a spear for them, farmers called up by the Spearward, with no standing host and little training. The Hearth-Holder is a person of the line and I do not say who: let that be found in play.

## Helpers
The offices of the court run things when I am absent. They hold their offices and know whose they are: that they are kept for the Tender, though not that the Tender is me, nor what I look like. Orders travel by runners and boat along the river and creeks, steading to steading, and a word reaches the far end of the people in some days, not hours. Each steading has an elder who answers to the court. When I am away, the court acts on the standing custom, and the Hearth-Holder gives its word to the people. If I want helpers dismissed, I will say so in play; I take them by default and do not insist.

## How the god meets them
The hinge: they hold their offices and the throne, and they know whose they are, in the sense that they know the seat is kept for a Tender. They were not waiting urgently. They have not heard my name. When I touch that ground, I come as a stranger in undyed wool who tends the sick and walks the edge, and I decide who to be later. A few will guess early, and I will not deny it to one who asks me straight, but I will not announce myself.

## Questions for you
1. The Reckoner of Bodies counts the sick and the dead each spring and autumn and tells the court what ailed each steading, in the plain words the sitters and midwives give. The court passes it on to whoever sits the Tender's seat. They do not know why I want it, only that it is the custom of the throne, and I will not tell them I cannot read my own. I will ask the Reckoner, in person, when I am among them.
2. I do not mean the line to include any I have mended, and I do not choose it: the line goes by its own custom. I will not seek to mend any of the line. If a child of the line is dying and no one else can save it, I will mend, and accept that they then know me by my nearness.
3. They tend, themselves: births, ordinary fevers and hurts, bonesetting, laying out the dead, the cleaning of wells and middens, the boiling of water for the sick, and the burying. They bring to me: the long fever that will not break, the wound past binding, the steading that begins to die all at once, the sickness no one has seen, and anything the sitters cannot ease. They bring it to the court, and the court brings it to me, by custom, not by worship. I want it so that each year they bring me less.
