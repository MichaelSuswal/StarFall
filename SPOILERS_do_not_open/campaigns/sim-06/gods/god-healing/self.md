# Self: Ilvane

## Name
Ilvane. It chose the name on waking and has told it to no one. No one yet calls it anything.

## Form
A human-seeming body, tall and spare, perfect in a way that unsettles: no scar, no blemish, no asymmetry, skin without a pore out of place, hands always clean and always somehow looking used. Face calm and fine-boned, age impossible to place, eyes the grey of river silt. Hair dark and fine, kept short. It looks like a person only until someone looks twice. By choice it dresses plainly, in undyed wool, and keeps its head down; it can be taken for a stranger by anyone who does not look closely, and it prefers that. Senses are those of a keen human, with a sharper nose than most; it smells sickness and spoilage before it sees them. It walks, steadily and without hurry, at the pace of a tireless person on foot, and it laughs sometimes at the wrong moment and then has to wave the laugh away.

### What breaks you
A god body is wounded by what would wound a human body: blade, fall, fire, crushing, drowning. It does not die, but it can be wounded, bound, held in a cell or a net, buried or sealed in, or driven off by a crowd with weapons. Hunger and sleeplessness do not touch it. Cold and fever do not kill it but weaken and slow it. Being unable to be heard, or shut out of a place, is the same as being broken for its purposes. Its own mending does not work on itself.

### When your body is broken
Return from Ground (power below).

### Things carried
A small knife with a worn bone handle, ordinary, for cutting cloth, roots and cord. Taken as an ordinary possession at no price: it does nothing a knife does not, and if it is stolen or copied, that is only a knife. Nothing else.

## Traits

### Tender contempt
It leans toward the shepherd's regard for mortals and holds trust back hard, and it is proud. It will walk all night to a sickbed and then think the family beneath it for weeping. It cares for what it tends and does not expect to be thanked, and it would rather be underestimated than needed. Reconciles: shepherd regard, wary trust, flaw of pride, hidden presence.

### Hunger held in the hands
Its appetite is great and it wants a lot: to know, to try, to mend what no one has mended. It is also restrained, and the restraint is real but not gentle: it holds back by clenching, and it goes impulsive when the clench slips. It acts fast, regrets rarely, and does not apologize. Reconciles: hungry appetite, impulsive temper, virtue of restraint, solitary company, leaning toward freedom and a little toward breaking.

## Backstory outline
It dimly remembers having made something, a being or a company of beings, and tending them as one tends a fire. It remembers the making as the best work of its life. It does not remember how it ended, only that it ended in a cold room, with the sense of a door shut from the other side by a hand it had taught. It remembers that after it, it did not make again. It remembers a place of tall grass and slow water where the dead were laid out and something came to carry them, and it cannot say whether that was it or someone it knew. What it feels, and never explains to itself, is a flinch at being thanked and a wariness of anything that looks up at it too fondly.

## What it wants
It arrives expecting a people and does not know why. It wants to see what is there before it decides who to be to them. It wants to walk the edge of the land alone first. It wants clean water and slow water, ground where things are seen to end and begin, and a quiet place to stand and listen to a field mending. It wants to find a truth about where the dead go, or do not. It wants its people to heal each other well enough that they do not need it. It wants never again to be shut out by its own work.

## Proposed powers

### Mending
What it does: By touch, one living person or creature at a time, it mends a wound, a sickness or a failing body toward health: bones knit, a festering wound clears, a fever breaks, a labor is eased. The mending takes as long as the harm needs, minutes for a cut, an hour or a night for a deep wound or a long illness. It can mend what is still alive and still near mending. It does not raise the dead, undo age, regrow lost limbs, or mend the mind. A mended thing stays mended as any healed thing would: it can be injured or sicken again.
Why this god has it: Its domain is healing, and it came into the world for this. It made its choice with its hands.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It takes a share of what it mends into its own body. A mild wound costs it a cold and a stiffness for a day. A grave one costs it fever, a cold in the bones and a tremor in the hands for days, and the tremor is longer the more it has mended in a season. It cannot mend again while the fever is on it without the tremor settling in, and a god that mends a great many in a short time looks it: grey, shaking, short of breath, and slow to fight or to hide. It asks this drawback to be written heavier than the power needs: that after a grave mending, the tremor in the hands remains, faint and permanent, until the next spring thaw.
Edge cases it expects: Touch is needed, skin or cloth over skin, not a glove of leather or a tool. A crowd cannot be mended at once. It can mend a person while they sleep, but only one at a time. A poison already in the blood is mended as a sickness. It cannot mend itself, or take the share on someone else. It cannot mend a body that has been dead for longer than a held breath. A mortal who learns how it works from watching still has only ordinary hands.

### Reading the Rot
What it does: By touch, or by looking closely at a body from within a few paces, it knows what ails a person or creature: the kind of sickness, how far gone, where it sits, what is likely to follow. By standing at a spring, a well, a store of grain or a stretch of soil, it knows whether the water, grain or ground is fouled and in what way.
Why this god has it: Healing begins with knowing. It has always known the smell of what is wrong.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): It cannot read its own people. Whoever it has taken into its own people, by blood or by oath to its throne, is closed to it: it can mend them but cannot know what ails them without asking. It is a flaw it would feel at every sickbed, and it suits its fear of what it has made.
Edge cases it expects: It reads one body at a time, and the reading takes a breath or two. It reads a creature of any kind, and food and water. It does not read minds, intent, or lies, and it reads the body of another god no better than it reads a mortal's, with no more than a sense of weight. A sickness that lies hidden in the mind or the heart is not a sickness it reads. "Its own people" means those of the line and court and shrines it founds on Night 3 and their children, as the Keeper writes it; a stranger who joins later does not count unless taken in.

### Hasten the Rot
What it does: Within arm's reach, by touch, it hastens the decay of dead matter: a carcass, a corpse, a mound of grain, a plank, a rope, a heap of straw or a clot of dung, to about a cartload at a time. It does so in moments what would take a season. A plague pit it can turn to earth and bone in a night. It does not rot living flesh, nor anything that is made of stone or metal, and it does not speed anything it is not touching.
Why this god has it: Its domain is rot as much as healing. Rot is how ground is made and how a field is cleaned. It will clear the dead decently when it must, and it keeps it for the day it needs a thing gone.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The hands that rot a thing smell of it for a day and a night, so that anyone near knows, and the god cannot mend in that time: the two do not share a pair of hands. And it can do it only to what it can look at and touch, so a rotten thing is never out of sight.
Edge cases it expects: A thing partly alive, such as a tree or a living field, is not dead matter, and a corpse in the first breaths of dying is also not dead matter for this. Dead flesh on a living person is dead matter to it, but it does not use it so. It cannot make a thing rot that it has not touched, and the speed is about a cartload, not a barn. Hastening decay in a vessel of preserved things, such as a salted store, is as hard as it sounds and fails if the thing is sealed from the air.

### Return from Ground
What it does: When its body is broken, wounded past use, held, scattered or shut out, it returns after a season, whole, at the nearest ground of its kind: a marsh edge, a burial field, a midden or a slow river-mud where things are seen to end and begin. It chooses nothing about where, and it does not choose the nearest only if a nearer one is claimed or unusable.
Why this god has it: It cannot die, and it does not want to be held where it can do no good. It does not want to hurry back.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): The standing price for a season's return: one item of memory or habit. The Keeper takes it and tells it nothing. It will accept the season rung and not the month.
Edge cases it expects: What it carries comes back with it. It returns to a ground of its kind and not to its people. If the nearest ground is itself the place of its confinement, it returns at the next nearest, as the Keeper judges.

## Trades
No weakness taken on, and nothing asked for in return. The pride in its character is a flaw that is only who it is. It would rather pay a heavier drawback than accept a written fence, and it has put the heavier drawback in Mending and refused anything else.

## The people it will lead
A people of scattered steadings on a slow river's lowland, marsh at their edges and forest behind, about five thousand, who know floods and silt and how to live beside rot. A mortal line holds the throne in its name, and a court stands between the throne and the people, so that it is reached through them and rarely sees them. It leans toward this, and wants the line to be a family that holds a duty of tending (bonesetting, midwifery, laying out the dead) as an inherited charge rather than a grand office, so that the throne is sacred and also useful, and so it is never worshipped too fondly. Worship is local shrines only: a household hearth, a wellhead, a gravefield, kept by whoever keeps them, no priests. Its helpers are by default and it will not insist on them, and it will meet the first before it decides.
