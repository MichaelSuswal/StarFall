# People: the Sarrow (their own name for themselves, which they say means "those who keep the sill"). Their winter home I leave unnamed.

## Who they are
Mortal folk of the common kind: broad, weathered, quick to cold and slow to warm, dark of hair and eye, good at being hard to see on a hillside. They are a host of herders, carriers and guides who live in the high broken country and who hold, by long custom, the passes and fords they move through. Their days run with the season: the herds up to the high grass in summer, down by the first snow, the road and the war band between, and the winter spent behind walls at the one place they keep. What fills a day is feeding the herd, mending harness and roof, carrying other peoples' goods for a share, standing the watch at a crossing, and keeping the fire and the table. This is my wish for them and their own belief about themselves. The world decides.

## How many
Eighteen thousand or near it, a host with a city. The city is their winter place, a walled town of timber and fitted stone at a crossing, holding perhaps four thousand in the cold months and swelling at the first snow. The rest winter in the valley round it, in hide tents and low houses. I accept the cost of a people this size: much food and fuel, slow to move, slow to grow, and a hard time of it in a bad winter. I did not ask for the number to be cheap.

## How they live
They are a close band on the move. From the thaw until the first snow the host is spread in columns along the road between the high grass and the winter ground, each column with its herds, its captain and its keeper of rite. At the first snow they come together in the winter city and shut the gates against the cold, and the passes are theirs to hold. In country like this they live by the pass and the ford: they know where the river can be crossed and when, they cut steps where the rock needs them, they carry loads where mules cannot go, and they take a toll in kind for safe passage, which they give. They eat meat, curd, barley and dried fruit, they keep goats, hardy cattle and horses, they weave wool and felt, they work wood and stone, they smelt and forge iron in the old way, bloomery iron, no steel. They keep count by the moon and by knots on a cord, and they reckon a year from first snow to first snow.

## Their code
They honor the guest and the host. A stranger who comes to a Sarrow hearth is fed, seated, and left in peace until he has eaten and slept, and no blood is spilled at the sill. They honor a toll fairly paid, and a word given at a threshold. The worst of them would still not break the sill: not strike a guest at the hearth, not turn a freezing traveler out into the snow to die, not sell a guest. These are oaths. An oath can be broken, and some will break it; I do not ask for them to be unable to.

## Skills and culture
Ordinary and free. They are good guides, packers, stonecutters and cut-timber builders, and know their country's weather. They keep a table custom: the host breaks bread first and eats first, to show the food is clean. They keep the empty seat at the table and the unlatched door at first snow, for the Guest, as set out below. They sing at the sill when someone crosses it. They teach their children the names of fords and passes as other peoples teach the names of kin. The women hold the hearths and the stores; the men and women alike may carry the spear. They are not gentle, and they are not cruel for sport.

## Edges and their weaknesses
I ask for no priced edge. I offer two tempers, which are who they are and can fail:
- They are hard to ambush in a pass. A habit of scouting ahead of any column, sending a runner up each side wall, never entering a narrow without one. A temper, not a guarantee. It fails when they are hurried, drunk, grieving or overconfident, and the weakness is the same one: they are slow to move because of it, and a column that stops to look is a column that can be caught on the open ground between.
- They are slow to turn a stranger away. The weakness is the same habit: they take in the wrong guest, and some of them will take in the wolf. It can fail either way.
If the Keeper reads either of these as an edge, I would sooner drop the first than argue it.

## Their magic
Simple, and it costs the worker. One office: the salt wife (any woman or man of the priesthood who takes it up, a few at a time, not more than a dozen among all the Sarrow). A salt wife lays a line of salt across a sleeping place, a door, a ford edge or a cattle fold, and speaks the sill rite over it. While the line holds and she is within a day's walk, she wakes, wherever she sleeps, when anything crosses it, and knows only that it was crossed and by how many, not what or who. The cost to her: she does not sleep deep while any line of hers holds; she wakes at every crossing, goes thin and grey, ages faster, and the more lines she holds the faster. A salt wife who holds many lines does not live long. That is who works it, and what it takes. It does nothing more.

## What they believe about gods
They say that gods are guests who have not yet been fed. A god is one who comes to the door at the end of the year, in a shape a little wrong, and the house is judged by what it does. They say a Guest is expected at the first snow, a tall woman, stooping, some say, with a key; others say a man, or a wind. They set the empty seat and leave the door of the warlord's house unlatched through the first night of snow, and fill a bowl and leave it on the sill. They do not agree on what the Guest is, and the old women argue it every winter. They say a house that turns the Guest away is cursed to a bad winter. This is their belief, as I wish it.

## Rule and worship
Rule: as my lean, a warlord. A mortal ruler under me rules by force through the war band and its captains, and one ruler answers to me and rules for me. I add nothing to this and I give no reason to depart.
Worship: as my lean, a priesthood. The keepers of rite, called Doorwardens, have standing among the people and keep the sill rite, the salt, the oaths and the table law. Offerings are brought to the rite fire at each camp and to the winter hall: bread, salt, a measure of grain, a bowl of milk. There is no tithe by law. They do not pray to a god by name: they set the table and open the door, and the rite is the worship.

## Hierarchy
Under me stands the warlord, Kessan, called the Lintel, a man of fifty winters, heavy, scarred, careful with his word and short in temper, who took the war band from his father and has held it eleven years. He is respected and not much loved, and he is wary of the Doorwardens, whose standing among the people is nearly his own. He is afraid he will not be ready when the Guest comes, and he tells no one. The war band is nine hundred sworn spears under eight captains, with a further three thousand who carry a spear when the host calls; the captains each hold a column on the road and a gate in the city in winter. Beside him stands the First Doorwarden, Idris Veld, an old woman, patient, shrewd, well liked, who has kept the rite for thirty years and is the one the people trust before the warlord. She advises him and he does not always listen. Disputes are settled at the sill: a death, a boundary, a marriage gone bad is brought to the nearest Doorwarden, who hears both sides at the doorway of the house or the camp, sets the price or the line, and has it confirmed by the captain of the column. A matter that is too hard, or that touches the warlord, goes to the First Doorwarden and the warlord together at the winter hall. These are who they are, and each can fail me in play. The warlord may overreach; the First Doorwarden may stay silent when she should speak.

## Helpers
The helpers who run things when I am absent are these offices: the Warlord (the host and the road, the war band, the passes); the First Doorwarden (the rite, the table law, the sill courts, and the priesthood of keepers, who carry word between the columns); the Steward of Salt and Stores (the granaries, the salt, the winter stock, who answers to the warlord for the counting and to the First Doorwarden for the fairness); the Marshal of the Road (the scouts, the guides, the toll houses at fords and passes, who answers to the warlord); and the Mother of the Hearth (an elder woman chosen by the hearth mothers to speak for the households, who may bring a grievance to either). Orders reach the far end of the host three ways: by horn and smoke from the city and the high watch posts for the plain words (move, hold, gather); by the Marshal's riders along the road, a day's ride between relays; and by the keepers of rite, who carry my word and the First Doorwarden's to every column's rite fire and speak it there. I know the doors of my people by The Latch, and I hear when my marks are crossed, which is as near a way to know how my people stand as I will have.

## How the god meets them
I am looked for. They are waiting, in the way a household waits for a guest who may be a blessing or a judgment. They know the offices and whose they are: the warlord holds the host, the First Doorwarden holds the rite, and each knows the other. The winter hall is ready, the seat is set at the table, the door of the warlord's house is unlatched for the first night of snow. When I come it will be at the first snow, to a door at the ford, and I will come stooping, as the old women say. I do not come to a people who have never heard of me; I come to one who has long expected a guest and does not know it will be me.

## Questions for you
1. Three lesser marks are your priesthood's to lay, in places your people hold. A people on the move holds what? Say what counts as a place of theirs.
   Answer: A place of theirs is a fixed doorway of something they keep and have built: the gates and the winter hall door in the winter city, the toll houses and waystations at the fords and passes where the Marshal keeps a post, and any standing house of the hearth mothers where a Doorwarden has taken the rite fire. A tent flap is not a place, because a door that is carried is not a threshold, and a camp is not a place until a standing doorway is built in it. A column on the road holds nothing. I name the three marks I would ask for first: the gate of the winter city, the door of the winter hall, and the toll house at the ford. I accept that on the move I hold few, and I say so, because it is what a moving people is.
2. You may not cross a threshold you have not blessed unless a keeper bids you in. Who keeps the warlord's door, and are you bidden?
   Answer: In the winter city the warlord keeps his own door, with a hearth guard of twelve at the sill, and the door is unlatched for the Guest at the first night of snow. I am bidden: the First Doorwarden has taught the household that the first one who stoops at the door is to be bid in, and the warlord has been told. On the road his door is a tent flap, which keeps itself, and the guard at it bids me or does not. If a guard does not know me, I stand outside and wait to be bidden, which is the law I took. Where the people hold the place, I may stand in the threshold to bless it without being bidden, as the ruling gives me, and the door of the winter hall is the one I would bless first.
3. After a step you stay within sight of the door you came to for a day. How do your orders travel to the war band in that day?
   Answer: I arrive at a door I have blessed, and my orders go from that door. At the winter city I stand at the hall door, within sight of it, and the warlord and his captains come to me; a horn calls the captains and the keepers of rite, and what I say to them is carried out on the road by the Marshal's riders and the Doorwardens. On the road I do not step unless I mean to stay by the door I arrive at, since I may not go beyond sight of it for the day. If I must be in the field with the war band, I walk, and I do not step. The step is for the winter city and the crossings. Where I cannot go, the First Doorwarden's rite fire and the Marshal's relays carry the word, a day's ride at a time.
