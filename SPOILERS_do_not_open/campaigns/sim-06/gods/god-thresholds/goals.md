# Goals: god-thresholds

Year 1, standing on the ground. In the order I would defend it.

1. Be met properly, and see how they meet me. My people have awaited a tall woman who stoops, with a key, and the five offices serve me from this night by my own account. They know whose they are. They do not know my name, which I have none of, or what I can do, and each office holder can fail me. This year I learn who Kessan the Lintel and Idris Veld are by their hands and by what they do at a table, not by what I wrote of them. I read each once, across a table, and accept that each learns I have read them.
2. Hold the doors I can hold. Bless the ford first, because it has no keeper and no one can refuse me, then the door of the winter hall and the gate of the winter city, where my people keep the door. Spend salt as if it were scarce, because the bag is small and I do not know where more is. Keep the oath: name as guests only those I have weighed, because every guest I name is one I may not harm, and my hand is quick.
3. Bring the host in before the snow and keep a winter. Eighteen thousand come down from the high grass by the first snow. I want the columns counted, the stores counted, the gates held and the passes watched, because a bad winter for a people this size is the thing that would undo me. Food and fuel first. Everything else after.
4. Know the land and its crossings. The road, the ford a mile off, the two passes, the river, the city. I do not yet know if the city two miles south-east is my people's winter place, and I will not assume it until I stand at its gate. By autumn I want to have walked the road and both passes, and to know who else keeps a crossing near mine.
5. Justice at the sill. The code my people keep is guest and host, and the worst of them would not break it. I want a Doorwarden's court at every column, and I want it known that a house that turns a freezing traveler out will answer for it. I take no oath to forgive the ones who do. I also remember that my own hand turns to breaking when I am afraid, and that I will be sorry in the morning, or will not be.
6. The strangers. Learn who deals fairly at the tolls and who does not, and who comes over the passes with odd tools and speech. Test them at a table, where I cannot lie and they may. Give no one more than a seat until they have eaten.
7. Find the others like me, face to face, at a door, and look at each before I decide. Not hunting for them. Standing in doorways and noticing.
8. The word I cannot name. If anyone says something over a threshold that is mine, I want to hear it. This year I ask one question, once, of Idris Veld, who has kept the rite thirty years: whether any rite her people keep says a name over a sill, and whose. I do not ask it twice, and I do not say why I ask.
