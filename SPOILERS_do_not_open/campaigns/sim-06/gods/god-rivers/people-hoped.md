# People: unnamed

We leave the name of the people and the name of their home to the mortals. What we say of them below is our wish, and what they believe of themselves is theirs. The world decides, and we will see.

## Who they are
Mortal folk of the common kind. A river and spring people of a walled town gathered close, on the first firm ground where a stream leaves the low hills and runs down to the wide lowland of many channels. We wish the town to stand where a spring rises inside or hard by the wall, so that the town never has to leave its water to fetch it. The smoke and the worn path along the stream, which the land shows, we hope are theirs: their hearths, and the path their own feet and ferry poles have made.

## How many
Small, about two thousand, within the range of one to three thousand. Most live in the town. A few families keep reed hamlets on the nearer channels of the lowland, in the dry season and the shoulder seasons, and come in behind the wall when the floods are at their height. We ask for no larger people and take the world's cost for the ones we have.

## How they live
By water and by the edge of it. In the lowland they cut reed for roofs, mats and boats, set weirs and traps for eel and fish, net birds in the thick of the spring flights, and gather from the marsh margins. On the drained flats between the channels, and on the terraced slopes by the springs, they grow barley and millet, flax, beans and onions, and they keep goats, geese and a few cattle on the hill pasture. They make reed boats and flat river barges poled across the shallows, baskets, rope, pottery, woven wool and flax cloth, and bricks of earth and straw. The town wall is a bank of rammed earth with a timber palisade along its top and a ditch fed from the spring. Their days are the days of the water: the weirs at dawn, the ferry at the ford all day, the markets when salt carriers and other travellers come through, the mending of channels and walls after each flood. In winter, when ice sleeps the channels, they spin, weave, make pots and tell long stories.

## Their code
What they honor: that the ford is open to anyone who comes to it, and that a stranger is fed at the first hearth they reach and asked their news only after. That a spring is never fouled and a well is never poisoned, in peace or in war. That a person in the water is pulled out before any question is asked of them. That every house sits at the council through its head, and that the council is heard out to the end. That a child is counted, named and fed before anything else is counted.

What the worst of them would still not do: foul a spring, leave a drowning stranger to drown, sell a guest, or shut the water gate on someone who is fleeing. These are their habit and their pride, and what they swear to each other in the old way is an oath that a person may break and be shamed for. We ask no guarantee that cannot fail. Some will fail, and be remembered.

## Skills and culture
Ordinary culture, freely. They keep count with knotted cords, one cord for each house and one for the town. They keep their stories and law by recital: a person trained to say, in order, what was decided at each sitting of the council. They mark time by the thaw, the flood, the ebb and the ice. They are loud, quarrelsome and funny at the council fire. They are kind at the edges: a stranger is a gift to them and not a threat. They tell the age of a ford by its stones and the age of a grandmother by how many floods she has counted.

They have a custom of a low seat kept by the water gate, an empty seat among the council seats, with a cup beside it that is filled fresh each morning for whoever the river sends, as a courtesy and not as a rite. The cup is for a guest, not for a god. No one remembers when it began or for whom. We hope for it, and we will see if the world gives it.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None asked. We propose no edge over other peoples. A habit of theirs that they are slow to lay hands on one another, and loud and fast to forgive, is a temper and not a guarantee, and it can fail.

## Their magic
None that we ask. No one among them works a thing that cannot be done by hand. Their ferrymen who know the weather, their midwives with a steady touch and their well keepers who know a spring's moods are skilled in ordinary craft, and some of them will be said to have more than that by their neighbours. Whether any of it is more is for the world to decide, and we do not ask for it.

## What they believe about gods
On the morning before we come, they say there are gods upriver and downriver, in the stories the salt carriers bring, gods of fire, sky and war, and that none of them lives here. They say the water is old, has its own moods, owes the town nothing and is owed nothing, and that it is rude to ask a river for what a person can fetch for themselves. They say the water listens, a little, at the edge of a field and not in a market. They say it is good manners to draw the first water of the day for a guest. They hold no god and ask for none. They are wary of being asked to kneel by any stranger, and slow to take offence at the asking. This is their belief, as we wish it.

## Rule and worship
We take the leans as given. Rule is a council of the town, of its own: the heads of the old kin houses, the ferry elder and the eldest midwife, about nine seats in all, deciding by long talk until no one will stand against it. The god is heard there and does not command. The council's helpers carry out what is agreed. The council chooses a Speaker at each spring flood, one of themselves, to open and close the sitting and to speak for the town to strangers. Worship: none. We ask no rites, no shrine and no offering. The cup at the low seat is courtesy. What the people do about us is theirs.

## Hierarchy
The council advises and rules. It is the only rule over the people. We hold a seat at it, and are heard, and carry no vote and no command. The Speaker presides. The Ferry Elder speaks at council for the ford and the water: its state, its crossings, who came and went. The Reckoner, an elder chosen by the council for an even hand and a good memory of old agreements, settles the rest: a boundary between fields or fish weirs, a debt, a marriage gone bad, a quarrel over a boat. A death is settled by the house of the dead and the well keepers and midwives who tended them, and brought to the Reckoner only if the houses disagree.

## Helpers
The offices that run things when we are away, each of them a person of the town with their own mind and their own quarrels:
- The Seat Warden keeps the low seat and its cup, keeps the cord of what was said and done at each of our sittings, and recites it back to the council when we come again, and vouches for us.
- The ferry keepers run the ford and the barges, carry word along the channels to the reed hamlets, and watch the water for the Ferry Elder.
- The well and spring keepers tend the springs and the town's water, and know their moods.
- The midwives tend the births, the hurt and the fevers, and know who is ailing in the town.
- The mill hands and weir hands keep the race and the weirs, and tell the council when the water is wrong.

Orders go from the council to the helpers by word at the sitting, and are sent to the far end of the people, the reed hamlets, by the ferry keepers in a boat on the channel and by runners along the path, with a horn from the water gate in an emergency. A word of ours reaches the council through the Seat Warden when we are not there, and reaches the far end by the same ferry keepers. We do not command them. We ask, and the council agrees or does not.

## How the god meets them
We come down the stream path in the woman's shape, in the spring thaw with the channels brown and full, on our feet, barefoot, from the springs in the hills toward the water gate. We make no show. A ferry child, or a ferry keeper at the ford, will see us first. We ask for nothing but the first hearth, according to their own custom, and give no name beyond the one the water has: the Lady of the Ford, or only the river. We sit where we are put. If there is a low seat, we are shown to it. They do not know whose it is, and we do not tell them. We hear the council out. We ask who is in the town and how each house stands, and we remember. We do not shift in the sight of anyone, and we leave the water shape for nights, the channels and the water itself.

## Questions for you
1. **What the water is and what we are, to them.** The water, to them, is an old neighbour with moods of its own: kind at the edges, owing nothing, and owed nothing. They thank it, as one thanks weather. They thank the water, the ferryman or luck for what we do, and we cannot claim our works. To them we are the Lady of the Ford: a guest of the town, sat at the low seat, a woman from "the water's side" with warm hands whom the water seems to listen to, taken in at the first hearth as any stranger is. Some will say we are the river's kin, and a few will say more, quietly, at the edge of a field. We do not correct them, and they do not ask. We do not ask to be called a god.

2. **How the council knows us when we come.** By the low seat, which they keep, and by the Seat Warden, who rises and recites to the council what was said and done at our last sitting and by whom, from the cord, and says "this is the one who sat there". By our hands, which are warmer than a body should be, for those who take them. And by the count: we can say who sat at which fire since the last sitting and who is missing from the town, and no one else can. Because a face does not stay with those who have not seen us within a season, they will know that someone was there, and what was said and done, and not what the face was. The cord and the recital hold what the face cannot.

3. **Who speaks for the water while we lie in it.** The Ferry Elder speaks for the ford and the river at council, as always, for the water's state. No one speaks for us. Our seat stays empty, the cup is filled, and the Seat Warden says only that we are at the water and will come back. We do not ask the council to wait on us, to guess what we would say, or to put anyone in our place. They decide without us and we take what they decided, as we do when we are heard and not obeyed.
