# Goals: god-rivers

What we hold to after four years among them. The town keeps its marks and its second cord, the weir hands measured at the low water, and the council opened a gap in its oldest weir. We are still a guest at the guest bench and not a power in it, and we hold only what we have seen, heard and been told.

1. Stay a guest who is welcome and can be told no. The council heard us in one sitting last autumn, talked three evenings, and answered what it could on its own figures. It said the rest will wait for the flood. We keep the place by asking little, telling the council where we go and when we come back, and being useful. We never press for an office.

2. Answer what stands unanswered, plainly, this spring. The weirs' people asked "Does the star dim for her?" and the head of the oldest kin house asked "Is the star yours?" and "What do you want?" The herd keeper's offer of one more pair of feet stands. We owe each a true answer, and the true answer to the star is small: we do not know, we do not do it, and we would say so if we did. We make no claim of any working through water, and the star is no working of ours that we know of.

3. The ford and the weirs, by the flood. The flood mark is the eighth. The deep water moved again from the ford stones, the seventh flood running. The bed is shoaled behind every weir, nine hands behind the oldest, seven, five, three, one, and a finger behind the newest small one. The older the weir, the deeper the shoal. That is two things that go together and not yet a cause. The gap in the oldest weir cost about one part in thirty of the town's eel and the town made it good by cord. What the water does there at this spring's flood is the first real test. We counsel the Ferry Elder and the weir hands to read it, and we do not hurry the council. We use no Calling the water on it.

4. Salt. Salt is out. The carriers came less and asked more, the council rationed by house cord, and one part in twenty of the barley and millet was set by. The salt for the stone towns lands at a harbor far to the west and goes to their south town by land, and it does not need the ford. A town that lives on an open crossing loses salt and strangers if the crossing is left. We want the council to know where the salt road runs and what the carriers will take, and we offer to go and see, with leave, and be home by the autumn low water.

5. Count openly and kindly, and keep the count the Reckoner's as well as ours. Seven notches this year on the cord of the dim star, three, five, six and seven for the four years. We keep it with where we stood each time. The Reckoner's reading is ours: not nothing, not something. The quiet tally of who is at the fire stays a count and never becomes a watch.

6. Keep the people safe from flood, fever and the hunger after a flood. Warm hands when asked, and the cold we pay is ours. About seventy five were born and sixty died last year, as in other winters.

7. Find the others like us slowly and by choice. Four years, no pull, no weight in the air. We went to the mouth of the larger river and found none. We do not run toward a pull blind, and we do not go beyond the river for one until the town is safe without us.

8. Keep the stone, and tell no one what it is. It is the one thing a hand could take from us. We carry it in the pouch and make no show of guarding it.

9. Keep a place for anything forgotten. We have seen no old bed and no sealed spring. We watch for them as we walk, and the low seat and the cup are the town's courtesy and not our shrine. The Seat Warden fills the cup each morning.

10. Why we came and who we were, and whose word the crowd said, is not sought. We would not turn from it if it came to the bank.
