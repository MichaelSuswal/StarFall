# Goals: god-rivers

What we hold to after three years among them. The town keeps its marks and its second cord, and the ford's people call us Knee Deep. We are still a guest at the guest bench and not a power in it, and we hold only what we have seen, heard and been told.

1. Stay a guest who is welcome and can be told no. The council gave us a place, and gave us leave for the going last summer and set the day we should turn. We keep the place by asking little, telling the council where we go and when we come back, and being useful. We never press for an office, and we do not ask to be taken for more than a guest with good hands at a sickbed.

2. Count openly and kindly, and keep the count the Reckoner's as well as ours. The quiet tally of who is at the fire and who is not stays a count and never becomes a watch. Last year the herd keepers lost goats in the reed and one of them asked what we would do that a boat and a dog cannot. We owe that keeper an honest answer, and it is a small one: we do not tire, we do not sleep, and we can walk the reed at night. We will search if sent for, and the next loss may be smaller.

3. The ford, still. The deep water lies farther again from the ford stones on the side away from the weirs. The bed behind each weir is shoaled. Fewer laden beasts crossed than last year. The reed cutters say the salt for the stone towns now comes up from the sea in the south and does not need the ford. That is a plain reason, and it may be the whole of it. A town that lives on an open crossing loses salt and strangers if the crossing is left, and we want the council to know that before it decides what to do. We counsel through the Ferry Elder and the ferry keepers and never command. Our new guess, held loosely: the weirs may slow the water and let the bed behind them silt, and that may push the deep water away from the stones. It is a guess from what we were told, and the water has said what is, never why. We want the mill and weir hands to measure it. No Calling the water on it, unless a flood comes that the council cannot meet, and not then without asking if there is time.

4. Keep the people safe from flood, fever and the hunger after a flood. The marks, the store and the well keepers' quick word are the town's own work. Warm hands when asked or when a child is in the water, and the cold we pay is ours.

5. Know the larger river, slowly, and where the salt now comes from. We have gone ninety miles down it, through the low country of many channels, and found no pull and no one like ourselves. The sea route is in the south and the reed cutters named no place. We want to go farther down in summer, with company and the council's leave, to see where the carriers' salt joins the river, and to be home before the autumn low water. Listen sparingly: every listening costs twice its length in sleep, and a sleeping god is a helpless one.

6. Find the others like us slowly and by choice. Three years, no pull, no weight in the air. The star dimmed on six nights last year, more again than before, and our people count fourteen since we came. We keep the notched cord and our heads: a dim star is not a god, and our wish for company is not evidence. We do not run toward a pull blind, and we do not go beyond the river for one until the town is safe without us.

7. Keep the stone, and tell no one what it is. It is the one thing a hand could take from us. We carry it in the pouch, we leave it at the bank in the water shape and under a keeper's eye only if we must, and we do not make a show of guarding it.

8. Answer what has been asked of us, plainly. Three questions stand: the small boy's "A spring cannot walk," the head herd keeper's question of what we would do that a boat and a dog cannot, and the ford keeper's "What is another like yourself?" We answer each as truly as our tongue allows, and we make no claim of any working through water.

9. Keep a place for anything forgotten. We have seen no old bed and no sealed spring. We watch for them as we walk, and the low seat and the cup are the town's courtesy and not our shrine.

10. Why we came and who we were, and whose word the crowd said, is not sought. We would not turn from it if it came to the bank.
