# Decisions: god-rivers, Year 1

Year 1 runs from the hour before first light at the spring head, in spring, round to spring again. These are the actions in the order we mean them, each with its season. The voice is ours: we, the Lady of the Ford, or only the river, as mortals may name us.

## Actions

1. **Spring, the hour before light: kneel in the stream at the spring head and listen for a short while.** We lay a hand in the cold water where it comes out of the ground and hold still for about as long as it takes to say a long story slowly, not longer, and so sleep about twice that afterward, lying by the spring in the woman's shape with the stone in the pouch at our waist. We do it before anyone is awake, with no one near, because we are helpless while we listen and while we sleep. We listen for the nearest things first: the town two miles down, whether its water is clean, what the stream carries, who crossed and drank, and whether there is a ford. We do not go beyond what the stream tells us in that short time. We stand in the running water as we do so, and we watch in it for a pull, upstream or down, that would tell us another like us stands in this river. Our reason: we have never seen this country, and we would rather know the water's first word before we meet the people it feeds.

2. **Spring, at first light after we wake: walk down the path along the stream to the town, barefoot, in the woman's shape, stooped, in our grey wrap and hemp cord.** We do not shift in anyone's sight, we do not hurry, and we leave the water shape for nights, channels and the water itself. We take the stone in its pouch and show nothing. We come to the water gate by day and ask for nothing but the first hearth, by the town's own custom as we hope it is. We give no name beyond the one the water has, the Lady of the Ford, or only the river, and we correct no one who calls us anything else. A ferry child or a ferry keeper at the ford may see us first, and that is as it should be. Our reason: we asked to be a stranger fed at the first hearth, and we mean to be one.

3. **Spring, the first days in the town: sit where we are put, eat what is given, ask only their news after we have eaten, and ask to be heard at the council.** We ask whoever feeds us who keeps the council and who speaks for the town, and we ask, through them, to be heard by the council once, to say who we are as far as we know it: a woman from the water's side, who wishes to stay a while among them, who asks for no rite, no shrine and no obedience, and who would be glad of a seat at the edge of their talk. If there is a low seat kept by the water gate, we will sit there if we are shown to it, and we will not claim whose it is. If the Seat Warden, the ferry keepers, the well keepers, the midwives or the mill and weir hands wish to hear us and take our word, we will let each do so in their own time and by their own custom, and we will win no office by pressing. We ask each who is in their charge and how each house stands. We remember what we are told, and count the houses, the heads, the children and those who are missing, and we say aloud that we are counting, so that no one thinks we count in secret. Our reason: no one acts for us until an office holder has heard us and taken our word, and we would rather win that honestly than be handed it.

4. **Summer: walk the town's lowland with the ferry keepers and the people of the reed hamlets, and learn where the water goes.** We ask a ferry keeper to take us down the channels to the nearer reed hamlets, by barge or on foot along the banks, and we ask each hamlet its name, its people and its worst flood. We stoop, sit low and eat at each hearth. Where a child is in the water, or a person is hurt or fevered and asks us, we use warm hands and take the cold, and we ask no payment and no thanks. We do not use Calling the water this year unless a flood comes that the council cannot meet, and even then we ask the council first if there is time. Our reason: we want to know where the water goes before we know where it harms, and we cannot count the people until we have walked to all of them.

5. **Summer, after the first weeks: ask the council, as a counsel and not a command, to keep a cord of the flood heights and a store against a bad flood.** Through the Ferry Elder and the Reckoner, and at the council fire, we ask that the ferry keepers cut a mark for the height of each flood by the water gate, that the council keep what grain, dried fish and salt the houses can spare against the year the water comes too high, and that the well and spring keepers tell the council at once if any water turns foul. We offer to carry the count of the houses back to the council if they want it, and we do not push it. If the council says no, we take their no as given and ask again only if they ask us. Our reason: hunger after a bad flood is what we fear most, and the best defence is the people's own habit of listening sooner.

6. **Autumn, after the harvest and before the ice: listen for a longer time at the water gate, in the open and in safe hands.** We ask a ferry keeper who has heard us and taken our word, or failing that the Seat Warden or a well keeper we trust, to sit by us while we kneel in the running water below the gate and listen for about half a day, and to wake the council if anything comes against us, though no one can wake us. We make no claim on the sleep that follows, which is the length of the listening twice over, and we ask the keeper to keep us from the cold and the dogs. If no helper has yet taken our word by then, we do not do it in the open town. We go instead by night to a quiet pool in the hills above the town with no one near, and listen for less. We listen for who crosses upstream and downstream, who drinks, what is coming down from the hills, what the salt carriers say at the ford, and whether any other like us stands in this river, which the pull will tell us only in a bearing. Our reason: the whole country is a body of water, and we mean to know this part of it before the winter closes it.

7. **Winter and the turn of the year: stay in the town, sit at the council through the ice, and be there at the spring flood and the choosing of the Speaker.** We tell stories at the fire, learn the names of the children, and count again. We keep the cup at the low seat, if there is one, and drink from it only if it is offered to a guest. Frozen water is silent, so we do not listen. We return to the spring head once at the end of the year, in the woman's shape, in the early spring thaw, and kneel in the stream where we woke, to mark the turn. If the pull has spoken in the water at any time this year, we will have it in mind and decide in Year 2 whether and how to go to it, slowly and by choice, and not before.

## What we tell our people to do, and through whom

We command no one, and until an office holder has heard us and taken our word we have no helpers in fact. We meet them as follows: in person, at their own hearth or at the council, in the woman's shape, stooped, barefoot, without show. We say plainly that we are asking and not ordering. We ask the Seat Warden, if there is one, to keep the cord of what we say and do at each sitting and to say it back, and we ask the ferry keepers to carry word along the channels only if they have chosen to. We ask the well and spring keepers to tell us, and the council first, if a spring is fouled. We ask the midwives to tell us of any child or mother at risk. We ask the mill and weir hands to tell the council when the water is wrong. All of these we ask, and the council or the person may refuse us without cost.

## Messages

None. We send no message to anyone not in front of us this year. We know of no one to send to.

## For the Keeper to rule

Nothing is asked beyond what our card plainly covers. We state our reading so it may be corrected:

1. Listening water, at the spring head and at the water gate: we read the card as covering a short listening at the head of the stream and a longer one below the gate, with the sleep as twice the listening. We read "no one can wake us" as binding on us as written, and we plan for it by asking a trusted helper to keep watch.
2. Warm hands: we read that the cold we take is counted in the days nature would have taken, and that we cannot heal one we have wronged.
3. Unsigned: any such work is read by the town as the water's, and we will not claim it in words.
4. We try to grow no power this year.
