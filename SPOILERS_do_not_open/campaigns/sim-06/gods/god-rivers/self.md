# Self: god-rivers

## Name
No name that we hold. The water has never said one to us. We say "we" of ourselves, and a mortal who needs a word may use the water's: the Lady of the Ford, or only the river. We will answer to either and correct no one.

## Form
We are two shapes and one body, and we shift between them as a river shifts between its flood season and its still one. The change takes about the time of three slow breaths, in the open or in the dark, and we choose when.

The first shape is a tall woman of broad shoulders and long arms, heavy in the hip and plain in the dress, barefoot by habit. Skin the colour of wet slate in shade and of river clay in sun. Hair dark and always damp, worn down and uncombed, with a few pale threads in it like foam on an eddy. A wide, calm face with a long upper lip and grey green eyes set far apart, eyes that look at a speaker steadily and a little too long. Her hands are large and warm, warmer than a body should be. She stands about a head over most mortals, but she stoops, slouches and sits low when among them, and she walks a bank as if she had done it for centuries. Senses as a strong mortal's, with a hearing for running water that is finer than any sense of her own kind has a right to be. She moves at a walking pace, a little faster downhill.

The second shape is a body of living water: a low, braided, shallow stream about the breadth of a cart, lying along the ground and flowing over it, grey green and clear in the day and dark in the night. It follows the fall of the land and the line of channels as water does, and moves at the pace of a slow current downstream and a walking pace across level ground, slower uphill, slowest of all up a steep face. It can pass under a gate, through a reed bed, into a ditch, a well, a spring pool, and it can lie in a river unseen as one more thread of the flow. It sees and hears as the water around it does, dimly. It cannot speak in this shape, nor hold a thing, nor carry the stone, which it must leave at the bank or have held by the water against its bed.

### What breaks you
In the woman's shape: being bound, pinned or caged and held fast, drowned in a place with no current, or struck hard enough to scatter the body into water that cannot gather itself. In the water's shape: being dammed, drained, dried in strong sun, spread thin across dry ground, poured into a vessel and sealed, or frozen solid in deep winter. A held body cannot shift. A scattered body is only water until it returns. Fire dries us; cold holds us; stone walls and sealed jars hold us. A spear or blade can hurt and slow the woman's shape but cannot end it.

### When your body is broken
Rise at the spring (below).

### Things carried
A smooth, flat grey river stone the size of a palm, pale at one edge, worn by water. We do not remember where it came from. It is held in the hand or worn in a pouch at the waist. It is also proposed below as the instrument of a power. We carry nothing else; the woman's shape wears a plain grey wool wrap and a hemp cord, ordinary things.

## Traits
**Mercy that runs ahead of judgment.** We help before we have thought, and we are open to anyone who comes to us with a need or a gift, and we are wrong about them more often than a careful god would be. This reconciles a shepherd's regard for mortals, an open and unguarded trust, and an impulsive temper with the virtue of mercy. The contradiction is that we are quick to give and slow to learn from being cheated; we forgive the trick before we have finished feeling it.

**Counts its own, and cannot stop counting.** We gather people close and want them near, and we cannot rest if one is missing: who is at the fire, who is at the ford, who has not come home. We will walk a night for one lost child. This reconciles the lean toward gathering and a builder's care with the obsession of our flaw. The contradiction is that we love freedom more than law, and want no one bound, no council commanded, no worship asked, while we hold a quiet tally of every one of them that we cannot put down. We would never fence anyone in. We would only like to know where they all are.

## Backstory outline
We remember a morning, or something like one: many hands dipping into us at once, and a sound, one word said over and over by a crowd, which we took to be ours. We remember being warm with it. We do not know the word.

We remember later that the word stopped. The channels that came to us were turned away and the hands went elsewhere. We remember a dry bed and a flat stone and an empty bank, and standing at it.

That is all. We do not know who we were or why the hands left. We do not ask it of ourselves. There is a place in us where the word was, and we do not stand near it.

## What it wants
We have come to lead a people, and we know no more than that. We want to walk up a river we have never seen and find someone living at the top of it who did not know we were coming and is glad of us. We want a people who speak freely at their own council and are not afraid to say no to us. We want them fed, and the children not drowned and the grandmothers not buried in a bad year. We want no worship and no rites, and we want, though we would not say so, to be remembered rightly by them. We want to find the others like us slowly and by choice, and to be liked by someone who owes us nothing. We want a place kept for anything forgotten.

## Proposed powers
### Listening water
What it does: By standing in, kneeling at or laying a hand on running water or a spring, we hold still and the water answers. We hear what it carries: words spoken on or near its banks within about a day, who crossed and who drank, what sickness runs in it, what the weather is doing upstream, what lies in a pool or under a bank. It is not sight; it is hearing, and it comes as the water's own muddled telling, old and new together, and we must judge what we are told. The water is one connected water, from where we listen to every spring, stream and channel that feeds it and every one it feeds, as far as it runs.
Why this god has it: Rivers and springs carry everything, and we are a thing that listens and is slow to be heard. It is the reach we want most: to know a country by its waters before we know its people.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): While we listen we are helpless and still, deaf and blind to everything that is not water, and unable to shift or stand. When we finish we are cold and drowsy and we sleep as long as we listened, twice over, and no one can wake us. This is the heavier price we ask, in place of any written limit on how far or how much we may hear. We would rather pay in sleep than be told where the water ends.
Edge cases it expects: It works on water that is truly connected and running or rising; a sealed jar, a cistern with no inflow and a lake with no outflow tell us only what is in them. It tells nothing of what is said far from any water. Frozen water is silent until thaw. Ice, a dam or a long dry reach stops it at that point. A god standing in the same water is felt as a weight in the air only as the world allows, no more.

### Calling the water
What it does: We raise or lower, speed or still, turn or part running or standing water that is already there: swell a stream to a flood, drop a ford to a trickle, hold a current still, push it against its own fall for a short time, shift a spring's course by a few paces, open a spring that is stopped with a stone where water lies behind it. We cannot make water from dry ground, only move what is. The greater the working, the more it takes of us.
Why this god has it: A river is force. A people on a river need a ford that holds, a flood that does not drown them and a mill race that runs, and a war at the water's edge should not be ours to lose.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): Each working draws on the water in our own body. We go dry, brittle and thirsty, cracked in the lips and slow, and we cannot shift or work again until we have lain in running water for as many days as the working was great, a day for a small working and a season for a flood that drowns a valley. When we work in anger the water goes where we feel and not where we aim. We ask this as our price and want no cap written on how large a working may be.
Edge cases it expects: It does not work on water in a vessel sealed against us, on water already held in a dam or channel built to hold it unless we break the hold first, on rain falling or ice standing, or on water in a living body. It moves no more water than is in reach; a dry river bed gives us nothing. Its working is seen as flood or ebb, which the people take for weather.

### Warm hands
What it does: With our hands on a hurt or fevered person, and water we have touched to drink or wash with, we mend broken flesh, bring down fevers, and clear the sicknesses that move in water and the sicknesses of childbirth. We give of our own warmth, and nothing is taken from anyone else.
Why this god has it: Mercy is our virtue, and a midwife whose hands are warm is the gentlest thing a water god could be. It is the gift most likely to be used.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): We take the chill. Our hands and then our whole body go cold, and we are slow and clumsy until the cold has lasted as many days as the healing would have taken in nature. We cannot heal ourselves. We cannot heal anyone we have deceived or knowingly wronged, as the warmth will not come.
Edge cases it expects: It does not raise the dead, restore a lost limb, undo age or cure what is not hurt, and does not move pain from one person to another. It cannot be sold: no one pays for it with another person's suffering, and it does not run on a debt of any kind.

### Unsigned
What it does: Our workings read as the water's own doing. A flood that spares the ford, a fever that breaks, a spring that comes back, a warning heard in the night: the people see it as the water's work and the water's mood and do not look past it for a hand. No mortal, hearing of it or seeing the result, is led to us by it. A god within a few paces of us still feels what we are, as the world allows; this power hides our works and not our nature.
Why this god has it: We stand out the way a great river stands out from a creek, and do not want to erase the creek. And we have been a name and then no name. We would rather work unnamed by choice than be forgotten without it.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): We cannot be thanked. Whatever we do for a people, they will thank the water, the ferryman or their luck, and we cannot turn this off, even once we want to be known. No mortal who has not seen us at our work with their own eyes can be told what we did and believe it. We take the stab of that, over and over.
Edge cases it expects: It does not hide us from anyone who sees us work with their own eyes, from any mortal who is told by one who saw, or from any god who can read us at close quarters. It does not hide what we say at council, only what the water does. It hides no deed that has no water in it.

### Rise at the spring
What it does: When our body is broken or scattered, it comes together again at the nearest spring or head of running water, in the woman's shape, after a season, with the stone beside us. We name no ground other than the nearest.
Why this god has it: We cannot die and would be a poor god of a river if we could not run again from the spring.
Drawback (something it would notice losing): One item of memory or habit, and we do not know which. The price on the standing scale for a return in a season.
Edge cases it expects: If the body is held and not scattered, no return takes place until the hold is broken. If there is no spring in reach, it is the nearest ground of our kind. What we carry comes back with us, or lies where we rise.

## Trades
**The stone as instrument of Calling the water.** We take the weakness: Calling the water comes only through the flat grey stone we carry. Whoever takes it takes that power from us until we hold it again, and in any other hand it is still only the ordinary thing it has always been, whenever the power is not theirs to use. For this we ask one improvement: a faint sense, from far off, of a matter of our own kind. When another god's body stands in or upon water that runs into or out of the water we know, we feel it as a pull, as another current in the same water. It tells us only that another is there and a bearing, upstream or down, and nothing of who, what it can do, how far it is or whether it means well. Only at close quarters does it become knowing.
Edge cases of the weakness:
What counts as taking it: any hand that carries it off, takes it in the dark, wins it in a dice game or has it given in trust, and also a gift. We may give it freely, and if we do, we give the power with it, to a hand that will have the same price to pay as we do. What does not count: it lying in our pouch while we are in the water's shape, left on the bank within our reach and unclaimed, or lost and not found, in which case the power is simply absent until we have it again. It is not taken if a hand only touches it.
How long: until we hold it in our own hand again. We feel where it lies as a faint cold in the bones, but are not told by whom.
What ends it: our hand on it, or, if it is broken or ground to gravel, the power is gone with it, and we leave the Keeper to write what comes after.
The faint sense is permanent while we hold the weakness. It does not come with a way to speak to the one we feel, and it is no help in telling a god from another water thing of strange weight.
A flaw that is only who we are: we hold on to what we gather. We do not ask a price in return for it. It is written into how we conduct ourselves.

## The people it will lead
We lean toward a small people, one to three thousand, in a walled town gathered close, at a ford where springs and streams meet, with salt carriers passing through and the river for a road. We take the lean as given: a council of its own, with elders, headmen and the heads of the old kin houses, and we sit in it as one voice among them and are heard but do not command. We take no worship and ask no rites. We would rather be answered with an honest "no" than a bowed head. The helpers who run things when we are away are the ferry keepers, the well keepers, the midwives and the mill hands, people of the water who know our hands, with their own mind and their own quarrels. We do not depart from any lean.
