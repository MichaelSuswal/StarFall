# Decisions: Vael, Year 5

Year 5 runs from the turn of the year, through my waking season, autumn, and round the four seasons to autumn again. I am a hind to the waist down and I move at a hind's walk. I cannot pass a low or barred door and I cannot climb a ladder. My body stays in the town and its near ground until the summer, when it goes to the harbor and back, as the last action says. Where I say "ask," it goes to the Warden of the Door and from her to the throne, and nothing is done until the office holder who does it has heard me and taken my word. I order nothing and I give no word in any dream.

## Actions

1. Autumn, the first month. I go to the yard of the watch on the wall and answer the two questions put to me there, with the Warden of the Wall by, his captain, and the four who sit my door. To the captain, who asked what the wall has been sitting if a lying hind is not me in a dream: I do not know. I do not sleep, and I have no memory of lying down on those nights. I will not say it is a dream and I will not say it is not. A body that stands and murmurs is me in a sleeper's dream. A body that lies still is something I cannot name, and it is mine, so you sat me and I cannot tell you what I was doing in it. I ask you to keep sitting it as you have, and to write what you see, whether it breathes slow or fast, whether the ears turn, whether the lamp on the cord dips, whether it stirs to a hand, and to tell me the next morning. I ask nothing of you but that. If fire comes, take me by the cord and lead me out, as I said last year. To the Warden of the Wall, who asked which house is nearest to a broken body: the one nearest to where it breaks that keeps a lamp burning through the night. It need not be a house kept for me. Nearly every hearth in the town burns a lamp till dawn, and any of them will do. I come back after a season and the walk, whole, and I may come back lacking some small thing I knew, a name or a song, and not know which. I do not ask you to carry me there, and I ask you to give me bread. Then, the same dusk, I go to the Keeper of the Count and give him my list of the days of my fourth year on which I was afraid, as I felt it, so that his cords of the seven dim nights can be set beside it, with his word of awake or lying against each. I say I do not know whether they agree and I will say so whichever way they fall. Through the Warden of the Door I tell her that I meant up, not down: the town lies four days up the river from its mouth, and I said it wrong, or it was heard wrong. I thank her for finding that the roll at the quay was the source of the other error, and I ask the Steward whether the roll is struck now. Reason: I left these a year. A question left unanswered is a door left open to the cold, and a rule that I speak I hold to the letter.

2. Autumn, the first month, in the same week. I go to the barrow, by day, to the two young households who hold two parts in three of the third song, with the Warden of the Door to take me through, and a man of those households who asked. I say first: I could not have walked the old singer's sleep. Her house kept no flame, and the power reaches no sleeper who keeps none. I would not have taken a song from her sleep in any case, since what a sleeper says there is hers. Then I ask to hear the two parts, and I sit and listen to the end and do not correct or ask for more. I ask the Warden of the Door to send the court's runners, through the headmen of the hamlets, to ask whether any old singer in any hamlet holds the third part, and to tell the singers' households where the answers are. If a singer of the barrow households keeps a flame and is dying, and the household asks me, I will sit the last sleep, and I tell them now that what is sung there fades unless it is sung again awake, so it is best sung awake first. I press no household to keep a flame. Reason: they asked in earnest through the Warden of the Door and have had no answer in a year. The song is theirs. I can hear it, and I can send the runners to look for the rest.

3. Autumn, the second month, after the grain is in. I ask, through the Warden of the Door and by the throne's leave, that the court judge this year's standing order for the harbor house. The Steward of the Store and the old cutter judge what stone can be spared against what the wall and the footings need, and whether to send the full sixty blocks, fewer, or none, since the picks wear twice as fast and the old iron is a third down. The throne decides whether to send it and who carries it. I ask the Steward to ask the factor, by the court's leader, whether his house will sell iron or picks in place of salt for part of the stone, and whether the iron in the harbor is soft too. I do not know why the iron is soft, and I do not claim it or blame anyone for it. I ask also that the leader, if the court sends one, learn at the river's mouth whether the house's ship lifted the stone that lay there, and tell the court. The people who carry it are the court's and carry its word. I send no flame, no lamp and no token of mine, and I send no spears. The two messages below go with the party. Reason: the stone and the salt are the town's road, and the iron is the town's trouble. The court holds both, and I help only by asking.

4. Winter, the long nights. Keeping Sleep goes to two households a night by the Keeper of the Count's lot, with deathbeds, newborns, fevers and fresh griefs first. Three only on named nights of deep winter, told the day before to the Warden of the Door and to the wall men at my door that same dusk, at most three nights in the winter. The seventh place stays empty. In kept houses that ask, I walk the last sleep of the dying and give no orders there. I go with the Warden of the Door to the Night Sitter's yard on a night she and the hamlet girl sit, if the Night Sitter will have me, and I sit beside them with my hands for the lamp and the kettle and take no work. I tell the girl plainly that my gift does not reach what the sitting takes from her, as the Keeper ruled, and I ask the seat, through the Warden of the Door, whether her household is heard by the court on what she needs. If the party sent in the autumn is home, I hear its leader at the yard of the watch with the Warden of the Door and the Steward, and I ask him what the factor said of the iron, of the stone and of the road to the delta. Reason: the cold is the town's test and I came to make the nights survivable. I have carried two and carried three on named nights, and I know what it costs.

5. Spring, when the river is high. I ask the Warden of the Door to bring the four offices to the hall, with the throne's leave, and I say to them what I mean to do in the summer: to walk to the harbor, no farther, in the dry months, with the lamp on my waist, and be back before the end of the year. I ask the throne's leave to go. I ask the Warden of the Wall for four of the watch who will come of themselves, not spears, to walk beside me and guard my body on the road and at the harbor, and I tell him the days beforehand, as he asked, and I ask what the road is like. I ask the Steward for a gift for the factor's table, linen and oil at his judgment, and for the leader of last year's party, who knows the road, to come as guide if he will. I ask the Keeper of the Count to name two households a night while I am gone, and not more, and to see the chain of flames tended as the four told me they would. I ask the Warden of the Door to hold my place and to answer a question put to me while I am away with that it waits. I remind them what the four told me last spring, and I keep to it. If the throne says no, or the Warden of the Wall says the road is not safe, I do not go, and I say so to the whole court. Then I walk the hamlets within a morning's walk of the wall, with the days told beforehand, and light a flame only where asked. Reason: I asked the four last year what the town would do if I were away, so that a later year could be free. This is the later year, and I would rather go with their rule in hand and their leave than go on an impulse and leave them to answer for it.

6. Summer, the dry months, if the leave is given. I walk down the river road to the mouth and along the shore to the harbor, at a hind's pace, with the four of the watch and the guide, the lamp on its cord on my waist the whole way. I do not set it down, I do not hand it to any hand, and I do not sleep, so I stand at night, with the watch to guard my body. I go no farther than the harbor. On arrival I ask at the factor's door as a guest asks, and I sit in his yard if his door is too low for me, and keep the guest's custom: bread, three nights, and no questions of his business until the third. On the third night I thank him for what he gave my leader, ask him plainly whether he would carry a word to the delta, and, whatever he says, ask him how many days the delta lies from the harbor by the coast and by ship, who goes there, and whether anyone outside his house would carry a word for pay. I ask what the people of the harbor say of the one at the delta market, and listen. I light no flame and send none to a people who are not mine. If I feel the weight of one like me, at the quay or in the street, I stay where I am in the open, near the watch, and let it come to me, and I do not follow it. If the road to the delta proves short and safe and the court's leave is not given for it, I come home and ask again in Year 6. I do not go to the delta this year. I start home at the latest at the end of the eleventh month, so as to be at the wall before the year turns, and the days I am away are told to the Warden of the Wall beforehand. While I am away, no more than two households a night are kept, and the fear still reaches me at dawn wherever I am, and I carry it as I carry two. Reason: I have not left the town in four years, and my fear is what has kept me in the doorway. I can go to the harbor and be home, and the town will know I do not vanish. I need to see the sea and the man who would not carry my word, and I need to ask him to his face.

## What I ask or tell my people to do, and through whom

All of it goes through the Warden of the Door to the throne, and the throne speaks it from the wall at dusk if it is a word to the whole people. I ask: that the Warden of the Wall, his captain and the four who sit my door hear me in the yard of the watch, and write what they see of my body on the lying nights; that the Keeper of the Count keep the lots, the roll of kept households, the cap of two a night, and the tally of dim nights with awake or lying beside each, and set my list of afraid days beside it; that the Warden of the Door take me to the barrow and to the Night Sitter's yard, and send the court's runners to the hamlet headmen to ask for the third part of the song; that the Steward of the Store and the old cutter judge this year's stone and the iron, and the throne choose the party; that the court's leader carry the two messages below and learn at the mouth whether the stone was lifted; that the four offices hear me in spring and give the throne's leave or refuse it; and that, if I go, the Warden of the Wall give four of the watch and the Keeper of the Count name two households a night. I take nothing as a due. I give no word in any dream.

## Messages

1. To: the one in the delta market town in the west, who is seated as keeper of the town's weights and says before the market that it is a god. I do not know its name. If the carrier cannot name it, he asks for the one who reads the scales.

The words exactly: "I am Vael. I am half a woman and half a hind, and I keep a lamp in a town in a river bend, four days up the river from its mouth. If another word of mine has reached you, it said down where it should have said up, and I ask you to forgive that. I have heard that you say before the market that you are a god. I do not know whether you are. I do not ask you to prove it. If you are one like me, I would like to meet you face to face, at a gate, as a guest meets a house, and I will ask nothing of you but to know you. I tell you where I am so that you may come or not as you choose. I do not send anyone to bring you and I do not ask what you can do. If you are not one like me, I wish you a good market, and I ask you to forgive a stranger's question. The people who bring this are guests at your table, and they keep a guest's custom: bread, three nights, and no questions of your business until the third. If you will send me a word, they will carry it home."

Carrier: the court's party named in action 3, led by whoever the throne and the Steward choose, who speaks it as I have said it, from memory and not from any token. The factor of the harbor house would not carry it last year. This year the leader does not ask him to. He holds it, and gives it to any carrier who is not of the factor's house, a coasting ship's crew or a free trader going to the delta, for linen or oil from the Steward's goods, if the factor does not object to that. If there is none, he brings it home and says so, and I carry it myself to the harbor in summer.

Route: down the river to the mouth, along the shore to the harbor, and from the harbor to the delta market town by whatever road or ship a carrier outside the house uses, as the leader judges safest. He does not go on himself unless the court allowed it before he left, and not in the gales.

Sent with it: nothing of mine. No flame, no lamp, no token that says what I can do. A guest's gift of linen, pots or oil at the Steward's judgment, from the court.

If the recipient cannot be found: the carrier leaves the words with whoever carried them, asks at the market for whoever keeps the weights, and if none will hear them he buys what he needs and comes home. If the recipient is hostile: the carrier does not fight and does not argue, and comes home with what he can, or winters where it is safe. If the recipient asks questions: the carrier answers only what he saw and was told. I woke on the stony ground above the town in the dark before an autumn morning, I do not know why, the seat takes me for the one awaited, and I keep a lamp that I light in a house that asks. He does not say what the lamp does, does not say that it can be taken, does not give or sell a flame or a lamp of mine to anyone, and does not say how many days I walk or which nights I keep. If he is pressed on any of it he says he does not know. If the stranger says it is a god, the carrier says what he heard and says no more. He does not say it is false and does not say it is true. He listens, he brings home any answer, and he brings home any word of another like me on that coast.

2. To: the factor of the house at the harbor town that sets the price of salt for the bend, the one who gave my leader bread and three nights and turned my word.

The words exactly: "I am Vael, the guest at the bend who keeps a lamp. I thank you and your table for the bread and the three nights you gave my leader last year, and for the salt at the price you set. I understand that your house does not carry a stranger's word to a market it sells to, and I do not ask you to. I ask only for what you will tell: how many days the delta market town lies from your harbor, by the coast and by ship, who goes there, and whether anyone outside your house would carry a word there for pay. If you will not say, I ask no more. My court asks whether your house sells iron or picks, and whether the iron on your coast is soft as ours is, and that is the court's question and not mine. The people who bring this keep a guest's custom: bread, three nights, and no questions of your business until the third."

Carrier: the court's party named in action 3, and the leader speaks it as I have said it, from memory and not from any token. In the summer I speak it to him myself, if I go.

Route: down the river to the mouth and along the shore to the harbor.

Sent with it: nothing of mine. The court sends the stone and the goods it chooses.

If the recipient cannot be found: the carrier asks at the harbor for whoever sets the price of salt, gives the words to him, and if no one will hear them buys salt as any trader would and comes home. If the recipient is hostile: the carrier does not fight and does not argue, and comes home with what he can. If the recipient asks questions: the carrier answers only what he saw and was told, and does not say what the lamp does, that it can be taken, or how many days I walk. If he is pressed he says he does not know. If anyone there asks whether another like me stands on that coast, he says he has seen none and has heard of none.

## For the Keeper to rule

Nothing I am using in a way the card does not cover. I do not mean to grow any power this year. One note, which is not a use: the wall's men sit my body on lot nights and see it lying still, and call it asleep. I do not sleep by my card, and I have no memory of lying down. I do not claim it is a power and I do not know what it is. If the Keeper rules anything about it, I ask only for what a watcher at my door would see.
