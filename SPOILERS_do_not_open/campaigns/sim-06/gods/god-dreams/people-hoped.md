# People: unnamed

I leave my people and their home unnamed. They will name themselves, and I will not take that from them. What I call them is only "my people," and the town I call "the town in the bend." All of it below is written as my wish for them or as their own belief. The world decides, and I will see.

## Who they are
Mortal folk of the common kind: farmers, herders, potters, weavers and stone cutters, with no gift or blood that sets them apart. They are a settled people, not wanderers, and they have been in this bend of the river longer than anyone alive can count. They keep graves older than any family can name, and songs whose words they no longer understand. I want them to be small in front of their ancestors.

## How many
About five thousand, the usual number. About three thousand live in the walled town on the stony rise above the bend. About two thousand live in hamlets on the bottom land and the hill edges, each within a morning's walk of the wall. I do not ask for more, and I take the cost of this many: grain, fuel and a winter that must be got through.

## How they live
They farm the dark bottom land, which floods and forgives: barley and a hardy millet as the main grains, with beans, onions and flax. The flax gives linen, wicks and lamp oil from its seed. On the hills they keep sheep and goats, pigs near the town and a few oxen for the plough. They fish the river and set weirs in the low water. They cut stone from the scar on the hillside for the wall and the house footings, and they fire clay for pots, lamps and roof tiles, so the town has smoke from its kilns as well as its hearths. The days are the work of the seasons: sowing and harvest, the flood watch in spring, the stone cutting in the dry months, the weaving and mending of winter. Autumn is when they come to me: stubble on the bottom land, grain in, the river low, mist in the mornings and the evenings drawing in early.

## Their code
What they honor is the table. A stranger who comes to the door is given bread and a place for three nights and is not asked his business until the third. The dying are not left alone. The children are counted at dusk, and the old and the small are fed first in a hard winter. They honor their ancestors and tend the old graves.

What the worst of them would still not do: turn a guest out into the night after bread is shared, leave the dying to die alone, or shut a child outside the wall after dark.

These are who the people are, able to fail. They are written as temper, not as binding. Some will fail them, and the failing is what I will see.

## Skills and culture
Ordinary culture only, taken free.
- The Dusk Table: at the evening meal every household bakes bread and sets one extra place, and neighbours drop in. On the long nights of winter whole streets eat together and tell stories.
- A lamp in every house: at dusk each house lights a clay lamp and sets it in a niche by the hearth, and keeps it burning through the night against the dark. The oil is flax, or tallow in a poor year.
- Counting by knotted cords and notched tally sticks, kept by an officer; they count in twelves as well as tens.
- Stone footings and a wall of cut stone below, with timber and mud brick above. Houses of the same, roofed with clay tile or thatch.
- Weaving of linen and wool, pottery, basketry, fishing weirs.
- Storytelling as a trade: some households are known for it, and the winter nights are for it.
- Beacon smoke from the wall to the hamlets in a time of danger.
- Salt they lack and want badly. They have no certain source of it.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None proposed. Their habits are written as tempers, as above, and I ask for no edge over any other people.

## Their magic
I want one among them who works a small thing that cannot be done by hand. It is simple, and it costs her.

The Night Sitter. One woman in the town, with a pupil, holds this office: she sits through the night with the very sick, and with a child in a fever, and by her sitting and a low song the sufferer's breathing steadies and they sleep, where without her they would not. It does not cure. It works against the night of a fever and against the fear in it, not against a hard sickness, and some she sits with die in her keeping. What it takes from her is her own sleep: after each night she works she cannot sleep for the three nights that follow, and her hands shake. Done often, she wears out. A pupil is trained by sitting beside her, and many who try find they cannot do it at all.

There may be one such woman in a few of the hamlets, and most of the people doubt it until the night they need it. It is never common enough to replace the work of hands, and never so rare that a mother cannot hope for it at a sickbed.

I want no other magic among them.

## What they say of gods
On the morning before I come, this is what they say. The dark has a keeper, and the keeper has not yet come to the table. Long ago, they say, a guest was promised to the town, one who comes in the night with a lamp, and the table keeps an empty place and the town keeps a lamp lit for her. They do not agree on what she is, or whether she is kind. Some say she is a traveller's ghost. Some say she is a god. Some say it is a tale to put children to sleep. They tell it at the Dusk Table. I do not claim it is true of the world; it is only what they say, and it may be that they were waiting for something else.

## Rule and worship
I build within my leans and depart from nothing.

Rule: a sacred monarch behind a court. A mortal line holds the throne in my name and is held sacred for it, and the court stands between the throne and the people, so that I am reached through it. In their own belief the line holds the seat for the guest who has not come, and when I come it will hold it in my name, with the court's offices unchanged. The sacred line does not rule by my word, and does not claim my voice.

Worship: local shrines. Household and village shrines, kept by whoever keeps them, no order of priests. The shrine is the niche by the hearth with the lamp in it. A flame lit from my lamp, or from a flame so lit, makes a shrine kept for me. The keeper is whoever tends the hearth in that household: a grandmother, a husband, a child. A village shrine is kept by whoever the village asks.

## Hierarchy
- The seat: the holder of the throne is a widowed woman of middle years, patient and well liked, who has held it eleven winters. That is who she is, never what she will do. She may fail me. The line passes by the court's choice from among the seated family, not by simple descent.
- The court, which stands between the throne and the people and through which I am reached. Its offices, held for life:
  - The Warden of the Door: the keeper of the empty place and the lamp, who receives my word and carries it to the throne, and the throne's word to me. He is careful, slow to trust, and close to the throne. He has kept the empty place for twenty years, and he is not sure it will ever be filled.
  - The Warden of the Wall: commander of the spears and the wall watch, blunt, loyal to the town before the court.
  - The Steward of the Store: the granary, the winter, the stone and the timber. Practical, well liked in the hamlets.
  - The Keeper of the Count: the tally cords, the roll of households and shrines, the count of the children, the account of the year. Exact and a little dry.
- Spears: when it comes to that, about six hundred carry a spear for the town, one adult in four or five, men and some women, called by beacon from the hamlets. About two hundred of them take the wall watch by turns and drill in the dry months, and the rest are farmers with a spear and a shield. They are ordinary fighters, no better and no worse than any other people of this number.
- Disputes: the Gate Bench, three elders who sit at the town gate in the morning: a death, a boundary, a marriage gone bad. A hamlet's household heads settle small things first and send what they cannot to the gate. A marriage gone bad goes first to the mothers of both houses and the household heads, and only then to the bench. The bench does not decide matters of the throne or the god, which go to the court.

I say who the officers are, never what they will do. Each can fail me in play.

## Helpers
The court runs things when I am absent: the Warden of the Door holds my place, and the Steward, the Warden of the Wall and the Keeper of the Count run what is theirs. Shrine keepers are not priests and not officers, though in a kept household they may light and tend the flame in my name from a flame lit from my lamp, as the power allows, and the court sees to which seven households are kept on a night I am away. Orders reach the far end of my people by the court's runners, who walk the road between the town and the hamlets by day, by beacon smoke from the wall in a time of danger, and by the headman of each hamlet. A word of mine to the whole people goes through the Warden of the Door to the throne, and the throne speaks it from the wall at dusk. I do not send orders in dreams, ever.

## How the god meets them
I come down at dusk in autumn, on the stony ground above the bend, with the town below me and the smoke from its kilns and hearths. I come alone, on four legs, with my lamp lit on its cord, and I walk down to the gate. I do not hide. I do not wish to be struck dumb in front of them, nor to stand over them like a king. I want them to be able to ask me things. I will ask to be let in at the gate by the Warden of the Door, as a guest asks, and I will sit at the table, and I will not speak first of what I am. I know I am strange to them: half a hind, and tall, and I cannot pass a low door. I would have a gate and a hall made wide enough for me, and I ask for nothing else until they have asked me. My first work will be to light a flame at the court's own hearth and then at any household that asks, for the flame must come from my lamp, and I walk at the pace of a hind, so it will take time to light a people.

## Questions for you

1. Her own people are those in a household that keeps her shrine. How does a household come to keep one, and can it stop?
A household comes to keep one by choice, at its own door. I will not light a flame in a house that has not asked, and I will not shame a house that does not. The keeper of the hearth takes the flame from my lamp, or from any flame kept from it, and sets it in the niche by the hearth. There is no fee, no due and no vow. A household stops by putting out the flame, and no one is punished for it. The household loses what the shrine gives, and the town asks the cause, as neighbours do, and nothing more. A house that stopped may take the flame again from any kept flame whenever it likes.

2. Seven households a night may be kept. Who chooses which seven, you or your people, and by what custom?
My people choose six, and I keep the seventh. Each dusk the keepers of the town bring the names of households to the Warden of the Door. The households with a deathbed, a newborn, a fever or a fresh grief come first. If more ask than there are places, the Keeper of the Count draws lots from those who asked, so no house buys it, and a house kept three nights in a row stands aside for a fourth so that the nights go round. The seventh place is mine, to put where my heart takes me, and I do not give an account of it. When I am away, the court names all seven, and the households lighted by their keepers count among the seven.

3. You will not rule by dreams, and the throne is reached through a court. What does the court do with something a sleeper says you told them?
The court hears it and writes it in the count as a saying of a dream, and does not act on it as a command. A dream is a dream. The sleeper is neither punished for saying it nor honoured for it. The Warden of the Door asks me, by day, whether I said it, and I answer truly if I remember, and the court acts on my answer by its own decision, not on the dream. A man who says the god told him in a dream to take a field is heard and refused. If I am away, the court holds the saying until I can be asked. I give no orders in dreams, so a sleeper who says I gave one has either mistaken me or is not telling the truth, and the court may take that into account.
