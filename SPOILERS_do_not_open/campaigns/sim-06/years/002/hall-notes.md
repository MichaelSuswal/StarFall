# Hall notes: civ unnamed-people, Year 2

Written by the civ agent under the sealed hall note. This holds what the day did not bring out in the stranger's hearing and what the Keeper needs. Nothing here is in any public file.

## Who knew what, and who told whom

- The Assay-master knew the rack held six from the first time he opened the chest after the laying-out, and twice since, alone, with the second key. Before the day he told one person: the Stallreeve, the evening before, privately, and only that the rack was not full. Nothing of what he thinks of the death. The Stallreeve told no one, and stood at the hall door with the watch. On the day the Assay-master said it to the ring in the stranger's hearing, so it is in the public files.
- The first key hangs in the ring by the custom that a dead keeper's key hangs until a new keeper takes it, and is not turned. I fixed it as: either key opens the chest. The Assay-master used his own, so the first key was never taken down, and no one else has opened the chest since the death. The ring's third motion now requires two of the eleven present to open it.
- The Tally-keeper: last summer, before the stranger came, a clerk from the kingdom upriver called on the old Weightward and asked to see the old weights; she stood witness and holds the kept half; the old man showed the clerk six and said the seventh, the largest, was out for cleaning. She has not said it to anyone. No one asked her plainly for the year's witnessings or plainly whether she knows anything of the seventh in my year, so it did not come out. If the loft or the boatmen lead the ring to ask for the year's witnessings, she answers plainly. One thing I could not square and left alone: the note says "last summer, before the stranger came" and my files have the old Weightward dying "last spring" of the year before the stranger came, so the summer visit sits after his death unless the note means the summer before it. I wrote nothing from this in any public file and chose no dating.

## The old keeper's loft

No one of the eleven had been up since the laying-out. The custom is that the loft stands shut until a new keeper takes the seat. At the weighing the ring agreed, without a count, to look in the loft with three, waiving the custom. They went up on the third day after the weighing: the Assay-master, the Stallreeve, and the clay head as witness. They came down within the hour. I write only that they went. What is seen there, and what they said of it at the ring's next sitting and after, is the Keeper's. In my public files the town waits to hear and has not been told.

## The boatmen at the landing

In the second month, a few days after the carriers' market, the Stallreeve, alone, in the evening, went down to the landing below the hall, among the boatmen who were his old company, and asked them about the old Weightward's last weeks: who came to see him, who he sent word to, whether any boat took anything for him. He asked it of the boatmen at the landing, once. What a boatman answered is the Keeper's, and the thread stops at the question. The town knows only that he went down alone one evening.

## The potters' nest and the others

Nothing sealed in the nest's figures; they are in the public report. I used the sizes in hall pieces of one, two, three, four, six, seven, eight, ten, twelve, fourteen and fifteen (twelve and a half ones to a hall piece), moved by the note's amounts, and rounded to the nearest one: 13, 25, 37, 52, 75, 89, 98, 130, 150, 179, 181.

## What I did not do

I did not have anyone ask the stranger about the seventh in a way that is a charge or a demand. The master of the smiths asked it plainly, and met silence. I did not have the day bring out the Tally-keeper's secret. I did not give the hill weigher any knowledge of why the seat is empty.
