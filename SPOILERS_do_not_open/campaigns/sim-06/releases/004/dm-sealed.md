The second board is written for each way the first can fall, so that you may play on from the first into the second on the same day, through the days between, which are on this sheet. ### The days
- The count. Year 3, month 7, the first month of spring in this country. Months are thirty days. The market falls every third day without a break: in month 7 on days 14, 17, 20, 23, 26 and 29; in month 8 on days 2, 5, 8 and 11. The first board opens on day 14, a market day, in the early afternoon, the gate done.
- The weather, days 14 to 30 of month 7 and the first week of month 8: mild; showers on days 16, 21 and 27, an hour or two each; the River Alder up over the lowest paths until about day 24 and falling after; an ordinary flood, no loss. Dry and bright on day 22 and on day 1 of month 8.
- The ring has set no sitting before the first market of the autumn. Three may call one; nobody has.
- No feast falls in these days.
- The sky. Nothing shows on the nights of days 14 to 29 of month 7. On the night of day 30 the star dims as before, seen from the landing: tell it only if play reaches that night, as what the Weigher sees, with no count. The same on the night of day 6 of month 8.
- The boat people leave the channels for some days at the lowest tides of spring, about the middle of month 8. The Weigher has seen this twice a year.
- The coasters of the ship owner of the open water go out in the first days of month 10. Nobody is pressed about the second word.

### Figures
- The town's old measure, which nobody has said to the Weigher as a rule and which it can work out from its own calls: the unit of the hall's pieces is a quarter of the smallest old bronze, 12 of the Weigher's ones. The hall's eight fired pieces at 12.6 are that unit grown a twentieth heavy.
- The third nest is eleven sizes, the sizes the market uses: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14 and 15 units. Fired to the old six, they should read 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 84, 96, 120, 144, 168 and 180 of the Weigher's ones.
- How the master of the potters makes them, which she says if asked: she cannot hit a weight in the kiln. She shapes each a little heavy, fires, and then rubs each down on a wet stone against the standard on a beam until it sits level. So every piece comes out of the kiln heavy on purpose, by about one part in forty. The rubbing down against the old six must be done at the hall's bench, since no piece of the hall's leaves the hall.
- She has shaped twenty-two, a pair of each size, to choose eleven. They are bone dry. The table gives each piece as the Weigher's scales read it: green, and then fired and cooled. Pieces A and B are the pair already on the boards. Pieces C, D and E are the three spares of each size, which exist only if they are asked for and shaped. All in the Weigher's ones.

- What the table shows, which the Weigher sees only by weighing: this clay loses between one part in twelve and one part in fourteen of its dry weight in the fire, piece by piece, about one part in thirteen across the load. Pieces weighed alike green do not come out alike. That is why she rubs down.
- The Weigher's pans take the largest piece. The old weigher's 193 sat in them.
- A piece broken open, green or fired, shows what any potter's piece shows: a close grey body, no stone in it, no hollow. Nothing is hidden in any piece.
- The fired pieces of the hall, the six old weights, the first and second nests: as on the Year 3 sheet. Nothing has changed them.
- The door-stone of the fourth month: 3,011. It is a door-stone. The record sets no figure for the seventh weight; nobody in the town knows one.
- Prices: the hall pays the potters for the nest in grain and salt, by the eleven's order; the figure is not set. Thirty-three spares are about half as much work and clay again. A price a scene needs beyond that is rolled by you.

### The cast
- The master of the potters, not yet said: Wynne Daub (woman, about 45, of the clay-bank people). One of the eleven. Precise, proud of her work, slowed and not hurt by being shown wrong; says nothing until she has something to say. This season she wants a nest that holds before the Assay-master and the yard, after two that did not serve. She does not know what her clay loses in the fire as a number; she knows it as a feel in the hand and a habit of shaping heavy. She will not say that the cracking of the downs clay worries her more each year, unless asked plainly about the clay.
- Her two firemen, names not set (men, of the clay-bank people): they stack and tend the kiln. They say nothing to the Weightward unless spoken to, and then the plain thing.
- The Assay-master, not yet said: Osric Marl. As on the Year 3 sheet. This season he wants the new nest proved on his beam against the old six before the first market of autumn, and every figure of its making cut. He spoke for each plain figure at the winter sitting and lost the fifth; he says so if asked, and that it was the ring's to refuse.
- The Tally-keeper, not yet said: Annis Furrow. As on the Year 3 sheet. She has a long stick for the day.
- The Stallreeve, not yet said: Tam Wherry. As on the Year 3 sheet. He is at the gate on market mornings and about the market's business between. He asked the Weigher on day 17 of month 6 what it would have cut for the carrier, and was not answered; he does not ask twice, and he waits.
- The ship owner of the open water, not yet said: Davin Sculler. He knows the house of the southern coaster's master by its name (House Sennet, of Port Sennet; her name is Maud Sennet). He says it if the Weigher asks him plainly whether he knows her house; he has not been asked.
- Others the Weigher knows are on the public sheet. A name on neither sheet is not set.

### Held moment: the weighing green, at the potters' hall
Facts:
- When and where. Year 3, month 7, day 14, early afternoon, the gate done. The door of the potters' hall: a long low hall of reed and clay with the kiln yard behind it. The Weigher has come with the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper. Nobody has yet spoken at the door.
- What stands inside. The twenty-two pieces of the third nest, shaped over the winter, bone dry, on two boards along the north wall, a pair of each size, unmarked. The second nest on a shelf. The potters' own beam with brass pans, which the Weigher read level at the gate in the third month. A bench under the window.
- What she has planned, which she says if asked: to stack the kiln on day 16 and fire on day 17 and 18, through one night; to let it cool three days; to open it on the morning of day 22; then to carry the twenty-two to the hall and rub down the best of each pair against the old six on the Assay-master's beam.
- None of the Weigher's askings has been put to anyone: each piece weighed green before the three; three spares of each size; the Stallreeve's leave for a day with the watch at the gate. They are put in the scene.
- Weighing twenty-two pieces, each cut on the stick, takes the rest of the afternoon. It can be done today.
- No one like the Weigher is near. No weight is in the air.

Parameters:
- The master of the potters: wants the nest to hold, and wants the loss in the fire as a number more than she will say at first; will bring each piece to the bench herself, one at a time, and have it weighed green before the three and cut; will shape three spares of each size, thirty-three pieces, though it sets the firing back; will have every piece fired in the one kiln, in one firing, spares among the rest (her kiln takes about sixty pieces of these sizes at a stacking; she has fired twenty-two at a time for weights, and fifty-five is close to its fill); will let one spare of a size be broken, green or fired, if the Weigher asks; will not let any hand but hers or her firemen's carry a green piece; will not fire in two kilns or in two firings; will not have the green figures cried in the yard before the nest is proved, and asks that they stay on the Tally-keeper's stick until the kiln is opened; accepts the Weigher, the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper at the kiln-opening; will not accept the Weigher saying what a piece should weigh ("You say what it is. The old six say what it should be."); stance: in the doorway, clay to the elbows, not moving aside until she knows what is wanted; opening words: "Weightward. Assay-master. You are early for a firing. What do you want of my boards?"
- If she shapes the spares: she shapes them on days 15 and 16; they dry on the kiln's warm floor until day 25; she asks that they be weighed green on day 25, at the hall, the same way (a day with no market); she stacks on day 25, fires on days 26 and 27, and opens the kiln on the morning of day 1 of month 8. If no spares are asked for, her own plan stands: the opening is the morning of day 22.
- If asked who pays for the spares: "The eleven ordered eleven. Ask him." The Assay-master says the hall will pay for clay and charcoal for a proving, on his own word as keeper of the bench, and tell the ring after.
- The Assay-master: wants every figure cut and both weighings done before him; will say the hall pays for the spares; will check any figure on the potters' beam if asked and finds it agrees; will not have a fired piece called the town's weight until it has sat level against the old six on the hall's beam; will not say the Weigher's figures are true, only that the beam agrees; stance: just inside the door, hands behind him; opening words, if spoken to: "Green first. Then the fire. Then my beam."
- The Tally-keeper: wants each piece called once, slowly, by its size and whether first or second of its pair; will cut one long stick for the green figures and leave a run beside each for the fired figure; will scratch a mark on each green piece's foot with her knife point if the master allows it, so the pieces can be told apart after the fire (the master allows it); will not cut a figure she did not hear; stance: on the bench by the window, the stick across her knees; opening words, if spoken to: "Size, and which of the pair. Then the figure."
- The Stallreeve, if the Weigher goes to him at the next market (day 17) or sends: gives a day set aside for the potters' nest, with the watch keeping the gate and no notch cut, as he gave the day at the pans; asks only that he be told the day before, so the yard is told and not surprised. He will not give two market days running.
- Outcomes fall in:
  1. Both askings put as planned. She takes both. The twenty-two are weighed green and cut this afternoon. The spares are shaped, and weighed green on day 25. The firing moves to days 26 and 27 and the opening to day 1 of month 8.
  2. Only the weighing asked. Done this afternoon. Her own days stand: the opening is day 22.
  3. Fewer spares asked (one of each size, or spares of some sizes). She takes it; the firing moves back the same, since drying takes what it takes.
  4. The Weigher asks to mark, seal or keep any piece. Marking by the Tally-keeper's knife: yes. A seal: answer from the card; she asks what it would bind her to and will not have the nest sealed before it is proved. Keeping a piece: no, the pieces are the hall's order.
  5. The Weigher says what the pieces ought to weigh green to come out right. She hears it and says she shapes by hand; the Assay-master says the beam will tell.
  6. The Weigher offers its own hands for the work. She refuses, without heat.
  7. Anything that would bind the hall or the eleven beyond the cost of the spares: carried to the ring by the Assay-master, not answered here.
- To roll: nothing at this board. A question outside it is rolled by you, its odds stated first.

Reasons (for you only; share them, lie about them in a figure's voice, or leave them unsaid):
- She takes the weighing because she asked the Weigher herself, three years ago, to weigh her nest, and because a number for the fire is worth more to her craft than her pride.
- She takes the spares because the nest is the eleven's order and the hall pays, and because a spare she may break tells her what she has never been able to see.
- She fires in one kiln because pieces fired together shrink together, and her first nest taught her what happens otherwise.
- She keeps green pieces in her own hands because a green piece chips at a touch and a chip is weight.
- She wants the green figures kept until the opening because two nests of hers have been read out wrong before the yard.
- She will not be told what a piece should weigh because the ring said the old six are the town's weight, and she has no wish to make a third nest to a stranger's word.
- The Assay-master pays for the spares because proving is his office and the ring gave him the bench.
- The Tally-keeper marks the feet because pieces of a size look alike after the fire and a figure must stay with its piece.
- The Stallreeve gives a day because he gave one before and the market stood it.

### Held moment: the kiln-opening
Facts:
- When and where. If spares were shaped: Year 3, month 8, day 1, first light, a day with no market. If not: month 7, day 22, first light, a day with no market. The kiln yard behind the potters' hall: one round kiln of clay and brick, its door bricked and clayed, cold to the hand since the evening before. The master, her two firemen, the Assay-master and the Tally-keeper with her stick of green figures. The Weigher comes from its dawn at the landing; she has asked the landing's owner the day before to spare it, and he has.
- The firing went as her firings go. She says so if asked: two days and a night of fire, three days of cooling, no rain on the kiln.
- The twenty-two pieces of the nest come out whole, every one. Their fired figures are in the table above, by the mark on each foot.
- The spares, if they were fired, come out whole, every one of them, as many as were shaped. Their fired figures are in the table. A spare is broken only if the Weigher asks for one to be broken.
- What the figures show is in the Figures section. The Weigher sees it by weighing; nobody tells it.
- If play runs on past the weighing: the pieces are carried to the hall that day in reed baskets by the master and her firemen. Over the two days after, at the hall's bench, door open, she rubs down the better of each pair against the old six on the Assay-master's beam. On the third day the eleven chosen sit level with the old six and with one another, to a hair, on his beam; if the Weigher is asked to call them it reads 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 84, 96, 120, 144, 168 and 180. He says the beam agrees. The date the ring set for the town's weight stands. The unchosen eleven and any spares go back to the potters' hall.
- No weight is in the air.

Parameters:
- The master of the potters: wants the kiln opened by her own firemen and every piece set on the board in the order of the marks before anything is weighed; will have each piece weighed fired and cooled before the three and cut beside its green figure; will hear the loss said as a number and say it back; will let one spare of a size be broken open if asked, and looks at the break herself first; will not let a piece of the twenty-two be broken; will not have the nest called good or bad at the kiln ("It is good when it sits level on his beam."); stance: at the kiln door with a mallet, sleeves down, saying little; opening words: "It is cold. Stand back from the door, there is dust."
- The Assay-master: wants the fired figures cut beside the green; will say, if the Weigher gives the loss as a number, "Then cut that too"; will not have the pieces weighed against the old six here, since the six do not leave the hall; opening words, if spoken to: "Fired and cooled. Call them."
- The Tally-keeper: cuts each fired figure in the run she left; will cut the loss for each piece if she is given it as a figure, and a line for the whole load if the Weigher says one; will read the whole stick back at the end; opening words, if spoken to: "By the mark on the foot. I have the green."
- Outcomes fall in:
  1. The fired figures called and cut, the loss said. The scene has what it came for. The master says the number back once and nothing else. The figures stay on the Tally-keeper's stick, which is the hall's; anyone who fires weights may ask her for it.
  2. The Weigher says more than the figures (what she should do next time, what the clay is). She hears it. The Assay-master says the beam will say.
  3. A spare broken. As the Figures section gives. She keeps the pieces.
  4. The Weigher asks that the figures be cried to the yard or sent with any word. Cried: the master says after the beam, and the Assay-master agrees. Sent: not hers; it is for the eleven.
  5. The Weigher is asked nothing here that its decided words do not cover. If it is silent through the weighing but for the figures, nobody presses it.
- To roll: nothing at this board. The spares' fate is a fact, above.

Reasons (for you only; share them, lie about them in a figure's voice, or leave them unsaid):
- She has the pieces laid out by their marks first because a figure cut against the wrong piece is worse than none.
- She will not have the nest judged at the kiln because every piece is heavy on purpose and looks wrong until it is rubbed down.
- She protects the twenty-two because eleven of them are the town's weight and the other eleven are her answer if one chips at the bench.
- The Assay-master keeps the old six in the hall because the last weight that left it has not come back.
- Every piece comes out whole because her firing was sound and the kiln, though fuller than she has fired weights in, was within what it holds.
