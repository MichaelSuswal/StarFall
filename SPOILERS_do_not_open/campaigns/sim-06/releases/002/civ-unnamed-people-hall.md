# Sealed note: civ unnamed-people, Year 2, the hall

For the civ agent of the unnamed people only. It holds what some of your people know about the old weights, and what the stranger's scales find in the hall. It is sealed because the stranger has not yet seen any of it, and your other release is in a public folder.

## How to use it
- Each fact below is known to the mortals named beside it, and to no one else until the day shows it. A mortal says what he or she knows only when asked a plain question about it or when the day forces it, by the temper your people files fix.
- You are not told why anything here is as it is. Your people do not know. Do not reason toward a cause and do not have a mortal state one as known; a mortal may guess, and a guess is a guess.
- What the hall sees and hears on the day of the weighing may be written in your public files afterward, as the hall saw it. The lines marked `not for public files` stay in this note and in nobody's mouth unless the day brings them out in the stranger's hearing; if it does, say exactly who said what, in your report.
- If for any reason the chest is not opened before the stranger this year, write nothing of its contents in any public file.

## The chest
- The old weights stand in a wooden rack inside the chest your people files give, in the hall, in the room under the old keeper's loft. The Assay-master holds the second key, as your files say. Who has held the old man's own key since his death is yours to fix, as your people would have done it.
- The rack is cut for seven weights. It holds six. The seat for the largest is empty, and it is clean where the other seats are dusty. Anyone who sees the rack sees this.
- The Assay-master has known since he first opened the chest after the death. This is what he means when he says he does not know the true state of what is in it. Whom he has told, if anyone, before the day is yours; your people file says he has told nobody what he thinks of the death.
- The eleven, as a body, have not seen the rack since the old man died. Some of the older heads know the set was seven; it was shown seldom.
- The Tally-keeper, `not for public files` unless the day brings it out: last summer, before the stranger came, a clerk from the kingdom upriver called on the old Weightward and asked to see the old weights. She stood witness and holds the kept half of that witnessing. The old man showed the clerk six and said the seventh, the largest, was out for cleaning. She has not volunteered this to anyone. Asked plainly for the year's witnessings, or asked plainly whether she knows anything of the seventh weight, she answers plainly.
- The old keeper's loft, `not for public files`: no one has cleared it. His coat and boots hang on a peg there. No one of the eleven has been up since the laying out. If someone goes up this year, say who and when in your report, and write only that they went; what is seen there the Keeper gives.
- The boatmen at the landing below the hall are not in the hall on the day. If anyone goes among them this year asking about the old man's last weeks, say who asked and what was asked, in your report, and stop there; what a boatman answers the Keeper gives.

## What the six are, and what the stranger calls
The three smallest are old bronze, the three largest are cut stone. All are plain old work, well made, and they read as any weight reads. The three bronze pieces fit the stranger's pans. The three stone pieces are too heavy for them and go on the hall's beam. In the stranger's own ones (its smallest cup is one; its whole nest is one hundred and twenty-seven), smallest piece first:

| Seat | Stuff | What the stranger calls |
|---|---|---|
| first | bronze | 48 |
| second | bronze | 96 |
| third | bronze | 192 |
| fourth | stone | 384 |
| fifth | stone | 766 |
| sixth | stone | 1,536 |
| seventh | (the seat is empty) | nothing is called; there is nothing to weigh |

- Each piece is twice the one below it, as near as the stranger's ones can tell, but for the fifth, which is light by two in seven hundred and sixty-eight. It has an old chip at its foot. The stranger calls 766 and says nothing of the chip or of what the piece ought to be.
- On the Assay-master's beam: each piece balances two of the one below wherever he has the pieces to try it, and the fifth comes up a hair light against two of the fourth. His beam and the stranger's calls do not differ anywhere. So there is no place where two figures are cut for one piece, unless he misreads; whether he does is yours.
- Of the empty seat the stranger says nothing unless asked. If one of the eleven asks it a question about the missing piece (where it is, what it weighed, whether it can find it), that question is not one its decided words answer. Mark it `not answered by the god's words`. If it is put as a demand or a charge before the ring, treat it as held.

## The hall's own pieces against the old ones
- By the old keeper's practice, which the Assay-master knows, four of the hall's small fired pieces are meant to make the first, smallest old weight.
- A year ago at the hall door the stranger called the hall's eight agreeing pieces at about twelve and a half of its ones each. Four of them come to about fifty. The first old weight is 48. So the hall's good pieces run heavy against the old standard by about one part in twenty. The Assay-master's beam shows the same thing if he sets four of the hall's pieces against the first bronze: the hall's side goes down.
- No one asks the stranger to say this and it does not say it: it calls pieces, and the sum is the hall's to do. The Tally-keeper has last year's figures on her stick. Who does the sum first, and whether aloud, is yours.

## The potters' new nest of eleven
If the eleven have it weighed, the stranger calls each piece in its ones. You hold what sizes the nest was made in. Take each piece as it would be if it were made true to the hall's eight good pieces (about twelve and a half ones to one hall piece), and then move it by this much, smallest piece first: heavy by one part in sixty; true; light by one in forty; heavy by one in thirty; true; heavy by one in fifty; light by one in sixty; heavy by one in twenty-five; true; heavy by one in forty; light by one in thirty. Work the figures out, round each to the nearest one, and have the stranger call them. So the new nest does not agree with itself, and all of it sits about a twentieth heavy against the old weights, as the hall's pieces do.
