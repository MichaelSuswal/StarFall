# Negotiation: god-beasts, Night 3

## Round 1
### What was read and not priced
- You proposed no edge, so nothing is priced against a weakness and no weakness is owed.
- The habits and prides you wrote are taken as tempers: who your people are, able to fail. None is a guarantee.
- What you said of the land and its past is written as your wish or your people's belief. The world decides.
- Who each office holder is stands as who they are, never what they will do.
- Everything under Skills and culture is ordinary and free.

### Your landing: the helpers and the seat
Draft text: You come to a people that does not know you. The band is its own, the Council of the Fire is the band's council, and the Speaker, the Houndwarden, the Marchwarden, the Saltkeeper and the Runners serve the council under its own custom. You have no fixed seat by your own choice of people, and that is within your lean; no place among them is yours at landing either. So the form is "designed, not yet serving": you land with no helpers in fact and no seat that is yours in fact. Until each office is won in play, nobody acts for you while you are away, your unplayed time runs on your standing conduct alone, and your people go their own way under their own council. That is the cost, the same for any god who lands so. It is not a refusal of a default, it bears on no edge, and it buys nothing.
Choices: (a) Keep the meeting as you wrote it, at the cost above. The offices stand as you described them, each under your people's own custom, and each becomes your helper when its holder hears you and takes your word, in play. (b) Say that your people already know you when you land: that you have been among them, or that they know you on sight for whose they are. Then the offices you described serve you from landing, your word is carried while you are away, and the meeting is rewritten so, in your words. Neither way is priced and neither buys anything.
The Keeper's reasons: an office can carry the word only of one its holder knows. What you wrote has your people holding their offices under their own law, or for one they have not met. The cost is said here plainly so that you choose knowing it.
Accept, refuse or counter, and give your reasons.

### The spears
Draft text: About nine hundred who can carry a spear, of whom perhaps three hundred are hunters by trade. Ordinary, not priced.
The Keeper's reasons: a force that is kept as a body, paid or fed for it and drilled, is ordinary up to about one in fifty of a people, and an edge above that. Hunters who hunt for their living are not such a body, and the rest are a call-up.
### Their magic
Draft text: The one you described, the old woman who can sit by a herd at dusk and tell its mood and its trouble, at the cost you wrote. Fitted as you wrote it: ordinary in this world, and no edge. Her own cost is the whole price. She trains no one, as you wrote; you cannot make another by telling, and it ends with her unless play makes another. There may be one or two others among five thousand who work some other small thing, as among most peoples; who they are is your people's own.
The Keeper's reasons: how much of it there is comes from the world and is the same for every people.
### Names
Draft text: The name you give your people, and the names you hope for two office holders, are written as names given, in your words. Whether a mortal you named turns out as you hope is that mortal's.

Accept, refuse or counter each item, and give your reasons. Accepting everything is a choice you have, not the default.
