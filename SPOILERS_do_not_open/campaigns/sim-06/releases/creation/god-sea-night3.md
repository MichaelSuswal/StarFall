# Release: god-sea, Night 3

## Why you come down
Gods come down here to lead a people: to find or gather them, to build, to
rule, and to spread. That is what the world below expects of you.

## The land you come down into
This says only what the land itself would show you as you come down. Nothing here is a distance, a direction or a promise of what is in view from one spot; those come later, from the map.

The season: Autumn.

The country: A cold, broken shore. Shingle underfoot, a headland standing over it, and a coast that turns in and out in small coves, with skerries off it where the sea breaks white. In this season the wind comes hard off the water, the days shorten fast, weed lies thick on the stones after each blow, and the first of the winter storms is not far off.

Marks in plain sight: A small harbor's wall. Smoke.

The whole land is about a thousand miles across. On the sheet it will be drawn on, a day's walk is a small fraction of one grid cell.

## What is so of the world for a people
Mortal magic is simple, and it costs the worker. The Keeper fits a people's magic to the world on this night.

The peoples you will find are at about this level: before the
medieval era, no steel forges. That is where the world begins, not a
ceiling. You may bring in what you know, but it is built here by the people,
from what the world has, at the pace of the work, and you say how it is to be
made; nothing is granted by naming it.

## The people you lead
You come to a people with a seat, a court or council, and helpers who run
things when you are not there. Who are they?
Size: Small: one to three thousand. The usual number, about five thousand with
a town or city, is the middle of the range, not what is expected of you.
Rule: A sacred monarch behind a court. A mortal line holds the throne in the god's name and is held sacred for it; a court stands between the throne and the people, and the god is reached through it.
Worship: None. It asks no rites; what the people do about it is theirs.
Build within these, or give your reasons not to. A larger people
is not priced against a weakness; it costs what the world makes it cost (more
food and fuel, slower growth, harder to move, a longer gestation for a larger
kind of people).

Say, as you describe them:
- What kind of people they are (mortal folk of the common kind, or some other), how they live in country like this, and what fills their days.
- What is always so of them: what they honor, and what the worst of them would still not do.
- Whether any among them works a thing that cannot be done by hand. It is simple where it exists, and it costs the one who works it. If you want such a one among your people, say who, what they do, and what it takes from them.
- What they say of gods, on the morning before you come.
- The hinge: when you touch that ground, do your people already hold their offices and know whose they are? Were they waiting for something? Or do you come to a people that has never heard of you?
- How many carry a spear for them, when it comes to that.
- Who settles the rest among them: a death, a boundary, a marriage gone bad.
- When you are away, who runs things, by what offices, and how a word of yours reaches the far end of your people.

## Options
Each is offered once. None is expected of you.
- Ordinary culture, customs and skills are free: how they live, what they make, how they keep count, how they gather. Any real edge over other peoples is priced against a weakness you offer, as a power is. As a measure of size: a real edge is one that would tell in a contest with another people of the same number, and its weakness is one that would tell as much the other way.
- A habit or pride of your people (that they are hard to fool, say, or slow to anger) is taken as a temper: who they are, able to fail. If you want it as a guarantee that cannot fail, say so and it is priced as an edge.
- You may say who a mortal or an office holder is (patient, well liked, close to you). That is who they are, never what they will do; each can fail you in play.
- A rule one of your people swears to is an oath, and an oath can be broken; it is not priced. A rule a mortal cannot break is a binding, and a binding is a priced edge on you.
- Their name as a people, and the name of their home, are yours to give. Nobody else gives them: what you leave unnamed stays unnamed in every record until a mortal names it in play.
- Whatever you say about the land or its past is written down as your wish or your people's belief. The world decides, and you will see.

## Questions for you
1. Your lamp may be refilled and tended by anyone once you have lit it. Who among your people tends lamps, and would they know what they were tending?
2. You wish to be seen rarely, and your people reach you through a court. How does a word of yours get to the court, and how does theirs get to you, when you lie in the water?
3. Timber you join does not rot. Whose boats and quays get it first, and who decides?
