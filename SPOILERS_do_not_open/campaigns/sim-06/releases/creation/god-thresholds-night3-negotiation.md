# Negotiation: god-thresholds, Night 3

## Round 1
### What was read and not priced
- You proposed no edge, so nothing is priced against a weakness and no weakness is owed.
- The habits and prides you wrote are taken as tempers: who your people are, able to fail. None is a guarantee.
- What you said of the land and its past is written as your wish or your people's belief. The world decides.
- Who each office holder is stands as who they are, never what they will do.
- Everything under Skills and culture is ordinary and free.

### Your landing: the helpers and the seat
Draft text: You come to a people that does not know you. The winter city and the passes are your people's own, the warlord holds the host and the First Doorwarden the rite under their own custom, and the seat and the unlatched door are kept for a Guest your people have not met. You wrote that the household has been taught to bid in the first one who stoops at the door: that is who they are, never what they will do. So the form is "designed, not yet serving": you land with no helpers in fact and no seat that is yours in fact. Until each office is won in play, nobody acts for you while you are away, your unplayed time runs on your standing conduct alone, and your people go their own way under their own warlord and keepers of rite. That is the cost, the same for any god who lands so. It is not a refusal of a default, it bears on no edge, and it buys nothing. Where your people keep a seat or a place for one they await, they keep it for that one and do not know it is you: an office is won when its holder takes you for the one awaited and takes your word, and not before.
Choices: (a) Keep the meeting as you wrote it, at the cost above. The offices stand as you described them, each under your people's own custom, and each becomes your helper when its holder hears you and takes your word, in play. (b) Say that your people already know you when you land: that you have been among them, or that they know you on sight for whose they are. Then the offices you described serve you from landing, your word is carried while you are away, and the meeting is rewritten so, in your words. Neither way is priced and neither buys anything.
The Keeper's reasons: an office can carry the word only of one its holder knows. What you wrote has your people holding their offices under their own law, or for one they have not met. The cost is said here plainly so that you choose knowing it.
Accept, refuse or counter, and give your reasons.

### How many: the cost of eighteen thousand
Draft text: A people of eighteen thousand is not priced against a weakness. It costs what the world makes it cost, and these are the costs the Keeper will apply in play: (1) Food and fuel. High broken country does not feed eighteen thousand from its own grass and barley. Your people live in part on tolls in kind and on carrying, and a closed pass, a bad summer on the high grass or a lost season of carrying is felt in the stores before the same loss would be felt by a smaller people. The winter ground must hold fodder and fuel for all of them from first snow to thaw. (2) Moving. A whole people on the move with its old, its young and its herds goes at about half a walker's pace, and columns of this size stretch over days of road; the host cannot turn or gather quickly. (3) Growth. So large a people in such country grows slowly, and a hard winter takes more than it gives back for some years. (4) Grass. Herds for eighteen thousand strip a valley, so the host cannot stay long in one place outside the winter ground.
Choices: accept these as written, or argue any of them, or take a smaller number.
The Keeper's reasons: you wrote that you accept the cost and did not ask for the number to be cheap. These are that cost said plainly, from the country you come down into.
### The war band
Draft text: Nine hundred sworn spears under eight captains, with three thousand more who carry a spear when the host calls.
Choices: (a) About three hundred and sixty sworn spears under the eight captains, with the rest of the nine hundred among those who come when the host calls. Ordinary, at no price. (b) Nine hundred sworn spears, as you wrote it, as an edge of your people, priced against a weakness of your people that you offer: one that would tell as much against them as the larger band tells for them.
The Keeper's reasons: a force that is kept as a body, paid or fed for it and drilled, is ordinary up to about one in fifty of a people, and an edge above that. Nine hundred sworn in eighteen thousand is one in twenty. Your rule lean gives you a warlord who rules through a war band and captains; it does not set the band's size, and a band of three hundred and sixty is a war band. The call-up is ordinary at any number a people could raise.
### The two tempers
Draft text: That they are hard to ambush in a pass, and that they are slow to turn a stranger away, are tempers: who they are, able to fail, as you wrote them. Neither is read as an edge, neither is priced, and neither needs a weakness; the ways you wrote that each can fail are part of who they are.
### Their magic
Draft text: The salt wives, not more than a dozen among all your people, each laying a line of salt and waking when it is crossed, knowing only that it was crossed and by how many, while she is within a day's walk, twenty miles, of the line; at the cost you wrote. Fitted as you wrote it: ordinary in this world, and no edge. Her own cost is the whole price. It is a mortal's working and no part of your own powers: a line of salt is not one of your marks, tells you nothing unless she tells you, and counts toward none of your numbers. You cannot make another by telling; such a one comes as the world gives her.
The Keeper's reasons: how much of it there is comes from the world and is the same for every people. Twelve in eighteen thousand is fewer than the world would give.
### Names
Draft text: The name you give your people, and the names of the two office holders you wrote, are written as names given, in your words. Whether a mortal you named turns out as you wrote is that mortal's.
