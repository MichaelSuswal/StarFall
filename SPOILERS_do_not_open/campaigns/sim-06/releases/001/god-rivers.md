# Release: god-rivers, Year 1, the year resolved

What you saw, heard, were told and felt in Year 1, and what your people report. You read this at the opening of Year 2, in place of a last year's release. It is your go for nothing by itself; your Year 2 release names your files.

## What you did and what came of it
- The listening at the spring head gave you the stream's morning: a town waking two miles down, clean water, people drinking and crossing at its gate, and, farther off and muddled, a ford where the brook meets a larger river. No pull. You slept, unwatched and unharmed.
- A ferry child saw you first at the water gate and called the Seat Warden, whose gate house hearth was the first hearth. She offered you the cup from the low seat, as a courtesy to one who came from the water's side. You drank. She fed you and asked nothing until you had eaten.
- About the ninth day the Speaker, newly chosen after the flood, gave you the council's hearing. The head of the oldest kin house stood against your counting. The council ruled that you may stay as a guest at the guest bench beside the low seat, not on it, and may count, with the Reckoner walking beside you with a second cord. By winter the Reckoner said your counts never differed.
- No one takes you for a god. The ferry keepers took to you for your questions about the water. The eldest midwife told the council you have a gift like her own with a worse cost. The Ferry Elder took your word on the marks and the store as counsel.
- Warm hands, three times: a child of the second hamlet fevered after a channel pull, a fevered woman in another hamlet, a man in the town with a reed knife cut gone bad. Each mended, and you paid the chill in days as your card gives. The hamlets say you have the midwives' touch in a larger measure, or that the water was kind.
- At your asking, and as their own: the ferry keepers cut flood marks on the gate stone, this year's and four earlier by the old ferrymen's telling; the council set aside about one part in twenty of the harvest and some smoked fish and eel; the well keepers told you and the council at once when the lower well turned, and closed and relined it.
- In autumn you listened half a day in the running water below the gate with the Seat Warden, a well keeper and a ferry keeper sitting watch. You slept a day and a night under a reed mat shelter with a fire. They called you and shook you and could not wake you; the town gathered and watched. Nothing came against you but a stray dog, driven off. What the water told: a day of the river's talk, old and new together; the salt carriers at the ford saying they cross less often; the deep water lying farther from the ford than it did; cold coming down from the hills. No pull.
- You wintered at the council fire and went back to the spring head at the thaw.
- The spring flood that opened the year was ordinary. When it went down the deep water lay a little farther from the ford again, the fourth flood running, the ferry keepers say.

## Asked of you and not answered
- The children, in winter: "Where do you come from?" You said the water's side; they asked again and you told a story.
- The Seat Warden will not say whether the cup waited for you.

## What the Keeper ruled
Your readings of your card are right as you stated them. The town lies about two miles down the stream from the spring head where you woke; the town's own spring is by its wall and is another.

## The sky
The star dimmed on three nights of your year: two in the first month, some days apart, and one in the fifth. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. Your people have seen such nights in other years. This year many of them tied the nights to you.

## Your people at the year's turn
About 2,000, loud, quarrelsome and kind, worried about the ford. A child named you the Counting Woman in winter and it has spread. Each of the four reed hamlets told you its name when you asked.
