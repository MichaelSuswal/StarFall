# Release: god-secrets, Year 1, the year resolved

What you saw, heard, were told and felt in Year 1, and what your people report. You read this at the opening of Year 2, in place of a last year's release. It is your go for nothing by itself; your Year 2 release names your files.

## What you did and what came of it
- The watch at the land gate entered you as a guest. The watchman called you the Counter to the next man, and it has stuck. The Clerk of the Tally drew a mark for you of his own: scales over a short stroke.
- The Granary Warden, with the Clerk standing by, showed you the harvest and store books, not the council's. The tally by the Warden's book and the tally by the sticks differ by a few measures in a hundred; you said so twice. The Warden said rats and damp. The count aloud in the second month, with witnesses, came a little under a twentieth short of his book, and the Crier cried it at market as the Clerk's count.
- The Clerk took your counsel as a trader's. The Boat Master took one weighing of a cargo. The Gate Warden did not like your answers and set a quiet watch on your room for a time. Late in the third month the council gave you a stool at the foot of the long table, on the stores only, five to four. You are not on the rolls.
- In winter you were asked three times at the table for a figure and gave it. In the deep of the winter a woman who keeps the tallies of the family that coopers and brews sat by you unasked and told you a thing in trust. You did not stop her. She asked whether the sum closed and you said it did not. You hold one kept thing.
- Your night as the dog: no dog of the town barked at you. You found the houses of two who work small things, by the marsh edge and behind the market street. A bone-setter woke to you lying across her threshold and gave you water. A quay watchman and a boy saw a brown dog they did not know. No one entered your room. You paid the day and the words.
- In spring the Clerk told you, over a cup: there are tallies of an old league in a chest in his house that no one can fully read; a private book holds a list of names sung by walkers when the old were children, which he will not show; twenty and some of those names are unlike any on the rolls.
- In the eighth month you walked the open ground you named with the key in your hand. You felt nothing put away on purpose in any of it. A man of the Gate Warden's watched you from a distance and saw you stop four times.
- In the tenth month you walked the worn track south-west. By the map it runs 37.5 miles, three days, and ends on open rolling grass with small streams, a plain country of drovers' trails. You met drovers bringing stock toward the town and no one else coming up. You were back inside the fortnight and looked first at the store.
- No office holder knows what you are. You felt no other like yourself.

## Asked of you and not answered
- The council, by those against you: "Who is he that will not give a name?" The Clerk answered for you.

## What the Keeper ruled
- The people of the town are your own people from the hour you came down; no roll makes or unmakes that. Their hidings are closed to you, as you chose to read it.
- The key is in your keeping only while it is on you. Left anywhere else, whoever picks it up holds it. That night no one did.
- Two small things of your own showed themselves. You keep figures you have read; the table saw it. As the dog you are let lie and given water where a strange dog would be driven off. Both are written on your card as knacks. Neither is a power.

## The sky
The star dimmed on three nights of your year: two in the first month, some days apart, and one in the fifth. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. Your people have seen such nights in other years. This year many of them tied the nights to you.

## Your people at the year's turn
About 5,000, busy and a little sour over the dredging of the river gate, where loaded boats grounded twice in spring. The lowest cellars took water again. Word has gone down the river that a counter sits at the foot of the council's table.
