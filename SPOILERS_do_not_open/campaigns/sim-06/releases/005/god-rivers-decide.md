# Decide: god-rivers, Year 5

## Why you come down
Gods come down here to lead a people: to find or gather them, to build, to
rule, and to spread. That is what the world below expects of you.

## The year
Year 5 begins at the turn of the year, in your waking season, spring, and runs round the four seasons to the same season again. A year here is twelve months. Your fourth year on the ground is over. The whole of Year 5 is yours to decide tonight. You will be told what came of it in your next release.

## What your people tell you
Copied whole from your people's own report of Year 4 (`SPOILERS_do_not_open/campaigns/sim-06/civs/unnamed-people-rivers/years/004-report.md`, the section "What the people tell their god"). It is their telling, in their words, and they may be wrong.

These are the town's words, put together by office as each would say it. They are the town's telling of its year at home, not of the going.

The Ferry Elder says: The flood mark on the gate stone is the eighth. The deep water has moved again from the ford stones, on the side away from the weirs, a little, the seventh flood running; I read it with you and said so, and I have no better word than that it is so. The low-water cord stands at twelve marks at the gate stone and twelve at the ford stone. The weir hands measured with their own pole at the low water, and I stood on the bank with my knee. Behind the oldest weir the bed is shoaled nine hands over the footing logs, behind the second seven, behind the third five, behind the newest of the four three, behind the first small fish weir one, behind the second a finger. The older the weir, the deeper the shoal. That is two things that go together, and two things that go together are two things. I gave my girl a pole when she was twelve and sent her to the ford and no farther, and she held to it. I counted five out at the landing post, and I count twice. The larger river is not the town's.

The Reckoner says: The cord stands at seven knots of the dimmed star this year, and three, five, six and seven for the four years; it does not count by ones. Six of the seven in the town, one away, the third, on the climb. Across the two years of marks the cord stood at ten nights at the sitting in the autumn, eight in the town and two away, and stands at thirteen now, eleven in the town and two away; you were away about one night in fifteen of the two years, and the star came while you were away two in thirteen, which I do not think is nothing and do not think is something. I keep the cord of the weir figures beside the marks as you asked, and it is the town's. Your counts and mine still never differ. A cord says what, and not why.

The head of the weir hands says: We measured because the council said so and because it was our pole. The silt behind a weir is the brook's own, which was there before the weirs and settles wherever the water slows. The oldest weir has the most years. If the deep water comes back to the ford stones I will eat my pole. The oldest weir takes about one part in five of the town's eel, three parts in four of it in the run of the autumn dark moon, and the gap costs about one part in six of that weir's year, which is about one part in thirty of the town's eel, and the town has made it good by cord. It is the first time the town has unset a weir, and my hands did it because nobody else knew where the pegs were.

The head of the oldest kin house says: The oldest weir is my house's. If it was wrong it was my house that set it wrong, and my house that should unset it. I asked which weir, and you did not know, and that was a plain answer. I asked whether the star is yours, and I would have the answer from you. I still ask what you want.

The Speaker says: I was called back, and I ask the plain question. The council heard you in one sitting and talked three evenings, and answered what it could answer on its own figures, which was the weir, and said that the rest will wait for the flood.

The Seat Warden says: The cup was filled each morning and has not been poured out. I kept the cord of the boy's word and your answer, and the herd keeper's, and the pasture's word, and said it back at the autumn sitting. I kept the question the weirs' people left with me the morning the star dimmed while you were away, and said it back.

The head herd keeper says: Nothing strayed. The pasture counted at dawn and dusk and kept three hands. The herd is nearly back to what it was before the loss, and the winter's milk and fat were less short. I watched you sleep and I have put it in the cord that you walk without tiring and sleep when the river asks. The offer stands, one more pair of feet.

The eldest midwife says: About seventy five were born and about sixty died, the old and the infants, as in other winters. I sent word of the child in the fever, and you held her. The star's pains come faster, and it has not borne.

The children say: The small boy said it first. The ferry child has a pole and has been to the ford. The old women say a spring that walks is only the spring going down the long way.

The town says: Salt is out. The carriers came less often again and asked more, and the council took what was offered and rationed it by house cord. We set one part in twenty of the barley and millet by again and smoked fish and eel with little salt, because salt is what we buy. The harvest was ordinary and the flood was ordinary in height. The Wary say the star dims when you stay, and the ferry keepers say it dimmed on the climb, and the Counters say the cord is the town's. The weir hands are sore, and the ferry keepers are glad, and the council has said it will speak again after the flood. We ask nothing of you that we did not ask last year. We are curious, steady and a little worried, more about the salt and the weirs than the star.
