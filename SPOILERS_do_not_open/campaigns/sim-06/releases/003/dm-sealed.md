### The days

- Days 6 to 8, which the Weigher has not yet seen: the same weather as day 5. Mist on the channels at first light, gone by the middle of the morning; dry; warm in the afternoon; a light wind off the Gulf toward evening. No rain before the third month. The Alder is low, as at every summer's end.

### Held moment: the ring's yes, and its asking for the words
Facts:
- When and where. Year 3, month 1, day 5, a market morning, about an hour after first light. The foot of the gatepost of the town's house. The line is formed, about forty people, the fish seller at its head.
- What the eleven did. On day 2, after naming the old six the town's weight, the ring took up what the Weigher had asked of the Stallreeve a year before and said yes, without a count. It named the ship owner of the open water to carry the word. It sent the Stallreeve to ask for the words. It gave him no leave to judge them (r-0348): he was sent to ask and to carry.
- What the eleven mean by the plain terms, which the Stallreeve says again if asked and does not add to: what each of the hall's pieces is now, in the Weigher's ones; and that the eleven have said which is the town's weight. If asked what they said: the old six, from the first market of the autumn that comes round next year; bargains made before then reckoned by the present pieces; the potters to fire a nest to the old six in spring; the seventh seat stays as it is.
- The stick. The Tally-keeper cut the eleven's asking on a split stick on the evening of day 2: the day, the eleven's mark, the open-water owner's mark, and a blank run for the word. The Stallreeve has laid one half at the foot of the post; she holds the kept half. By the town's custom, as it was with the two half-sticks already in the Weigher's coat: taken up, the half is a yes to the asking as spoken, and the Weigher then owes the words; left lying, the asking stands unanswered.

- The Good-Faith Reading, if the player chooses to use it on a bargain struck here: the Stallreeve means to keep the terms as he speaks them, and the eleven meant their yes. The Weigher told the ring it reads no one in the town without their asking; nobody here asks. The card's drawback holds if it reads anyway.
- No one like the Weigher is near. No weight is in the air.

Reasons (for you only; share them, lie about them in a figure's voice, or leave them unsaid):
- The Stallreeve takes only the plain terms on his own word because the eleven sent him to ask and not to judge, the ring is split on the seat and keeps its say close, and a year ago he told the Weigher himself that such a word was not his to give.
- He wants it said at the post and cut because a word nobody saw given is a word somebody can say the stranger wrote alone.
- He is in no hurry because the coasters do not go until summer and because he would sooner be a little late than wrong.
- He will not have the word go by another hand because the eleven named the carrier, and a second carrier is a second word.
- The Tally-keeper cuts only what is spoken because a stick is worth what was said, and she is a little afraid of mistakes.
- The Assay-master wants the fifth said as 766 because he read it level first and wrong, said so before the ring, and will not have the town's word tidier than the weighing was.
- He will not say the calls are true because he has a beam and no way to weigh a god; agreeing is as far as his test goes.
- He will not have a piece leave the hall because the last thing that left it has not come back.
- The yard is quiet because the weight has been the town's shame up the river and down the coast, and it wants to hear how the shame is to be said.
- The ship owner wants a short word and a stick because he trades by other people's weights and has been asked about this town's at every quay; he takes no fee because he will not be said to have been hired by the one who keeps the weights.
- He will not carry a closed thing he did not see filled because he answers for his cargo to every house he sells to.

### Other sealed matter
- The seventh weight: what three in this scene hold and the Weigher does not. None of it is offered. Each is said only as written here.
  - The Tally-keeper. Asked plainly whether she knows anything of the seventh weight, or asked plainly for the old Weightward's witnessings of his last year, she answers plainly, there or wherever she is asked: in the summer before his last winter a clerk from the kingdom upriver called on him and asked to see the old weights; he showed six and said the seventh, the largest, was out for cleaning; she stood witness and holds the kept half. She knows no more than that and guesses at nothing. Asked anything less plain, she says what was asked and no more.
  - The Stallreeve. Asked plainly what the boatmen told him, or why the channel was dragged: at the post, before the yard, he says "Not at the post. At dusk, if you want it." Asked away from the line, or at dusk, he tells it: a boatman lent the old man a punt on a night of the spring flood, his last spring, and got it back before light with silt in the bottom and a wet rope, and thought nothing of it, since old men fish eels on the flood. He names the boatman only if asked for the name, and the name is not set. He gives no reading of it.
  - The Assay-master. Asked plainly whether the ring heard more than the loft: "Yes. It was not my seeing, and I told you I would tell what I saw." Asked whose it was: the Stallreeve's and the Tally-keeper's. He says no more of it.
  - The ring has had all of this since the winter sitting of Year 2 and has given the looking up. Nothing of it has been said in the Weigher's hearing but the loft. Where the weight is, nobody in the town knows.
