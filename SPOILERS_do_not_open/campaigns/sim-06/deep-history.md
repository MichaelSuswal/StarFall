# Deep history: sim-06

Written on creation Night 3b, 2026-11-24. The truth of the land's past, to the depth the surveys set (9: many thousands of years, ruins under ruins, with gaps). No agent but the Keeper and the checker reads it. What each people tells of this, partly wrong, is in that people's own record; the truth stays here.

Dates are years before the arrival, written "BA". Year 1 is the first year after it. Rolled dates carry their roll ids. A date with no id was set by the Keeper from the rolled dates round it.

## The star over this country

- The star hangs over the middle of Whitmoor [r09], above the Whitmoor Ring [f76], and has never moved against the land.
- It hung at one height, about 136 miles, until 1,668 BA [r-0177]. Since then it has been sinking, slowly at first and faster each century. Tonight it stands 94 miles over the Ring [r-0161]. It comes down in Year 485 [r-0160]. The shape of the descent is the Keeper's, set before the rolls (keeper log, 3b.4): the height falls as the fourth root of the time left.
- What that gives a watcher. From the Gannel Stones [f80], 94.8 miles from the Ring on foot over open ground, the star stood 55.2 degrees above the level in 2,556 BA, 54.1 in 1,350 BA, 49.3 in 430 BA, and stands 44.8 tonight. In the first fifteen years of play it sinks there by about a quarter of a degree, and by about six tenths in forty. From the delta, 305.2 miles from the Ring, it stands 17.1 degrees up tonight and sinks about an eighth of a degree in fifteen years.
- It is a blue disk about eight miles across: a degree and a half wide from the delta, near five degrees from under it. On a clear night it gives light enough to walk by. By day it is a pale mark in the sky.
- On some nights it dims for about an hour, and while it is dim a thread of blue light stands between it and the Ring. The count for each year is in the sealed phenomena file and nowhere else.
- The last fall here was 60,400 BA [r-0159]. What it left is a layer of blue-grey glass that rings when struck, under everything else, reached only where something has been dug or cut very deep.

## The ages, oldest first

### Before any telling (60,400 BA to about 9,000 BA)
The fall poured new country over spent ground: rivers that cut their beds in a season, timber, and beasts of forms no one now has seen. The Whitmoor Ring [f76] is older than that fall. It stood under it and came through, as a thing dense enough does. The water folk are the last of the first people of the new country: they were on the gulf when it was twice its present size, and their saying that the towns stand on their old fishing grounds is true. The great beasts dwindled as the land aged. Their bones lie in the Cliff of Bones [f32] and under Ribhill [f42].

### The reed and shell peoples (about 9,000 BA to 5,300 BA)
The human story here begins with fishers and reed cutters on the Gulf of Maun [r31] and the southern rivers. They left shell heaps under the Reedmouths [r14] silt and nothing with a name on it. Where they came from no record says.

### The mound builders (5,300 BA [r-0166] to about 4,400 BA)
A farming people of the western and southern river lands who buried their dead with great care. They raised the first of the Morrow Mounds [f56] and all of the Ellow Barrows [f52]. Their hunters killed the last herd-mother of the great beasts on the Farrow Grass [r22], at Ribhill [f42], with the help of a worker who held the beast still while they speared it. That was the first great working this land remembers in its ground: the grass on Ribhill [f42] has had no seed heads since, and herds will not graze it. The mound builders ended in a sickness that came down the rivers in three summers. The living left the valleys. The songs the bend town still sings at the Ellow Barrows [f52] are theirs, in their tongue, learned by ear by the people who came after.

### The Kennoch rule (2,556 BA [r-0165] to about 1,900 BA)
Hill kings of the Kennoch Heights [r02] who cut the Pass Road [f65], the steps of Stair Pass [f62] and the Kennoch Gates [f64], and took toll of everything that crossed. Their miners opened the first workings on the Hask Ridge [r20], under what is now Haskhold [f72], and went deep enough to strike the blue-grey glass. The first of the line, Arvel Kennoch, had the Gannel Stones [f80] set in 2,556 BA to frame the star at its height, so that the kings could swear by a thing that did not move. In 1,668 BA it began to move. His line's last kings spent their rule arguing over whether the stones had shifted. The rule ended when the high grass failed three summers running and the toll-payers found the plains horse-herders would guide them round. The Kennoch Gates [f64] were shut from inside by the last toll-warden and have not been opened. Behind them is the deepest cutting of that rule: a stair down to the glass, and in the glass, whole, a single dressed stone with a name cut on it in no script the Kennoch kings knew.

### The Assize of Maun (1,350 BA [r-0162] to 361 BA)
A league of river houses at Aldermoot [f70] that agreed on one set of weights and one way of keeping accounts. The accounts were the Mollen's: a slow, long-lived people, not human, who had walked in from the west some generations before as the last of a line out of the country in the plane's p02 entry, long before that country fell. Each of them lives about two hundred and fifty years, and they forget no bargain. The league was called the Assize for its standard. It built the Alder Road [f24] and the Old Rudd Way [f23], the sea-door now called the Maun Stair [f27], a second sea-door where Corrith [f74] stands, and factories as far as the Iss Levels [r07] and the Wellen Hills [r25]. Its surveyors cut a second notch on the Gannel Stones [f80] in its first years and noted in a roll that the star stood "a finger under the old kings' mark".

In 600 BA the country in the p02 entry fell, and what it threw came down here: Maunath in the delta country, and Hernaud on the north coast. Whatever else that fall threw came down beyond the sheet. Maunath was a pattern of roads kept open and dues paid. It took the Assize as its people and the Assize took it as its patron, and for a hundred and eighty years the power was at its height. The gulf, the stair and the flats carry what is left of its name.

Under Maunath the Assize kept a yard of workers on the Pellow Downs [r15], more than sixty at its largest, the only school of mortal magic this land has had. Their great workings:
- The held channels. They fixed the three gulf rivers in set beds through the Reedmouths [r14] so that laden barges could reach the Maun Stair [f27]. The Bank Way [f21] levee is that work.
- The spring under the stone. In about 520 BA a worker named Merrit Loam stopped the head of a southern river under a great flat stone, the Stopped Spring [f48], to send its water down the River Wellen [f08] to the Assize's ford. The river she starved is the Dry Braid [f49]. It did not dry so much as stop being a river. She could not say her own name afterward; it survives in a Mollen wage book.
- The yards themselves. The ground where the school worked is the stretch of the Pellow Downs [r15] the delta people call the old yards. Nothing particular grows there, and its clay has no temper.
The workers who were used up, the hollow, were housed in a long shed at the yards and fed by rule. There were several hundred by the end. They are buried in a walled field at the yards with no marker on any grave, and the coping of the wall is set to lean inward, so that no one now can say whether it was built to keep something out or in.

Maunath grew past what the ground would carry. Its stewards saw its shadow pale and the steps of its high house at Aldermoot [f70] go soft at the edges, and took both for signs of favor. On a dim night in 416 BA [r-0163] it walked down the Maun Stair [f27] to the water, lighter at each step, and was not on the ground anymore. A boat crew of the water folk saw it go up. The stewards gave out that the patron had gone into the sea, and the delta's telling that a larger one drowned there comes from that.

Hernaud had watched. It was a pattern of a watch kept on a shore, and it had gathered the fishers of the Garrow Shore [r01] into one harbor people under a line of lamp-wardens. After Maunath rose it would not be held in mind any longer. It made the lamp-warden Brennis one promise, that a boat showing the harbor lamp would be kept count of, told her to keep the lamp and let its name go, and in about 410 BA walked into the Hern Sea [r30] and north along the bottom, out past the living country. It has not come back. It is not on the ground at the arrival. The promise, the bronze bell under the lowest quay step and the jar of notched sticks are what is left.

The Assize outlasted its patron by fifty-five years [r-0164]. The grain of its oldest fields, the Kings' Acres round Aldermoot [f70], had begun to weigh true and feed poorly. The held channels needed the school and the school needed more workers each year. Debts were written against debts. In 361 BA the last stewards shut the sea-doors, dismissed the yard, and sealed the Pellow Vaults [f26].

### After the Assize (361 BA to the arrival)
- 388 BA. Before the end, the harbor town of Low Garrow, at the shore end of the Drowned Causeway [f33], went under a winter surge. Its lamp-wardens' line and about half its people had already moved to the unnamed harbor town [f30]. A boat master named Ivo Strand led the rest east along the coast and up the River Iss [f11] to the Assize's emptied northern factory, and they are the oldest families of the unnamed walled town [f35]. Its lowest cellars are that factory's.
- 361 BA. The steward Ysmay Tallow sealed the Pellow Vaults [f26] with her own ring. Inside are the Assize's master standards in stone, its last ledgers on fired clay, and jars of seed. The ledgers show what the stewards did not want read: at the end the river houses owed the delta's salt rakers, the Mollen and the miners far more than anyone owed them. Captain Sabeth Corr shut the southern sea-door, kept the fleet, and founded the harbor league; Corrith [f74] has her name, worn down. The bonded miners of the Hask Ridge [r20] walked out under a foreman named Jory Hask and held the workings as their own.
- 358 BA. Corvin Alder, a steward's son, carried the river rolls to Aldermoot [f70] and was made king. The Aldermark's [r10] claim to be the Assize's heir is true of its rolls and false of its debts. One line of its founding roll has been scraped.
- 317 BA. The carriers first came (below).
- About 300 BA. The Sarrow, pass herders who had guided for the Assize's pass-wardens, took the fords and passes for themselves. Marda Sill was the first to hold Marle Ford [f61] for toll.
- About 243 BA. Nessa Ferry, first ferry elder of the unnamed spring town [f45], set the low seat and the cup by the water gate, after the carriers' round of 245 BA had sung there.
- About 220 BA. Edda Marsh, a sitter of the Morrow Fen [r13], began the custom of boiling water for the sick. She had it from a carrier's song about a tending ground and did not say so.
- 165 to 160 BA. With no school to hold them, the last of the held banks in the Reedmouths [r14] failed. Five flood years drowned the channel hamlets and the old salt works. Hale the Raker led the salt people onto the levee, and the four groups became one town there in 160 BA. The flood drum on the bank is said to have been his.
- About 75 BA. Odo Penn, the Aldermark's [r10] last reeve on the levee, went home with the market's due for his last year unpaid. The reckoning of it, a split stick in the old measure, stayed in the keeping of the town's Weightward.
- 36 BA [r-0167]. Calla Wend walked out of the Norwold [r11] and down to Aldermoot [f70] (below).
- 29 BA. The carriers' last round before the arrival.
- 12 BA [r-0168]. Josse Tarn was wrecked on Sutter Strand [r28] (below).
- The spring before the arrival. The last Weightward of the unnamed town [f20] died (below). The player left this mortal unnamed; no name is set here.
- The night of the arrival. The country in the p01 entry fell, 910 years of walking to the north-west, and what it threw came down here in the hour before first light. A warm blue-grey stone, the unnamed fallen stone [f77], came down with them in the Bren Hills [r05]. Shepherds on the hill saw it fall.

## The Whitmoor Ring

The Ring [f76] is one living thing in the shape of about a hundred and fifty standing stones, each twice a man's height, in a circle about three miles across at the middle of Whitmoor [r09]. The stones are pale, carry no tool mark, and are warm to the hand in frost. It eats what the star rains thickest, which is why the heath round it is bare where it should be the richest ground in the land. It is fed from above on dim nights, by the thread. It wants the fall and will be under it. It is patient past any god.

About three hundred herders keep sheep on the rim of the heath. They are descended from Kennoch toll-payers who settled there for the grazing. Over about five hundred years of living in sight of the stones they have lost the use of names for one another. They point. They have words for everything else. Plainsfolk call them the pointers and trade with them at the heath's edge and do not stay the night.

## The carriers

The deep lore calls them the Longwalkers. No people here uses that word, and god-beasts's own people carry a name one word off it by their god's choice; the Keeper writes "the carriers". The delta calls them the list-singers, the Aldermark [r10] the reciters, the Sarrow the counted road.

A line of them, about forty walkers at a time, has kept a round through this country since 317 BA, returning every 36 years [r-0170]: nine rounds before the arrival [r-0171], the last in 29 BA. The next begins in Year 7 [r-0169]. They walked here from the north-west, and on the way their forebears crossed the country in the p01 entry more than nine centuries before it fell. They carry its old names, and they sing them with all the others at every stopping place.

That is why so many peoples here keep a place for someone. The carriers do not preach. They sing lists, and between lists the old ones tell what a name was the name of. Peoples took up the figure that fit their own lives: the fen a tender of the dead who would come and sit; the bend town a guest who comes at night with a lamp; the pass people a tall stooping guest at the door with a key; the spring town whoever the river sends. None of those peoples knows the tale came from the singers, and the singers do not know that what they described has now come down.

Which names of the far country stand in their lists, by the rule and the rolls in the keeper log (3b.4): Hesmarrow, the Ovane, Tolcarrath, Marrask, Varrow Chase, the Lamp Gate and its name Ashevar, and the Low Field [r-0176] are sung. Dhen [r-0172], Skerr [r-0173], the Calder Hands [r-0174] and the House of the Even Pan [r-0175] are not. Vael is too young a word to have been taken up. Ilvane was chosen here and is in no list.

They planted one name here with their own hands. In 245 BA they named the old sea-door of Port Sennet [f75] for a gate they carried, and the town still says the name, Ashevar, when a boat comes in under it, without knowing it is anything but the door's name. This is a likeness to the common past, placed on purpose and logged.

## Travelers from afar

- Calla Wend, 36 BA. The last of a line that left the country in the p02 entry before it fell, more than six hundred years ago, and walked east ever since, each generation born on the road. How she crossed: on foot through the bare country and then the western forest, alone for the last eleven years after her mother died. She came down to Aldermoot [f70] thin and unsure of her own village's name. She carried the iron parts of a hoisting winch with a toothed wheel and a catch that holds the load, and could not say how the teeth were cut. The chancery's river crane at Aldermoot [f70] has it still, and the catch is called Calla's dog. She died in 9 BA and is buried at Wenlow [f71].
- Josse Tarn, 12 BA. The last of a line out of the fresh country under the young star in the p03 entry, walkers who took to boats where they found coast. How he crossed: on foot for most of his life, and then in a small boat of his own building along the southern shore from beyond the sheet's west edge, until it broke on Sutter Strand [r28]. He was pulled out by a Port Sennet [f75] crew and lives there, about fifty now. He hung his boat's steering board on the sternpost by two iron pins, a thing no league shipwright had seen, and three of the newest coasters carry it. He does not remember how the rest of his people's rig went.

## The twelve fading places

Where the land has spent more than the star gives back: the oldest, the most storied and the most worked. Each loses one kind a decade. The year of each first loss in play is rolled (keeper log, 3b.6) and stands on the schedule.

1. Aldermoot [f70] and the Kings' Acres. The old fields' wheat weighs true and feeds thin. The river houses' old speech has flattened to the market's.
2. The old yards on the Pellow Downs [r15]. Clay with no temper; grass with no seed heads.
3. The shore at the Maun Stair [f27]. One grey mussel where there were several kinds of shell.
4. The Morrow Mounds [f56]. The fever moss that grew only on the oldest mounds is failing.
5. The Ellow Barrows [f52]. The old songs have lost verses no one can sing back.
6. The Kennoch Gates [f64]. The blue pass flower that grew only on the cutting is nearly gone.
7. The Drowned Causeway [f33]. The cut stones are wearing to the look of any beach stone.
8. The unnamed walled town [f35]. The marks on its oldest tallies are harder each year to tell apart.
9. The Dry Braid [f49]. A river that stopped being one; its striped reed is going plain.
10. Ribhill [f42]. The bare patch, slowly widening.
11. Whitmoor [r09]. The rim sheep have lost their markings; few birds.
12. The old workings under Haskhold [f72]. The black-band ore that made the best iron smelts more like any other each year.

## The last Weightward

The office is older than the town. It was the Assize's ward of the standard for the delta market, and it kept two things: seven old weights of stone and bronze, and a sealed jar of kept halves of split sticks, among them Odo Penn's last reckoning of the market's due to the Aldermark [r10], struck in the old measure.

Last summer a chancery clerk of the Aldermark [r10], Peverel Stane, came to the unnamed town [f20]. He counted stalls. He also called on the Weightward and asked to see the old weights, saying the king's rolls gave the market's due in them. The Weightward showed him six and said the seventh, the largest, was out for cleaning. The Tally-keeper witnessed the visit and holds a kept half that says so.

The old man understood what the clerk was for. A due written in the old measure can only be struck with the old standard, and the largest weight is the one the due is counted in. During the winter he made up his mind. On a night of the spring flood he borrowed a punt, took the seventh weight out on the main channel of the River Alder [f03] below the hall, and put it over the side. He was past seventy. He was in the water to his waist getting the punt back onto the bank. He took a fever in the chest and died nine days later, in his bed, of that and of his age. No one killed him and no one meant him harm.

What the vacancy holds: a rack with seven seats and six weights; a jar that has not been opened; a coat and boots stiff with river silt on a peg in his loft; a punt returned with silt in it; and a market that has had nothing to check its fired clay standards against since, so that each new one is made from the last and they have drifted. Who gains by the empty place: the Assay-master, who has the weights by default; the kiln-masters, who have a market with no arbiter of measure; and the chancery, which has a town that cannot answer it in the old measure. None of them did anything to bring it about. This is the answer to ruling L-0029. It was set from the Assize's standards and the Aldermark's [r10] unpressed claim, both in the record since Night 3a, and from nothing the player sealed.

## What is not explained here

Why the land keeps accounts, what the star is to itself, and whether Maunath is one of the voices that come down are not set by this file. The first two the deep lore keeps. The third: Maunath is in the star, among those who have risen, and is too young there to be more than one voice among the few that still want anything.
