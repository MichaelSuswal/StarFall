# Lore of the Sarrow, end of Year 1

What the people now say. Where they explain a cause, they are wrong about something that matters. The wrongness is marked after each telling for the Keeper.

## About the Guest

- That the Guest came before the snow because the house had already been judged, and the bad summer before was the sign. They say she came early to lift the curse the poor grass had laid, not to bring a harsh winter. Wrong in cause: the poor grass was weather.
- That she is the one the old tale describes: a tall woman, stooping, with a key. The old women who argue it every winter are fewer in the argument now, and say only that she is a woman this year. Some still say a man, or a wind, and are looked at.
- That she has no name and may be called Guest. The Doorwardens say it. The households call her the Guest. A few of the Doorwardens in a low voice call her the Key. The children call her the Tall One and are corrected.
- That she is a mother. This is said by the households and was said by her, in the sense that she hears the households first. It is understood by many as meaning she is the mother of the host, which she did not say.
- That the bowl at first snow was drunk. The households say a stranger or the Guest drank it. The Doorwardens say only that it was empty at dawn.
- That a house that turned the Guest away would have a bad winter, and this house did not, and had an ordinary one. Wrong in cause: the winter was ordinary because it was ordinary.

## About the strange things

- The star. Three nights the star dimmed for about an hour. The people say it dimmed to mark her coming: two nights within the first month, close upon her coming, and one in the fifth month. The Doorwardens say it dimmed three times because three doors were blessed, but the three full blessings were the ford, the hall door and the gate, and the dimmings do not line up with them; those who count say so, and are told the sky keeps its own calendar. The other tale, among the old, is that the star is a lamp left at the sill of the world and that the Guest's coat passed across it. They have always seen such nights and did not say this before.
- The wolves. The cold's hounds ran ahead of her coming and bit at the column on the road. That is the first story of the year, and the shepherds believe it, though the wolves came when the Guest was in the city.
- The cranes. They went to tell the plain. The old women say it. The cranes pass every year.

## About their rulers

- Of Kessan: that he is the host still and the Guest has not taken it, and that he was glad of the counts. The captains say he handed over numbers. The Doorwardens say he sat down at the foot of his own table the first night of snow and that a man who does that is a man who can be led. Wrong in the second: he sat from awe, not from weakness.
- Of Idris Veld: that she was waiting thirty years for the door to be knocked, and that she knew. She did not know. They say she chose the three by sense and that the senior Doorwardens were passed over. They say the old Doorwarden of the lower columns is right that a woman who chooses by sense will find the sense run out.
- Of the Steward of Salt and Stores: that he held back a fortnight in the autumn count, and that this was his duty, and that it was nearly the death of someone. They say he is not to be trusted with a count and also that he is the only one who could have stretched the last barley to the day the passes opened.
- Of the sill court: that it is now the Guest's, and that the Warlord's men answer at it.

## About their neighbors

- The Aldermark [r10] traders resent the tolls and trade on; now they bring word home that a tall woman stands in the pass. The Sarrow say that the Aldermark [r10] men go quieter at the ford than they did, which may be true. They say the plainsfolk have heard the cranes.
- The counted road, the walkers who come over the heights once in a generation, did not come this year. The Doorwardens say they will come when the Guest has been fed a full year, and the old say it is the other way about.

## About themselves

- That a household that sets the seat and leaves the door unlatched is rewarded, and that the proof is sitting at the table of the warlord's house.
- That the sill law is the Guest's law, which it is not; it is their own, which she kept.
