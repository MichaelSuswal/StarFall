# Lore of the Sarrow, end of Year 4

What the people now say. Where they explain a cause, they are wrong about something that matters. The wrongness is marked after each telling for the Keeper. Year 1 tellings that still stand are kept and marked.

## About the Guest

- That she does not know why she came before the snow, and said so to the gate guard. The households say it is how a mother speaks. The Doorwardens say it is how a Guest speaks. Some captains say a god who does not know can be argued with. Wrong in all three: whatever she did or did not know, it was not said to be argued with.
- That she is a woman who eats at a table and cannot lie until she rises, and the households have tried it. A game among the low quarter's children is to ask her, in play, what she had for her dinner. Wrong in the part that matters: they take the bar on lying as a weakness to be used, and not as a thing she told them so that they might trust what she says.
- That she said she does not know whose the star is. The households are divided between taking it as true, which frightens them more than any story, and taking it as a silence she has been told to keep. Wrong in the second: nothing in her words says she was told.
- That the Guest has given the right hand of the table to the Lintel and the right hand of the sill to the First Doorwarden, and that this is a choosing of the host's heir. Wrong: it was a seat, and Idris said so.
- That she is a mother, and the mother of the host. Said still by many. She did not say it.
- That the bowl at first snow was drunk by the Guest. The old women this year argue that a Guest who has come once cannot come again, and others that she must be bidden each winter. They have not settled it, and the argument is longer than any since the first snow of the world.
- That she heard the lower columns' senior Doorwarden at the hall door and gave him no oath, and that this is why the lower columns are cold to the high rite. Wrong in the cause: they were cold to it before.

## About the strange things

- The star. Five nights this year, more than last. The households say it dims when a door is blessed, that it is a lamp at the sill of the world and the Guest's coat passes across it, that it dims when someone dies who should not have, that it is an eye that blinks and sleeps in the cold, that something passes before it. The Doorwardens say five dimmings and five blessed thresholds, the ford, the hall door, the city gate, Stair Pass and Far Notch, and say at last the count agrees. Those who count say the dimmings and the blessings do not fall on the same nights and that last year there were three dimmings and four blessings, and are told the sky keeps its own calendar. Wrong in the count: the dimmings were eight over two years and the blessings five, and the Doorwardens have set this year's five against the sum of both.
- The flower. The blue pass flower did not come up on the old cutting at the high gates. The oldest woman of a lower column says it marks the summer's height and that no flower means no height, and that the cold is walking down. A keeper of rite says the Guest's blessing at the passes passed on the flower. Another says it is the star. The old herders say it has been going for years and nobody listened. The Kennoch Gates [f64] are said by some to be cooling, and no one has gone near them. Wrong in the cause given by the Doorwardens and the households: the flower was failing before the Guest, and before the star dimmed oftener. Whether the old herders are right is not theirs to know.
- The wolves. Last year's tale is kept and has grown: that the cold's hounds ran before her and bit at the column, and this year the dead herder is remembered at the hall door and the old women say the star dimmed for him. Wrong: the star dimmed on the night after the hall had sat, and they have put the cause the wrong way round.
- The cranes. They went to tell the plain, and some now say to count the doors. Wrong, and the plainsfolk have no word of it.

## About their rulers

- Of Kessan: that he is at the Guest's right hand and the host is still his. That he said the fold was his and the failure his, flat, to the table, and the captains have not forgiven him for saying it where the Guest could hear. The Doorwardens say a man who says it so is a man who can be led. Wrong: it was said so that the captain would not be the one carrying it.
- Of Idris Veld: that she has the sill and not the table, and does not mind. Wrong in the second part, as she knows.
- Of the Steward of Salt and Stores: that his count is now a thing the hearth mothers set beside their own, and that he is, for the first time, in the same room as the Mother of the Hearth on a count. The old tale of his holding back a fortnight has hardened into a saying: a fortnight is the Steward's measure. Wrong in the saying, which makes of one year's count a habit, though the second count did differ.
- Of the Marshal of the Road: that he went quiet on the road back from the far houses and did not speak of the roofs. The riders have told it in the low quarter, and the story of the new roofs and good horses is told as a thing the Marshal's house has, with no word yet said of what it means. Wrong in the telling that makes it already a charge.
- Of the sill court: still the Guest's, say the Doorwardens, and the Warlord's men answer at it. Said less than a year ago, since the Guest has spent the summer with the lower columns.
- Of the senior Doorwarden of the lower columns: that he asked for the oath and was answered, and the Guest was right, and the Guest was wrong, according to who says it.

## About their neighbors

- The traders of the Aldermark [r10] come as last year and pay as last year, and the watchers say they are quiet at the ford. A trader told the older watcher at the ford that the river had a poor year, and the watcher tells it as the reason the trains come short. Not known to be true.
- The two farthest toll houses are said to have new roofs, good horses and full smokehouses, and the Marshal's house to have gone quiet. The watchmen at those houses say they were mended by the carpenters of the trains, that the horses are toll in kind and the smokehouses are full for the night's feeding. Not known to be true.
- The counted road did not come, as last year. The Doorwardens said they would come when the Guest had been fed a full year, and a year and more has been, and they have not come, and the Doorwardens have stopped saying it. The old say it is the other way about, and will come when the house has been judged.
- The plainsfolk have heard the cranes, the old women say.

## About themselves

- That a household that sets the seat and leaves the door unlatched is rewarded: kept from last year, and the argument whether it must be done each year is the new thing.
- That the sill law is the Guest's law. It is their own, which she kept. Said less.
- That the sharing from first snow is the Guest's law of hunger, and that a people fed by hearth in the cold are fed by the Guest. The Mother of the Hearth says it is the households' own custom, which the hearth mothers have always kept in a bad year and the host never did. Wrong in the first: the Guest said she would rather a short true count than a kind one, and the households have given it a name.
- That the old women are quieter this winter. This is true and is not lore.

## Year 3 tellings (added; Year 2 tellings above that still stand are kept)

### About the Guest

- That she ate at the senior Doorwarden's table in the open and read him in front of Idris, and that this is why the lower columns are warmer. Wrong in the cause: they were warmer because he was heard twice, and nobody outside the hall knows what the reading was.
- That she gave no word on the toll, and that this is a judgment already made. The Doorwardens say she waited, the households say it was Kessan's to say, some captains say it was consent. The old women say a house that raises the toll in the year she is among it will be judged on what it does with the toll. Wrong in all three: none of them knows what she thought, and she said nothing.
- That the account for the dead herder's household is paid from what people bring her, and so every bowl at the rite fires is a bowl for him. True in part, and the households have begun to bring a little more bread, believing it a thing owed to the Guest's account and not to the Steward's.
- That she said she did not know whether the cold is coming down, and went up to look. The herders say it is the first time a god went up to look at the flower. Wrong in the thing that matters to them: they think a god who looks has a reason to look.
- That she asked at every fire what Marda is, and that a god who asks the same at every fire has lost a thing. The lower columns say Marda is a woman at the ford. The Doorwardens say the Guest is asking because she knows the answer and wishes to hear who will say it.

### About the strange things

- The star. Six nights this year, the most yet, the Doorwardens say fourteen in three years against five blessings, and are told again the sky keeps its own calendar. The households have a new telling: that the star dims in pairs because it blinks twice when it is surprised. A pair did fall in month 5 and a pair in month 7. The herders say it dims when the grass line moves. The lower columns say it dims for the toll. Wrong in every telling: none of the dimmings fell on the night the order was given, and the people have set the cause after the night.
- The flower. Bare again at the cutting. The herders now say the flower is not coming back, and that the good grass has been going up for forty summers. The Doorwardens say it will come back if the toll is fairly paid. The oldest man of the lower columns says it will not, and that the high gates were green once. Wrong in the Doorwardens' telling, which ties it to the toll. The herders are right that it is going, and do not know why.
- The cranes. Said still to count the doors, and by a few now to count the stores. Wrong, and the plainsfolk have no word of it.
- The letter. Said by the households to be a king's debt for a road the Sarrow never took from him, and by the captains to be a trader's trick. Said by the Doorwardens to be the first time the world beyond the passes has spoken to the sill in its own hand. Wrong in all three: no one knows what the claim means, and the claimant's words are the clerk's.

### About their rulers

- Of Kessan: that he raised the toll as a warlord does, in the hall, before the Guest, and she did not stop him, and that he said it was his. The captains say he is the Lintel and has always been. The households say he did what he said he would do in Year 1, and that he said so. Wrong: he did not ask her leave and did not say he was not asking it.
- Of Idris Veld: that she said the toll was the host's to raise and the sill's to hear, and the Mother of the Hearth says it is the first time anyone has said where the host ends. Wrong: she said the sill's part is to hear, not that it was the host's.
- Of the Steward: that he gave a hard number first this year and that the saying a fortnight is the Steward's measure is now said with a smile. Wrong in the saying, which still makes of one year's count a habit, though he gave a hard number this year.
- Of the Mother of the Hearth: that she laughed in the hall. Said in the quarter as a good sign. Wrong in the sign: she laughed at the Steward, and not for joy.
- Of the Marshal of the Road: that he was told to walk his own road and did. In the low quarter it is told as a shame and a trial. Said by his riders to be a courtesy, since the Guest said she did not accuse him. Wrong in the shame: no one has said what was found.
- Of the first captain of the band, who walked with the Marshal: that he is the Lintel's eye on the road, which no captain has been. Wrong: he went because Kessan said he would not like the Marshal's riders.
- Of the senior Doorwarden of the lower columns: that he sat at the Guest's table and was read and came out walking, and that he is quieter. Said as his triumph by his own fire and as his bending by the high rite. Wrong in both.
- Of the younger Doorwarden of the lower columns: that he asked the Guest to judge the grass and she did, and the hillside is settled. The two households say it is settled as it ought, one of them without liking it.

### About their neighbors

- The traders of the river kingdom paid the raised toll after the thaw with complaint, and the watchers say it was loud at the ford and quieter at the sill. A trader asked at the sill whether a toll could be raised by half in a year the ford was thinner, and was fed and heard and sent on.
- The river kingdom's chancery has written to the ford. The households say the king wants the road. The Doorwardens say the king is the heir of the old wardens of a league in the lowlands, whom the Sarrow once guided for, and that the claim may be older than the Sarrow's own toll. Wrong in the first; unknown in the second.
- The two farthest toll houses are said still to have new roofs, good horses and full smokehouses, and the Marshal's house to be quiet. The Marshal and the first captain walked them and returned. What they said at the hall is told in the quarter as it was said and no more.
- The counted road did not come, again. The old say they will come when the house has been judged, and that the house is being judged now.
- The plainsfolk have heard the cranes, the old women say.

### About themselves

- That the sharing by hearth from the first snow is now the households' own custom, and the Mother of the Hearth says it every month. Said by the Doorwardens to be the Guest's law of hunger. Wrong in the Doorwardens' telling.
- That a household that sets the seat and leaves the door unlatched is rewarded, and that this is why the winter held. Wrong in Branch B, where it did not, and the households then say the door must be set better.
- That a band that eats last two winters running has done the host a service. The captains say so in the hall, and say the opposite at the fire.
- That the line of the grass is going up. The herders say it without grief, and the households do not yet believe them.

## Year 4 tellings (added; earlier tellings that still stand are kept)

### About the Guest

- That she has a name now, Mother of the Door. The households say it is the old name and that she told it to the king of the river as one tells a name to a guest. The Doorwardens say it is the name of the sill itself, that she has said she is the door and not the one at it. Some captains say a god who names herself in a letter has begun to bargain. Wrong in all three: she said it in a word to a king, and said they call her Guest, and gave it no more weight than that.
- That she sat alone with the Lintel at the hall table the night before she spoke, with bread, and that he came out pledged. The captains say the Lintel was given his road back; the Doorwardens say he was warned. Wrong in both: nobody but the two of them knows what was said, and Kessan has said only what he said.
- That she said the toll is the sill's to keep and the host's to take, and that this is why the toll came down. Said in the quarter as the Guest's law of the toll. Wrong: the toll came down by what the cords showed, and by Kessan's own mouth with Idris's, and what she said at the table is hers.
- That she walked to the farthest door and laid salt on it and counted the road. The riders say it is a thing a god does when she does not trust a house; the households say she told the keeper first and so it was no accusation. Wrong in the riders' telling, which she had named in advance.
- That she stood at the rite fire through the names of all the winter's dead and did not sit, and that she wept. Said by the old women, and the households say it was a thing owed. Wrong in the weeping, which no one saw.
- That the stone she asked the herders to cut is the Guest's mark on the grass and that the line stands because of it. Said by the herders of the upper columns, who cut it, and by the households. The herders themselves say a stone cut to count a line is not a stone that holds it, and the households do not hear them.
- That she asked again at every fire of the upper columns what Marda is, and was told five things, and that a god who asks the same at every fire has lost a thing, as the lower columns said. The upper columns say the answer they gave is the true one, and each means a different thing by it.

### About the strange things

- The star. Seven nights this year, the most yet, twenty one in four years against six blessings, and the Doorwardens say the count does not agree and are told again that the sky keeps its own calendar. The households say it dimmed for the toll when it was settled, and the herders say it dimmed on the high grass because the line stood. The old women say it dims for the dead who were named at the first snow. Wrong in every telling: none fell on the night a thing was settled or a name said, and the people have set the cause after the night.
- The flower. Bare again at the cutting. The herders say it is not coming back and that the stone will count the line of the grass year on year whether anyone listens or not. The Doorwardens say it will return when the toll is fairly kept. Wrong in the Doorwardens' telling, which ties it to the toll.
- The cranes. Said still to count the doors, the stores, and now by some the dead. Wrong, and the plainsfolk have no word of it.
- The summer without the river's trains. The households say the king has closed the road for the letter's answer. The captains say it was the raise, which the traders complained of, and that the lowering came too late. The Doorwardens say the king's heir keeps his claim by keeping away, and that no claim has been made at the sill, which proves nothing yet. The older watcher says only that he has not known a summer without one. Wrong in some part that matters, and not known to them: the cords say only that the river's trains did not come, and the trains of other peoples did.

### About their rulers

- Of Kessan: that he was told first and told not alone but by one who would keep his road, and that he halved his own raise at the table with Idris and the Guest and the captains have not forgiven it. The households say he did what he said he would do in the autumn, and said so. Wrong: the cords said it and he read them.
- Of Idris Veld: that she said the sill takes one toll and not two, and took the middle of it, and that she carried a folded word to the ford with her own hand. The Doorwardens say she is the Guest's own door. Wrong in the last: she sent it by a boy and gave it no more weight than a word.
- Of the Steward of Salt and Stores: that he gave a number first again, and that he now seals the seed with a mark beside the Mother's and says aloud the order the stores would fail in. The saying that a fortnight is the Steward's measure is said now with respect and no smile. Wrong in that it was never the measure, but he learned to give the number.
- Of the Mother of the Hearth: that she holds half the seal and will not give it up, and that she said it was the first time she had heard the host's word held to a count. Said in the quarter as a good sign and a hard one.
- Of the Marshal of the Road: that he stood at the farthest door with his cap in his hand, and that he sent riders for the loan and did not ride himself. The riders say it was a Marshal's duty. In the low quarter it is told as a thing he was not trusted to do. Wrong in the telling that makes it already a charge, in Branch A; in Branch B the farthest house's words are told as a thing that was not answered.
- Of the youngest of the eight captains: that he said aloud that the Guest's thanks were the first the band had had for eating last. Said at the fires of the band. Wrong in the sense taken, that the band wants something, which he did not say.
- Of the older toll watcher at the ford: that he took the Guest's word to the king of the river kingdom and kept it dry in his father's chest until a train master would carry it. Told at the ford as a thing the sill did. Wrong in the part that makes him the Guest's messenger to a king, which he says he is not.

### About their neighbors

- The river kingdom's trains paid the raised toll in the spring with open complaint, loud at the ford and the far houses, and did not come at all in the summer. The watchers say they know the faces and the faces did not come. Wrong in nothing the watchers know; wrong in every cause the hall gives, since the hall does not know the cause.
- The word the Guest sent the king left the ford with a master of a river train who would take it for a waived toll, after another would not. Said at the ford and in the high rite, not in the quarter. Whether it was given is not known.
- The two farthest toll houses are said still to have new roofs, good horses and full smokehouses, and the Guest to have marked the farthest door with salt and the oath before the keeper's eyes. The keeper said that his cord had nothing in it the knots did not say. Told in the quarter as it was said.
- The counted road did not come, again, and the old say they will come when the house has been judged.
- The plainsfolk have heard the cranes, the old women say.

### About themselves

- That the seed is sealed under two marks and that this is the Guest's law of the seed. Said by the Doorwardens. The Mother of the Hearth says it is the Steward's and the hearth mothers' own, made after the winter that ate it. Wrong in the Doorwardens' telling.
- That the sharing from the first snow is now the households' own custom and the band's eating last is the band's own. The captains say so in the hall and say the opposite at the fire.
- That a band that eats last three winters running has done the host a service, or has been used. Said both ways.
- That the names of the winter's dead were said at the first snow for the first time, with the seat set, and that a house that names its dead is rewarded. Wrong in the reward; the winter that followed is the answer, and the people read it by the branch it took.
- That the line of the grass stood this year. Said by the herders without hope and by the households with some. Wrong in the households' reading, which takes one year as the turn.
