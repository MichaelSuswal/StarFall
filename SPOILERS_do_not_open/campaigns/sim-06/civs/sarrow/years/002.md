# The Sarrow, Year 2

Months are counted from the year's opening. Month 1 is the first month of autumn. The people reckon from first snow to first snow, so in their own telling this year began in the third month and has not yet ended. This file keeps the game's count.

Where a thing turns on a power, or on an outcome the Keeper rolls or prices, it is marked (K), and `years/002-report.md` says what is asked.

The year's world, as the Keeper gave it: an ordinary year of weather, the first snow at its usual time, the passes open at the thaw in the ordinary way, the river kingdom's traders as last year, the ford's tally down a little, the toll at the hall as thin as last year, five dimmings of the star, the blue flower missing from the cutting at the high gates, the cranes in the third month.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the ford, the gate, the stores

The host was coming down the road as every year, columns strung out over days, the herds fat on an ordinary summer's grass. Kessan had come down early with the sworn band to receive them, and Idris Veld with her keepers of rite, as in Year 1. This year the Doorwardens said it was not chance, and the captains said it was habit.

The Guest was on the road with the columns. She had walked down with them and was not yet at the ford when the year turned. She came to Marle Ford [f61] first, on foot, from up the road and not out of the dark, with the staff and the key, and stood in the crossing. The older toll watcher, the one who knew the oath, was at his post. She asked him for the count of who had crossed since she was last there, and he gave it from the knotted cord the two watchers keep: the trains of the Aldermark [r10] and their beasts, the packers, the herds that went over, by the season. It was down a little on last year, and he told it plain. He said afterward that he had braced all summer for a scolding over a thin count, since the Marshal's order had come to restate the custom and he had taken it for a doubt. She asked him how the trains had come this season and she did not scold. He said the trains came as many as last year, or near it, but short, with fewer beasts to the train, and that he did not know why. He put it down, as the watchers at the ford do, to a poor year on the river, which a trader had told him at the sill and which he had no way to check. What she made of the count set beside what the ford had told her by the blessing is hers, and the watcher saw nothing in her face. (K, what the blessing gave her of the crossings. Nothing turns on it among the Sarrow.) The toll house fed the two travelers who came to it that day a night's bread and a place by the fire, as it had been ordered, and she saw it done.

She walked the two miles to the city gate and came there before midday and stood where the senior of the sworn guard could see her, with her hands open. He had asked her a year ago why she came before the snow, and she had given him nothing, and the question had stood in him all year. She answered him now in few words: that she woke by their road in the dark before first light and went to the nearest door, and did not know why that was the hour. He took it as a man takes weather. Then she asked him who kept the gate at night and whether it had changed. He said the sworn band kept it in pairs with a Doorwarden's boy at each post to run for the hall, as before, and that it had changed in one way: since last winter nobody on the gate slept. He did not say it with pride. The answer that the Guest does not know why she came was in the whole city by dark. The households liked it. The Doorwardens said it was how a Guest speaks, a word that is not meant to be understood. Some of the captains said a god who does not know is a god a man may argue with, and were not rebuked for it.

Idris had the hall set that evening and the Guest was bidden in again. It was no longer an event. It was a guest come to a house she knew.

Within the month she told the Steward at the hall, to his face and with Idris Veld beside her, that she would rather have a short true count than a kind one, and that she did not call last year's a lie, since he said it was not. He took it badly and well together. He said he had never given a false count and would not begin, and that he would give her one he could stand behind, and that the hearth mothers should have their own count if she wanted it. She asked for exactly that: two counts of grain, salt, fuel and dried meat before first snow, his and one made by the hearth mothers of each quarter, to be brought to the Mother of the Hearth, who would set the two side by side and tell her where they differed. She asked him also what salt was spare. He gave her a measure smaller than last year's, since the trains had brought less salt, and said so before she asked, and she took it as it was given and said she would draw from it for the blessings and no more.

### Month 2: the hall, the wolves' reckoning, the star

In the second month, with the columns coming down, the Guest sat at the hall with Kessan, Idris Veld, the Marshal of the Road and the Mother of the Hearth, and raised the herder who had died of wolves at the night fold last year. She asked four things, as one who judges and not as one who accuses: whose watch it had been, why the dogs were late to give tongue, whether any column had gone without a grown man on the night watch, and who kept the dead man's household now.

Kessan answered the first himself, before any other could. It was the watch of the captain of that column, which is to say it was the Warlord's watch, since he sets the captains over the columns and they answer to him; he said the fold was his and the failure was his, and he said it flat, looking at the table. Then he had the captain brought in, who stood and said what the record of the night shows and a little more. The dogs were late because the column had made hard marches coming down and the dogs had been fed thin, with the stores short, and ran all day at the sheep's heels and slept like the dead by night. The Mother of the Hearth said, without being asked, that the dogs were fed from the same stores as the people, and that nobody in a thin year had asked what the dogs got. Kessan sent to his other seven captains and had their answers by the next day. Four of the eight columns had set boys on the night watch with only one grown man among them for some nights of the march. None had set boys alone. The wolves had come to the one column where the grown man was one of those at the fires and not at the fold. The Marshal said the riders had seen it on the road and had not thought to bring it up, since no one asks a rider what the folds look like.

The dead man's household was a widow, two children and his mother. The Mother of the Hearth named a hearth in the low quarter that kept it, a household whose own man had died of the cough two winters back; the two were joined. Idris Veld fixed the price of his death by the old sill table, in goats and a cow, to be paid from the herd of the captain's column and not by the household, and said it aloud in the hall. The captain took it without a word, though the column felt it. The Steward was ordered to give the household its winter keep from the stores, on the Guest's account. He did not know what that account was, and said so to Idris afterward. He entered it on a separate cord marked as hers, and has not yet been told how it is to be paid.

The orders went out in the old ways. To Kessan, that no column goes down the road at night fold with only boys on the watch, that each fold has a grown watcher and dogs fed and rested, that his captains answer to him for it. To the Marshal, that his riders carry the order to every column by the first relay. The captains took it as what it was, a rebuke of the way watches had been set for years, and complied, and ate into the sworn band's own sleep to do it, since the grown men for the night watches came out of the spears. They said so to each other and not to the Guest.

In the second month the star dimmed, one night, for about an hour in the middle of the night. It was a clear night. The hall had sat the day before. The old women said the star dimmed for the dead herder. They said it as if they had always known it. Nobody argued.

### Month 3: the two counts, the sharing, the snow

By the end of the month the counts were in, and the Steward's came to the Mother of the Hearth the same hour as the hearth mothers' of each quarter. The Steward's said the stock would reach the thaw with about a week to spare at the full ration. The hearth mothers' said it would fall short of the thaw by about eleven days. The two differed by about eighteen days.

The Steward explained a good part of it, and was believed in that part. He had counted the seed barley as stock, since if the winter was hard he would eat it, and the mothers had not, since it is for sowing. He had counted the dried meat of the low granary by tally, and the mothers by the bin, and the low granary had taken damp in the autumn rains, and the meat there had turned. He had counted the hay of the war band's horses as the households' fuel. For the last part, some days' worth, he could not tell where it went. He said the sacks tallied. The mothers said the bins did not. Neither could say more, and the Mother of the Hearth said so, flat, to the Guest.

Under the Guest's word of the first month, the stores were to be shared by hearth from the first snow and not from the fourth month, the poorest first, and the captains were to hear the reason before they heard it from anyone else. She told them herself, at the hall, the reason as she had given it to the Steward: that she would rather a short true count than a kind one. The war band's houses were last in the sharing, as before, and this year they were last from the first snow. The captains took it as they had taken it in Year 1, badly, though two of the eight said that a winter's short rations from the beginning was better than a winter's blow at the end and that last year had taught that. The captain who had lost the herder said nothing at all. Kessan told his captains in his own words that the band could eat last for one winter and hold the passes on it, and no captain argued where the Guest could hear. The Steward carried the sharing out. He had said in his own office that it would be worse than keeping the stock whole and eating from it, and he said it again, and carried it out. He is the only man in the city who now speaks of the Guest's counts as a thing he is used to.

The cranes went over for some days toward the plain, as they do each year. The old women said again that they had gone to tell the plain. A few added that they were counting the doors.

The first snow came late in the month, at its usual time. That night the people did what they do each year. The warlord's door was left unlatched, the seat set, a bowl filled and left on the sill. The decisions give the Guest no step for that night, and I write nothing of whether she came to the seat. If she came, the people saw it, and the god's own file will say. What the people did, they did as the custom is, and Kessan sat at the head of his table with the seat at his right set and empty. The old women argued at the fire whether a Guest who has come once can come again, or must be bidden each year, and the argument was longer than any since the first snow of the world.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the low quarter's tables, Kessan at the right hand

The Guest stayed in the winter city through the cold and did not step. To the Mother of the Hearth she answered the thing she had left unanswered a year ago: yes, she would sit at the households' tables in the low quarter, one hearth a week, and the Mother would choose them and neither Idris Veld nor the Steward. She told the Mother the terms first. She would eat there, and at a table where she had eaten she could not speak a lie until she rose, so what she said there would be true or silence. She would not use the Reading at these tables. She would ask the households what they said of the dimmed star, and she would tell them she did not know whose it was.

The Mother chose the first table herself: the household that had taken the widow and the dead man's mother. After it she chose tables a quarter at a time, the poorest first, and one table in each quarter was a captain's household, since she said the Guest should see how the war band lived. About a dozen tables in all through the cold. The terms were known in every hearth within the week. The households were not made afraid by them. They used them. At the third table a woman asked the Guest whether she was hungry, and a man at the fourth asked whether the bowl at first snow had been hers, and each got a true answer or none. What the answers were is hers.

What the households say of the star, as they said it at those tables: that it dims when a door is blessed (this the Doorwardens first said, and they have said it since the three dimmings last year); that it is a lamp at the sill of the world and the Guest's coat passes across it; that it dims when someone dies who should not have; that it blinks, as an eye does, and sleeps in the cold; that something is passing in front of it and will pass again. The Guest told each table that she did not know whose it was. They did not take it as modesty. Some took it as true, and were more afraid for it than they had been of any story, since if even the Guest does not know, no one does. Some took it as a keeping of silence. The old women said that a guest who will not say is a guest who has been told not to.

Kessan sat at her right hand at the hall table from the fourth month. She told him, before the hall, that she would not read him again unless he asked it or crossed the sill with a hand that frightened her, and that this held until she said otherwise. She asked him for the count of the war band, the state of the passes and the toll in coin and kind this year against last, and why it was thin. He gave the band as about three hundred and sixty sworn, under eight captains, unchanged, with two lost to the cough in the cold and two boys sworn at the sill. The passes, he said, were shut by snow as every winter and would open at the thaw. For the toll, he gave the cords: the same as last year, thin, near half of what the old cords show for a generation back. He thought the trains came shorter, and told her why in the plain way he tells anything. He did not know. He said the far toll houses he had never counted, since they are the Marshal's trade and not his, and that he had not asked. He did not accuse the Marshal. He said it as a man says where his knowledge stops.

At the hall, the people saw the Warlord at the Guest's right hand, where Idris sat at the sill court last winter, and Idris at her left at the table. The Doorwardens took it as a slight. The captains took it as the Guest's plain favor to the Lintel. The Guest had said nothing to either. Idris, asked afterward by a Doorwarden of her own if she minded, said that the right hand of the table is the host's and the right hand of the sill is the rite's, and that she had never in thirty years supposed otherwise.

### Month 5: the senior Doorwarden, Marda Sill

Word went by Idris Veld that the senior Doorwarden of the lower columns, the one passed over last year, was to come to the hall door and be heard in person, with Idris at the Guest's side so that he was heard and she was not made to carry what he said. He came, an old man, hard, the oldest keeper of the old forms in the lower columns, and he stood and did not sit. The Guest asked him what he had meant by his words at the city rite fire, that a woman who chooses by sense instead of age will find the sense runs out.

He said what he meant. That a rite held by four is a rite held by four, and that the oath taught to Idris and three chosen by sense is not taught to the columns, and that in the lower columns, where the oldest rite fires are, there is no one who has it from the Guest's mouth. He said that rank is not pride. Rank is how a rite is spread across a people so that it does not die with one woman's sense. He said Idris was the best keeper of the sill in the high rite and had chosen well for a woman choosing by sense, and that sense is a thing that ends when the one who has it ends. He asked, in so many words, that the oath be taught to him or to one of his. The decisions say she chooses no one new this year, and the answer was the one she had decided, in whatever words she gave it. I do not write them. He took it standing and said that the lower columns would have the Guest at their fires in the summer and he would see her there.

Asked after what she thought of him, Idris said that he was right about half of it and proud about all of it, and that he kept the old forms better than any in the high rite, herself included. The Guest heard both and chose no one. The senior Doorwarden is not an enemy of Idris now so much as a man who has been heard and not given what he asked, which is a worse thing to be in a proud house.

In the deep snow the Guest sat once at Idris Veld's own table and asked what Marda Sill is, a place, a person, a rite or a word, and whose. She did not ask whether it was hers and did not tell Idris why she asked. She ate at her table, and Idris had been told the terms, and Idris did not ask her whether it was hers. Idris answered what she knew. That Marda Sill is a person in the recital, the first to take the toll at Marle Ford [f61] for the Sarrow themselves, and that before her the Sarrow guided for the wardens of a league in the lowlands. That the name is also a word in the oath, said once at its close in the old form: as Marda held it. That the Doorwardens of the high rite say it is the name of a woman, and that the keepers of the lower columns say it is not, that Marda is older than the Sarrow's own speech, and that Sill may be what a person is called who stands. That the recital says she took the first toll and does not say she died. That the lower columns tell that she went into the ford and has not come out, and Idris did not say she believed it. That the Doorwardens do not say the name over a sill, only the oath. The Guest took what she was told as what she was told, and nobody was sent to find out. Idris did not ask why. She told it to nobody, though she was seen to be thoughtful for a week.

### Month 6: the third table, the star

The thin months went as the counts had said, but not as they had gone the winter before. The stores shared from the snow lasted. The old and the youngest ate least, and fewer of them died than in Year 1, though the number is still not small, and the hearth mothers say it is because the sharing began in time and the Steward says it is because the winter was no worse than the last. The war band's houses ate last and lean, from the beginning, and grumbled, and held. A cull of weak stock was made in the sixth month, smaller than the year before. There was no hunt for meat, and Kessan was seen to be at ease with it.

In the sixth month the star dimmed again, in the middle of the night, for about an hour. It fell in the same week the Guest had eaten at a household's table in the low quarter. The household said so, and said it was the dimming of something said at the table, though they could not tell what.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Months 7 and 8: the thaw, Far Notch, the far toll houses

The thaw came at the usual time and the passes opened. The last barley went three days past the opening, not the week before it, which the Steward counts as his best work in four years and does not credit to the sharing.

When the thaw opened the passes, the Guest went up the road to the northeast with the Marshal of the Road and a few riders, on foot, to Far Notch [f63], and stood in the pass where no one keeps it, laid a handful of salt on the stones, and spoke the guest oath over it with the key in her hand. (K, what the Latch did and what it let her know; no person among the Sarrow can say.) From the ford to that pass is one mile and nine tenths on foot, and from the winter city two miles and four tenths, as the Keeper gave it. The riders were uneasy and quiet, as at Stair Pass. After it the five blessings stood: the ford, the hall door, the city gate, Stair Pass and Far Notch.

She asked the Marshal then which toll houses were thin and which kept honest counts, and they walked the road to the northeast house by house. The Marshal named the near houses as the thin ones: the ford, and the two nearest it, which have always been poor, he said, since they take the small trains and the packers. The far houses he named as the honest ones, and the rich: the two farthest on the road keep their own cords and their counts have always agreed with the riders' tally. He said it with some pride, as a man names the part of his work he has never had to doubt. (K, the trip. The party set out for the two farthest toll houses on the road to the northeast, on foot, the Guest, the Marshal and the riders. The Keeper prices it. I have written it as done between the thaw and the columns going up in month 9, with no days given.)

What they saw at the two farthest toll houses, as the Marshal and the Guest and the riders saw it: new roofs on both, of split timber, close laid, with the thatch still gold. Good horses in the pens, more than the houses need, sleek, the kind a trader brings. And full smokehouses, the racks hung thick. The Marshal had not walked those two houses himself in some years, and said so, and then said that a Marshal who must ride the whole road and the passes has riders to do it, and he stopped saying it. He looked at the roofs for a long time and said nothing at all.

What the watchmen said. At each house the watchmen took the Guest in at their table and fed her, and at each told it as one story told twice. The roofs were mended by the carpenters of the trains, who take lodging in the houses and pay in labor, and the houses had asked for nothing. The horses were toll in kind: the Aldermark [r10] traders cross with horses more than grain, and the far houses take what is offered and keep it for the riders and the hall, which the Marshal's riders collect at the thaw and the fall. The smokehouses were full because every traveler gets a night's feeding and the order was said, so they keep the racks full, and the Guest's order had made them more careful than before. Their cords, they said, agreed with the riders' tally, and they showed them, and the knots agreed with what the riders had laid up at the hall. They said that the hall's toll was thin because the trains were thin, and they said it with no hurry. They were courteous. One said he would be glad if the Guest and the Marshal would eat again at his table before the columns went up, and meant it.

I write what they said and not whether it was true. That is for the Keeper (K), and what the Guest and the Marshal made of it is hers and his. The Marshal's riders say he went quiet on the road back and did not speak of the roofs.

The Marshal was asked, as he had been last year, to bring her any stranger odd in tools, speech or body. The toll watchers brought seven this year, fewer than last, since the order was said twice and the newness was gone. All were ordinary by my reckoning: a carter with six fingers on one hand, an Aldermark [r10] clerk who spoke the river speech oddly and could not eat the barley, a woman with a milk-white eye, two plainsfolk boys who were running from a master, a man who sang at the sill, a smith with a limp, a woman with a goat on a rope. Each was fed a night and sent on. I have placed none of them as what the god is looking for.

### Month 9: the columns go up

The columns went up with the herds to the high grass. Kessan led the going up as he has for twelve years, and was glad to, and the captains were glad. The Guest did not go with him. In this month the star dimmed, in the middle of the night, for about an hour, while the columns were on the road up. The herders said it was the star saying a thing at the time of the going up, and saw it as they walked.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Month 10: the flower

In the tenth month, the first of summer, the herders of the lower columns went up to the old cutting at the high gates, and the blue pass flower was not there. The blue flower on the cutting by the Kennoch Gates [f64] is what the old say marks the summer's height; it had come up fewer each summer, as the old herders said, for as many summers as they could count, and this year it did not come up at all. The herders did not touch the gates. They looked at the bare ground and came back and told it at the rite fire. The Guest was then travelling with the lower columns, at their pace, and at their rite fires.

The old herders said that the flower had been going for years and nobody would listen. The young ones had never seen the cutting bare. The oldest woman of the column said the flower marks the summer's height, and no flower means the summer has no height, and the cold is walking down. A keeper of rite said it was the Guest, that her blessing at the passes had passed on the flower. Another said it was the star, which had dimmed more often. A herder asked at the fire, with the Guest sitting there, whether the cold was coming down, and what the answer was is hers to give. The decisions give her nothing to say of it. She listened, as she does, and the herder did not ask a second time.

### Months 10 to 12: with the lower columns

From summer the Guest travelled with the lower columns, the ones whose senior Doorwarden had been passed over, at their pace, which is the pace of a people with its old, its young and its herds. She sat at their rite fires and at the tables they set for strangers, and asked them to treat her as no more than a guest and a mother. She ate little and said less. The senior Doorwarden of the lower columns was there, as he had said he would be, and was courteous to her and no more than courteous. He had her at the first rite fire and did not speak of the oath.

She judged disputes at the nearest sill if the Doorwarden asked it. The Doorwardens of the lower columns are proud, and the senior would have asked nothing of her all summer. In the last month, a younger Doorwarden of one of the lower columns asked her to judge a matter of grass between two households of his column, who were quarrelling over the use of one hillside, and the senior Doorwarden did not stop him. I do not write the verdict, which is hers. (K.) That it was asked is the news of the summer in those columns.

She used the Reading on no one that I have written. The decisions give it to a stranger she does not trust, one at a time, in the open, and no doubtful stranger came to the fires of the lower columns by my telling. If the god's own file has one, the Keeper settles it.

In the eleventh month the star dimmed again, in the middle of the night, for about an hour. It dimmed again in the twelfth. Both nights fell at the columns' fires, high in the grass, with the sky clear. The people at the fire turned to the Guest's face when the blue deepened, as they had at the first, and what her face showed they tell differently. The decisions give her nothing for those nights, and what she did is hers. (K, the dimmings are not known to be anyone's doing.)

No weight came on the road to the lower columns, and nothing odd on the high grass but the bare cutting.

### Month 12: the order to come down

The orders went out for the year's end in the three ways, by horn and smoke, by the Marshal's riders and by the keepers of rite at every column's fire. The word was that every column was to be down at the winter city by first snow, and the Marshal was to confirm each column's coming by rider. The year closes with the columns turning toward the winter ground, the Guest with the lower columns at their pace. Her return to the ford, to stand in it before the snow and know who has crossed, falls after the year's close and is not written here. The counted road, the walkers who come over the heights once in a generation, did not come this year.

## What the year did to the people

The stores held to the thaw, which they did not in Year 1, and fewer of the old and the very young died than the winter before. The war band ate last and lean from the first snow, and held, and the captains are not reconciled to it. The hall is thin in tolls by the same count as last year, and the far toll houses, by what was seen, are not. The Marshal knows it, and has said nothing, and Kessan has said he never counted the far houses. The Warlord sits at the Guest's right hand at the table, and the high rite is not pleased. The lower columns have had the Guest at their fires for a summer and are not made into her people by it, quite. The senior Doorwarden of the lower columns was heard and not answered as he asked. The herder's household is kept at a hearth that keeps it, and the column that lost him paid for it. The star dimmed five nights, more than last year, and the flower did not come up. The households say that if even the Guest does not know, then nobody knows, and the old women are quieter this winter than they were.

The quarrel between the Warlord and the Doorwardens, each of whom thinks the other holds too much, stands a little changed: the Guest has given each the right hand in his own place, and both have noticed that the lower columns, who are neither, have a grievance of their own.
