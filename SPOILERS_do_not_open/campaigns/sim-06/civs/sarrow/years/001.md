# The Sarrow, Year 1

Months are counted from the year's opening. Month 1 is the first month of autumn. The people themselves reckon from first snow to first snow, and they say so, so in their own telling this year began in the third month and has not yet ended. This file keeps the game's count.

Where a thing turns on a power, or on an outcome the Keeper rolls, it is marked (K) and the Keeper's ruling is awaited in `report-001.md`.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the Guest comes early

The host was still coming down. Some columns were already in the valley round the winter city, the rest strung out along the road behind. Kessan the Warlord had come down early with the sworn band to receive them, as he does, and Idris Veld the First Doorwarden had come with her keepers of rite, so that both were in the winter city and at the winter hall when the Guest came, which was luck, and the people said afterward that it was not luck.

In the hour before first light on the first day, before the horns of the morning watch, the toll watch at Marle Ford (two men, as a toll house is kept) saw a tall woman walking down to the crossing out of the dark, stooping though nothing was over her, with a staff and a key at her neck. She stood in the middle of the ford and laid salt on the stones and spoke over it. The older of the two watchers, who had the oath of the sill as a boy at his mother's hearth, knew the words and said later that she spoke the guest oath in the old form, the form the Doorwardens keep, and that he stood at his post with his hands open and did not move, because a man does not move at the ford when the oath is being said. (K, what the blessing did and what it let the god know.) The younger watcher ran for the city at first light. The older stayed, and when she came out of the water and passed the toll house he bowed his head and she did not stop. He said he was neither asked for a toll nor asked his name.

She came to the gate of the winter city by midmorning and stood outside it with her hands open and empty and the key showing. The gate guard that day were two of the sworn band and a Doorwarden's boy. The senior of the two did not bar the gate and did not bid her in. He sent the boy at a run for Idris Veld, and the two stood and looked at her, and she said, "I am a guest and I ask the sill," and waited. The household's teaching was to bid in the first one who stoops at the door, and she was that one, and she was at the gate three months before first snow, a guest at the wrong season, and the senior guard did not know whether the teaching held out of season. He said afterward that he held for the length of a long breath and then Idris came up the street with her stick and bade the Guest in, by word, in the old form, and the gate guard stood aside.

Idris sent for Kessan and for the other offices. The hall was set within the afternoon: the door open, the seat laid, the host (Kessan, as the Warlord holds the table in the winter hall) breaking bread first and eating first. The Marshal of the Road was already in the city, having come down ahead of the columns. The Mother of the Hearth and the Steward of Salt and Stores were sent for from their houses in the valley and from the granaries, and came in before dark.

At the table, before the bread was broken, the Guest said to Kessan and then to Idris, each across the table, that she would ask one thing of each, and asked it, with the key in her hand and her eyes on theirs. (K, the Reading of the Host.) Neither of them had been asked such a thing before, and each said afterward that it was not like being questioned. Kessan said it was like being looked at from the inside by someone patient. Idris said only that it was courteous. Neither of them in fact meant the Guest any harm; Kessan's fear was of being unready, not of her, and Idris had waited thirty years for the door to be knocked. If the Guest read otherwise it was her own reading, which the Keeper rules. Kessan did not like it. He said nothing in the hall, being a careful man with his word, and said at his own fire that night that a guest may ask what a guest likes, and that a host may mind it.

Then she ate, and spoke little. What she asked of each, and what each said, is in the report. In short: to Kessan, the host on the road and in the gates was his, and she asked for his counts, the war band, the columns and the day each should come down, and the passes. To Idris, the rite and the table law and the sill courts were hers, and she would teach her the oath herself. To the Steward, the winter stock. To the Marshal, the toll houses, the two passes, the ford and the road, and who crossed. To the Mother of the Hearth, that the households' grievance would be heard first. She named only Idris Veld a guest, that night, and no other. The people were told: "I am the Guest. I have no name. You may call me Guest." They called her that, and some of the Doorwardens in a lower voice called her the Key.

Kessan gave her his counts the next day without being asked twice, and gave them true, as far as the record of the band goes: about three hundred and sixty sworn under eight captains, about three and a half thousand more who would carry a spear, the columns still on the road and what each had behind it. He was relieved, a little, to be asked for counts and not for the host, and said so to his captains, which was a mistake, because the captains took from it that the Lintel had been told to hand over numbers.

The Steward gave her a count of the winter stock that was favorable by about a fortnight. He did not lie. He counted what the granaries held and left out what the columns had yet to bring, which he knew would be thin. He is a man who does not give a host a hard number in autumn, and he says so: a people given the hard number in autumn eats worse all winter.

Two nights in this month, some days apart, the star dimmed. The blue deepened, the star stood dim for about an hour, and it was as before. Those awake saw it. The first of them fell within days of the Guest's coming, and by the second the city was saying the two were hers. The people have always seen such nights, but not within the same fortnight as a woman with a key.

### Month 2: the wolves

The columns were still coming down. At the night fold of one column on the road, wolves came among the sheep before the dogs gave tongue. The column lay on the road a good many days from the winter city; the Guest was in the city and had no part in it. (K, the outcome.)

The column's circumstances, as they bear on it. It was a column of the host as the record has it: its herds, its captain, its keeper of rite. The fold was at the roadside with the fires a short way off. The night watch was six herders, among them two boys, with eleven dogs, and three of the sworn band at the fires. The dogs were late to give tongue, which is the whole of the trouble: a pack among the sheep in the dark before the alarm is three parts won already. The herders had slings, staves and the sworn band's three spears. The captain's other spears slept at the fires. Light: a late moon.

Success would be the pack driven out in the first minutes with a handful of animals lost and none of the herders hurt. The fold held and the flock gathered by dawn. Failure would be the fold broken, the flock scattered in the dark along the road, a herder or a boy killed or mauled, and days spent gathering what could be found, with the sheep and goats lost counted in the tens at least. I write the rest of the year in both branches only where it matters, and it matters little: in the first branch the column comes down with a flock short by a few head and in a good humor, with a wolf skin to show; in the second it comes down short by several dozen, with a funeral behind it, and in a low mood, and the Steward's count in month 4 is thinner by that much. The state numbers at year's end are set between the two, and a note says by how much each branch moves them.

In the city nothing was known of the wolves for days. When the rider came in, the people said at once that the Guest had driven the cold's hounds ahead of her, which is the first story of the year and is wrong.

### Month 3: the doors, the oath, the first snow

Late autumn. The columns were coming down. In the third month the Guest blessed the door of the winter hall and then the gate of the winter city, each with a handful of salt, the key and the oath, standing in the threshold, and was not asked to wait at the sill, since in the winter city she had been bidden in. (K, what each blessing did.) The Steward watched her draw the salt from her own bag, a small bag, and said afterward that it was a very small bag.

She taught the oath, in her own person, to Idris Veld and to three Doorwardens Idris chose. The three were not the three most senior. Idris said she chose by sense, as the Guest asked, and the senior Doorwardens took it as an insult, in particular one old Doorwarden of the lower columns who had expected to be asked, and who said at the rite fire of the city that a woman who chooses by sense instead of age will find the sense runs out. The three chosen, who are not named in any record of mine because no one said their names aloud in my hearing, laid three lesser marks at the Guest's order: one on the toll house at the ford, one on the standing house of a hearth mother where a Doorwarden has taken the rite fire, and one on a doorway Idris chose and told her, which was the door of the salt store, kept by the Steward. (K, whether the marks hold.) The Steward did not say a word about the salt store door. He stood at it while the mark was laid and watched the Doorwarden's hands. The next day he moved his own ledgers out of the room beside the door.

She asked the Steward how much salt she might draw for blessings without hurting the winter stock, because her own bag was small. He gave her a measure that would not shame a poor household and looked at her while he gave it.

The cranes passed for some days toward the plain in the late weeks, as they do each year. The old women said afterward that the cranes had gone to tell the plain, and that is wrong in a way the plainsfolk will not enjoy.

The first snow fell late in the month. That night the warlord's door was left unlatched, the seat set, and a bowl filled and set on the sill, as the people do every year. The Guest, who had been waiting inside the city for weeks and was not waiting at the door, was seen to come to the table of the warlord's house that night and sit in the seat that was set. That did something to the room. Kessan, who had set the seat for thirty years for a Guest nobody had ever seen, watched the bowl for a long time and then sat down himself at the foot of his own table. The old women who argue every winter whether the Guest is a woman, a man or a wind were in the room, and did not argue. The bowl was found empty at dawn, and the people say a stranger or the Guest drank it; the Doorwardens say only that it was empty.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the gates and the count

The Guest held the winter city and did not step. She walked the gates by night, once a week, unannounced, and the gate guard of the sworn band learned within a fortnight to be at their posts when the Guest might come. She heard the Mother of the Hearth, and the households' grievances before anyone else's, as she had said. The grievance of that month, which the Mother brought, was an old one: that the war band's horses were wintered on hay the households had cut, and the hearths went short for it. The Mother asked that the Guest sit at the households' tables in the low quarter. (K, nothing, but the god's reply is hers.)

She asked the Steward weekly for the count of grain, salt, fuel and dried meat, and the Steward gave it. In the fourth month, for the first time, the count came in lower than the first one. The Steward did not say why. It was not in dispute that the autumn count was favorable, only whether it had been a lie, and he said it was not and the Guest weighed it. (K, whether the god read it.) By that count the stock would run short of the thaw by about ten days at the full ration.

### Month 5: the sill court, the third dimming

The First Doorwarden hears hard matters at the hall door. In the fifth month a hard matter came that touched the warlord: a rider of the sworn band had struck one of the Marshal's toll watchers over a toll in kind, at the ford, in an argument over the weight of a barley measure. The nearest Doorwarden sent it to the First Doorwarden and the Warlord together, as the custom is, and they sent it to the Guest, who sat at the hall door with Idris at her right hand and Kessan at her left. She judged by the sill law, which is the people's law and not hers: that a blow struck at a toll watcher at a sill is a blow struck at the sill, that the toll was to be paid as the measure of the house stood and not as the rider thought, and that the rider should house the watcher at his own hearth three nights and feed him, as the old sill remedy goes. The rider did so, grudging. Kessan said afterward that it was a just thing and that he would have liked it better if the Guest had given his man a day to answer. The captains' murmur began here and went on all winter: the Guest holds the sill court and the Warlord's men answer at it. The people at large did not notice. The Doorwardens did, and were pleased.

Once, privately, she asked Idris whether any rite the Doorwardens keep says a name over a sill, and whose. Idris told her what the Doorwardens' recital says: that there is no name said over the sill, only the oath; that the first to take the toll at Marle Ford for the Sarrow themselves was Marda Sill, whose name the sill is said to bear; and that before her the Sarrow guided for wardens of a league in the lowlands. She did not ask why the Guest asked, and the Guest did not say. She did not ask again.

The star dimmed a third time, in the fifth month, in the middle of the night, for about an hour. The gate guard on the wall saw it. The city, which was mostly asleep, heard of it at breakfast. The talk that morning was that this was the third, and that three had been blessed.

### Month 6: the thin end

By the sixth month the weekly count showed what the Steward had held back in autumn. The thaw was still some weeks off and the stock would run out ten days before it at the full ration. The Guest ordered the stores shared by the hearth mothers, the poorest households first. The Mother of the Hearth backed it at once. The Steward resisted, not in the hall but in his own office, where he said that the hearth mothers could not be trusted with a count; and then he did it, because the Guest had ordered it and Idris had backed it and the Mother had said she would have it done whether or not he would. The sharing was by hearth: the poorest households in the low quarter first, the war band's houses last, and the war band's captains took this badly, though they said nothing to the Guest. Kessan ordered his riders to hunt. They came back with two ibex and a boar and a good deal of cold, and the captains had their share and ate it. This is the first time the Lintel and the Guest did something together.

The old and the youngest ate least. More of them died this winter than the autumn count would have predicted, not many more. The hearth mothers say the number would have been worse without the sharing, and the Steward says it would have been worse with it.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Month 7 and 8: the thaw

The thaw came at the usual time and the passes opened. The last of the barley was eaten the week the passes opened. The Steward had managed it to the day.

The Guest walked out with the Marshal of the Road and a small escort of his riders, up the road to the northeast, and walked both passes on foot. The Marshal named Stair Pass as the more crossed, and in it, where no one keeps it and no toll house stands, she laid salt and spoke the oath and blessed it. (K.) The riders were uneasy. They said afterward that she looked at everyone who passed, and that the packers and drovers she looked at looked back with the shy look of a man who has seen the host's Guest and has been told not to stare.

The Marshal ordered the toll houses to keep an honest count and to give any traveler a night's feeding before asking his business. Some of the toll watchers mocked it, because the custom is to feed first anyway and ask later, and the order only said what the custom said. The Marshal did not enforce it beyond saying it once. He is a careful man and does not like orders that restate the custom, because they sound like the custom being doubted.

The Marshal also told every toll house to bring the Guest, and not turn away, any stranger with odd tools, odd speech, or any who was wrong in one way, tall or shadowless. Over the spring and summer the toll watchers brought her eleven. I have no one among them to report. They were ordinary travelers by my reckoning: an Aldermark trader whose river speech was strange to the watcher, two lowland brothers with rope makers' tools, a carter who was the tallest man the Sarrow had seen, a woman with a limp, a pair of plainsfolk herders, a smith, a man with a scarred face, a boy, and an old man who sang at the sill. Each was fed at a sill table and sent on. I have not placed a stranger of my own making among them: if the Keeper wants one of them to be what the Guest looks for, the Keeper rules which. (K.)

### Month 9: the columns go up

The columns went up with the herds, as they do every spring, to the high grass. The Guest did not go with them, being at the Marshal's toll houses on the road. Kessan led the host's going up as he has for eleven years, and was glad to, and the captains were glad of it, and he said at his fire that the host is the one thing the Guest has not asked for.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Months 10 and 11: on the high grass

The high grass was ordinary this summer, better than last year's. The herds fattened. The columns had the grass to themselves, since the trains from the river kingdom crossed and paid as they do, and no more of them than last year. The tolls that came in to the winter hall were thin again.

From summer the Guest travelled with one column at a time, at the column's pace, which is the pace of a people with its old, its young and its herds, and sat at the rite fire of each. She did not ask to be treated as more than a guest and a mother. Over the summer she went with two columns, each for some weeks, and the keepers of rite who sat with her said she ate little, said less, and listened. She ate at the tables the Doorwardens set for strangers. She used the Reading on those she did not trust, one at a time and openly: three in all, a trader of the Aldermark who had carried a false measure, a Sarrow packer who was in debt to the toll and had sold the loading of two mules, and a captain's cousin. (K, what the Reading gave.) Nobody was struck. The trader paid in kind, and the packer was housed by the rite fire for a month, and the captain's cousin said afterward that he was grateful to have been asked and in truth was frightened.

She judged disputes at the nearest sill if the Doorwarden asked it. The Doorwardens asked it rarely. A Doorwarden who asks the Guest to judge hears at the next rite fire that he could not judge himself, and the keepers are proud. She did not insist.

### Month 12: the order to come down

Orders for the year went out through the three channels: horn and smoke for the plain words, the Marshal's riders along the road, and the keepers of rite at every column's fire. The word was that all columns were to come down to the winter city by first snow, and the Marshal was to confirm each column's coming by rider. The Guest's return to the ford, to take the sill once more, falls after the year's close and is not written here. The year closes with the columns turning toward the winter ground.

## What the year did to the people

The wolves, the thin stock, the sharing, the late harvest of tolls: these are the ordinary things, and the people met them in the ordinary way. What was not ordinary was the Guest, who is in their house now. The Doorwardens' standing is up, since the Guest has taught the oath to the First Doorwarden and three of hers and has sat at their court. The Warlord's standing is neither up nor down, but his captains are wary, since the Guest has the sill court and a name for the host and the Warlord keeps only the road. The households are warmer toward the Guest than anyone else, since she heard them first. The Steward is uneasy. The old women have something new to argue about.

How the quarrel between the Warlord and the Doorwardens, each of whom thinks the other holds too much, stands at the year's close: a little worse on the surface, because there is now a third who holds the sill, and a little better underneath, because for the first time both of them have been asked the same question by the same one.
