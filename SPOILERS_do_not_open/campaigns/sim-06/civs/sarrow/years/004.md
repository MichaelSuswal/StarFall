# The Sarrow, Year 4

Months are counted from the year's opening. Month 1 is the first month of autumn. The people reckon from first snow to first snow, so in their own telling this year began in the third month and has not yet ended. This file keeps the game's count.

Where a thing turns on a power, or on an outcome the Keeper rolls or prices, it is marked (K), and `years/004-report.md` says what is asked. Where the year runs two ways, both are written briefly and marked Branch A and Branch B.

The year's world, as the Keeper gave it: an ordinary year of weather, first snow at its usual time, the passes open at the thaw in the ordinary way. In autumn the trains crossed as last autumn. From the thaw, in month 7, fewer trains came to the far toll houses than last spring, by about a third, and those of the river kingdom paid the raised toll with open complaint. In the summer, months 10 to 12, no train of the river kingdom came over the road at all; trains of other peoples came as before. No envoy of the river king came. No stranger out of the ordinary came by the road. The blue pass flower did not come up again. The star dimmed seven nights (months 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, 11, 12), and the cranes went over in month 3.

My numbers start from the Keeper's line on Year 3: the stores failed some weeks short of the thaw, about three hundred died over the ordinary, the sowing was short. The people stand at the opening of this year at about 17,650, with the seed drawn down and the herds culled hard.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the ford, the gate, the hall

The columns came down the road as every year, the herds smaller from two winters of culling. Kessan and Idris Veld were in the winter city to receive them. The Doorwardens said it was custom and the captains said it was habit, and nobody argued the word for long. The Guest came down with the lower columns and was not yet at the ford when the year turned.

She came to Marle Ford [f61] first, on foot, with the staff and the key, in the first days. The older toll watcher gave her the count from the knotted cord of who had crossed since she was last there: the trains as many as last year or near it, shorter, fewer beasts to the train. He told it plain. This autumn's trains were crossing as last autumn's had.

Then she asked him what she had not asked before. In his father's time, and in the old cords, was the toll reckoned by the beast, by the load or by the head, and what would it have been for a train of the size that crosses now. She told him she did not accuse the toll house of anything, and she wrote what he said on a cord of her own and said she held it as his answer and no more. The older watcher said by the beast. A knot for a laden beast, a short knot for a man carrying on his back, and the head never tolled: a man was fed, and the loaf was the feeding and not the toll. He said the knot a beast had stood unchanged in his father's cords and in his own until the raise, and that the raise made it a knot and a half. For a train of the size that crosses now he could not say what his father would have taken, because his father's trains were larger, with more beasts to the train and more of them, and a train of today's size did not cross in his father's day. The younger watcher, asked if he would answer, said by the load: that his grandfather had told him a mule was reckoned by what it carried and not by its legs, and a half-laden beast paid by half. He said he did not know which was older. The two did not quarrel in front of her. Both said the head was fed and not tolled.

She asked whether any train this season had been met at the ford by someone other than a watcher, and what for. The older watcher said no. Trains are met by the watchers, fed and counted. Nobody else stood at the ford to meet a train this season that he saw, and he said he would have seen. The younger said the same.

She walked the two miles to the gate and was bidden in by the gate guard with no held breath. At the hall she was met by Idris, and the hall was set.

That evening, before she spoke to anyone else, she asked Kessan to sit with her alone at the hall table with bread between them. The door was shut. What the people know is that Idris Veld stood at the door with a Doorwarden's boy and that the two sat for the length of a meal and a little more. Kessan came out and walked to his own house without speaking. At his own fire that night he told his captains only that she had told him first what she would say in the open, so that he should not hear it before the offices; that she had not called the raise a theft; that she had said a man afraid acts alone; that she meant to keep his road and his spears; and that she would not read him unless he asked it. He said he had not asked it. The captains took it as the Lintel's due and some as a favor, and he said it was a courtesy and was not asked to say more. (K, nothing; what she said and what Kessan said back at the table is in her file.)

The next day at the hall, with Kessan, Idris Veld, the Steward, the Marshal and the Mother of the Hearth present, she answered three things aloud, each of them asked a year ago.

To the Steward, who had asked what he was to do if what was brought was less than the cord: the household's keep is not cut. If the offerings fall short, the shortfall is drawn from the hall stores and entered on the separate cord as the Guest's debt, and cleared from the next offerings before any is put to other use. She takes no part of the offerings for her own table. She would tell him the number she wanted at first snow. Idris entered it and stood witness. The Steward said that was a debt he could write, and wrote it.

To the Mother of the Hearth, who had asked whether the toll is the host's to raise or the sill's: the toll is the sill's to keep and the host's to take. A raise is the host's to propose and the hall's to hear, with Kessan, Idris and the Guest at one table, and it is not made by one mouth. Kessan's raise stands through this year, since the stores need it, and she asked no one to unsay it. After the first trains of the thaw are counted, the three of them sit with the cords; if the trains have fallen by more than the raise has bought, the toll comes down to where it was or to what the cords bear. The Mother carried it to the households and told them it was said in front of her. She said in the hall that it was the first time she had heard a host's word held to a count. Kessan, who had heard it the evening before, kept his face and said nothing.

To the keeper of rite who had asked whether the king's letter should be answered, by Idris's carrying, since he was not in the hall: yes, and not by a clerk. Kessan's answer stands as far as it goes, and the sill hears a claim made in person. She would send that word under her own hand to the chancery of the river kingdom, and she told Kessan and Idris what it said before it went. She said it aloud, in the hall, in the hearing of all five. The words were: "To the one who wrote to whoever holds the ford. Your letter was heard at a rite fire of the Sarrow. The Sarrow keep the sill. A claim made by letter is a claim not yet made, and the sill hears a claim only face to face. If the king, or one he trusts to speak for him, will come to Marle Ford, he will be met at the ford as a guest, fed and seated and left in peace until he has eaten and slept, and heard at the hall table. No share is owed by the Sarrow, and none is refused, until the claim has been heard. Bring the old measure you name, and the word of how it was kept. I have no name to sign. They call me Guest, and Mother of the Door." Kessan said it was what he had said, with more door in it. Idris said the sill is a door and that the last words were the first the Guest had said of herself that was not a refusal. The Marshal and the Steward said nothing. The Mother said, low, "Mother of the Door," and was heard.

The folded word, tied with a cord and a pinch of salt, was set in Idris's hand as witness, to go down to the ford. Idris sent it that afternoon by a Doorwarden's boy and a gate guard to the older watcher, with the terms as the Guest gave them: that he give it, folded and tied, to the master of the first train leaving the ford for the river kingdom this autumn, with her toll for the train waived for the carrying; that if no train left before the snow he keep it dry and give it to the first train after the thaw; that if the master would not carry it he was not to press him and was to bring it back to her; that he was to say nothing of where she is, what she can do or the size of the host, and not argue the claim. (The decisions give no step for how the folded word reached the watcher. I wrote it by Idris's hand and boy.)

The Marshal asked, before the offices, what he was to do if a house did not send. She said to tell her which houses sent and which did not. She did not accuse any house; she asked what a host asks of a host. (See the loan, below.)

The older watcher read the terms back twice, and kept the folded word in the chest with his father's cord. The first master he asked, of a train of the river kingdom that crossed in the second week of the month on its way home, looked at the salt and the tie and would not touch it: he said it was a king's business and he was a carter. The watcher did not press him and fed him. Some days after, a second master of the river kingdom, with a train of mixed goods and a lame mule, said he would carry it for a waived toll, and asked who it was for and what was in it. The watcher said it was a word of the Sarrow to the one who had written to the ford, and that the Sarrow keep the sill; he said nothing of the Guest or of the host. The master looked at the salt a while, said he knew the road to the chancery and would give it to a clerk there, and put the word inside his coat. The toll for the train was waived by the watcher in the Guest's name, and the knot for it was tied apart on the cord with a mark. The train left the ford for the river kingdom that morning by the road north-east and the river, as the master chose. (K, what becomes of it on the road and at the chancery. I write nothing past the moment it left the ford.)

The Marshal's order. The Marshal told every toll house that for this year the toll was to be taken in grain and seed barley first, wherever a train carried it, before cloth, iron or coin, and that every train carrying grain was to be sent word of by rider. The toll watchers took it in the way they took the order to feed a traveler, as a restating of what the stores already needed, and some of the traders took it badly: a train that carries cloth and a little barley for its own beasts finds its barley taken first.

The loan. The Marshal sent riders that same week to the two farthest toll houses on the road to the northeast, with the hall's word. He did not go himself. He sent the senior of his riders, the one who had carried the far cords to the hall two years running, and two others, with fodder for the horses and no escort. They set out for the two farthest houses by the road, and the Keeper prices the ride. What they said at each door was what the hall had given: that the hall asks of the houses, as a loan, in the open, a share of grain and seed from their stores and smokehouses, to be returned from next year's toll; that the hall accuses no house; that each house is to say what it sends and what it does not, and the riders bring it back. (K, the loan, below.)

### Month 2: the counts begin, the star

The columns were down. The Steward and the hearth mothers of each quarter began the two counts of grain, salt, fuel and dried meat, as ordered, each in his own way, the Steward by tally and the mothers by the bin. The seed grain and seed barley were counted apart from the stock this year, from the first day, and the hay of the band's horses was counted as hay. The Steward said he had learned that much.

The star dimmed one night this month, for about an hour in the middle of the night, clear. The households said it was greeting the Guest at the ford again, and the Doorwardens said they would count it.

The riders for the far houses came back before the month ended, by their own account, with the answers. (K.)

### Month 3: the two counts, the seed, the captains, the dead, the first snow

The two counts came to the Mother of the Hearth in the same hour. The Steward gave a hard number and gave it first, as he has since last year. He said that at the full ration the stock would fall short of the thaw by about twenty days. The hearth mothers' count said about twenty five. The two differed by about five days. The Steward explained three of them: the low granary's damp, which was mended in time this year, had still taken a little in the autumn rains; the herd's milk fat was counted by what the herds gave in summer and the herds gave less; the toll in kind had come in grain more and cloth less and he had counted by the old tally. Two days he could not explain, and said so. The Mother set the two side by side before the Guest and said the mothers' was nearer, as it has been every year, and that this year it did not matter whose was nearer, since both said the same thing. The ration from the first snow was set at the lower of the two counts, as the Guest had said: cut by about a quarter from the first snow, the poorest hearths by about a fifth, the band's houses by about a third. The Mother told every hearth why in the low quarter before it was done.

The seed. By the Guest's word of the first month, the seed grain and seed barley was counted, set apart, and sealed with the Steward's mark and the Mother's mark together on a knotted cord clayed over, in the old granary beside the hall, with Idris Veld standing witness. The Steward said aloud, as he had been told, that if the stores failed the herd would go first, then the dried meat, and the seed last of all, and that no captain or hearth mother was to be asked to choose it in the dark of the last fortnight. He said it flat. The Mother of the Hearth said she would hold the seal's other half and would not give it up for anyone's asking. The Steward said he had not asked her to.

The captains. Before the snow the Guest told the eight captains in the hall herself, before any of them could hear it elsewhere, that the stores were shared by hearth from the first snow, the poorest first and the war band last, and why. She thanked them for last winter's patience and asked for this one's. It was the third winter. The captains had less to say than last year. Three said they had no argument. One said that a band that eats last three winters running is a band the passes will see thin, and that he said it to the Guest because she had said she would rather a true word than a kind one. Another, the youngest of the eight, said at his own fire that night that the Guest's thanks were the first anyone had given the band for it and that he would not say it was nothing. Kessan told them the band would hold the passes on it, and that if it did not, he would be the one to say so.

The loan, reported. The Marshal told the Guest, with Kessan and Idris by, which of the two farthest houses had sent and which had not, and what. (K, below, by branch.)

Loan, Branch A: both houses sent. The riders came back with pack beasts from each, loaded with grain and seed barley, a share that the Steward counted as about a week of the full ration across the whole people, the two houses together. The watchmen at each door fed the riders, said the hall was welcome to a loan and that the smokehouses were for meat and not for grain but that the stores had barley, and sent with the grain a cord entered as a loan, to be repaid from next year's toll. The Marshal said it was what the far houses are for. The Steward entered the loan on a separate cord apart from the Guest's.

Loan, Branch B: one house sent a little, and the other sent words. The nearer of the two sent a load of grain and a few sacks of seed barley, about two days of the full ration, and said it was what the toll of the thaw had laid in that was not yet carried to the hall. The farthest sent a rider's meal and a message that its smokehouse was full of meat for the night's feeding, as the order had said, and that its store was the toll of the autumn, which the riders had not collected, and that it would send what it could spare at the spring collection. The watchmen said it to the senior rider with courtesy, and did not hurry. The Marshal said at the hall that a house that cannot send in autumn what the order asked in autumn was a house that would be asked again, and said it without heat. He was quiet after. The Steward entered what came on the separate cord.

In both branches: the riders reported, and the Marshal repeated at the hall, what each watchman said at each door, as they said it, and what they saw, which was what had been there before: new roofs of split timber, good horses in the pens, smokehouses full of meat.

The first snow came late in the month, at its usual time. At the rite fire of the winter hall, with the seat set empty as at every first snow, Idris Veld said the names of the winter's dead of last year, by hearth, as the Mother of the Hearth had brought them: about three hundred, the old and the under fives, and the dead herder's name among them, whose household is kept. The names took the length of the rite. The Guest stood at the fire through all of it and did not sit and did not eat. She told the Steward at the fire the number she wanted drawn for the Guest's debt, and he entered it, and the figure is hers and not in my record. The warlord's door was left unlatched, the bowl filled and set on the sill, as the people do. I write nothing of whether she came to the warlord's table that night. The decisions give her no step for it.

The cranes went over for some days toward the plain. The old women said they were counting the doors, the stores and now the dead.

The star dimmed once in this month, in the middle of a clear night. It was before the snow.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the right hand, the tables

The Guest stayed in the winter city through the cold and did not step. She sat at the households' tables in the low quarter, one hearth a week, as before, chosen by the Mother of the Hearth, the first a household of one of the dead of the winter before. She ate at each, and the households tested the bar on lying as they had learned to, and she answered true or was silent. Kessan sat at her right hand at the hall table, as before, and the Doorwardens' murmur was quieter this winter.

No envoy of the river king came before the thaw. The ford was watched. The older watcher said at the hall that no one of the king's had come to the ford to be met.

The star dimmed once in this month, a night with the deep snow on the ground.

### Months 5 and 6: the stores

By the fifth month anyone who could read the Steward's cords knew what the ration was for. The Mother and the hearth mothers held the quarter cut and told every hearth again why. The seed stayed in the old granary under the clay, the cord whole, and the Steward went to look at it every week and told the Mother each time that it was whole. The herd was culled less than last year because there was less herd, and the herders said it with no heat, since they had said last year what a culled herd would give. The meat was dried and eaten in the quarter that made it. Kessan sent riders to hunt in the snow as in Year 1 and Year 3, and they came back with an ibex and a deer, which was less than last time and was shared by hearth and not by the band, since the band ate last. The captains took it with no word.

The old and the very young ate least. By the end of the sixth month the first of the old in the low quarter had died of the cold and the thin food. The Doorwardens of the high rite said again that the sharing was the Guest's law of hunger. The Mother of the Hearth said again that it was the households' own custom. The Steward said that the stores did not know whose custom.

A hundred or two more of the old and the very young die than in an ordinary year, and the people come out thin and quiet and with the sowing whole.

Branch B, the stores fail some weeks short of the thaw: the herd goes first, and then the dried meat, and in the last week the Steward and the Mother break the seal together, with Idris Veld standing witness, on a part of the seed barley, as the Guest said it would be the last of all, and the people eat it to the day the passes open. The old and the under fives die beyond the ordinary, some hundreds over. The sowing is short again, and thinner than last year's. Hunger sits in the low quarter and the band's houses most.

Both branches: the star dimmed no more this winter after month 4.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Month 7: the thaw, the farthest toll house

The thaw came at its usual time and the passes opened. Branch A: the last barley went in the last days before the opening. Branch B: it had been gone some weeks.

The Guest went up the road to the farthest toll house on Pass Road with the Marshal of the Road, on foot, with a few riders. Before they went, at the hall, she told Idris Veld why she meant to lay a sixth full blessing on that door: to know by her own marking how many go in and out of it, and not to accuse any man. Idris said she would carry it to the Doorwardens as the Guest said it. The Marshal heard it said, and said at his own fire that it was one more thing he had not been asked. The party set out for the farthest toll house, on the road to the northeast, on foot. The farthest house stands on the road in the Kennoch Heights [r02], short of the road's far end; the whole road to its far end from the winter city is 68.2 miles and five days on foot by the Keeper's reckoning, and I give no other figure. The Keeper prices the walk. (K.) I have them at the farthest house in the first weeks after the thaw and back in the winter city before the sitting below.

At the door. The party came to the farthest house at an hour the watchmen were not looking for them. The keeper of the house, a man of the age to have grandchildren who has kept it a good many years, was told by the Guest, at the door, before she did anything, what she meant to do. She told him that she would lay a handful of salt on his threshold and speak the guest oath over it with the key in her hand, that she did not shut his door and did not step, that she did it to know by her own marking how many go in and out and not to accuse him or any man. He listened. He said it was a house of the Sarrow and a guest might stand in its door unasked. He said the cord of the house had nothing in it the knots did not say. Then she laid the salt and spoke the oath. What the people there saw is what the people at the ford, the hall door, the city gate, Stair Pass and Far Notch saw: a tall woman who stoops, with salt and a key, saying words they knew and had not heard from anyone but a Doorwarden. The watchmen stood with their hands open and did not move, as the older watcher at the ford did the first morning. The Marshal stood at the edge of the threshold with his cap in his hand. The riders were uneasy and quiet, as at Stair Pass. After it the blessings stood at six. (K, what the Latch did and what it let her know. What the mortals there saw is above.)

What the house showed. The roofs were the new roofs of split timber, the horses in the pens were good horses, the smokehouses were full of meat. Branch A (loan sent): the store held fewer sacks than the cord showed last autumn, by what the loan took, and the watchmen said so and showed the loan's cord, which agreed with what the Steward had entered at the hall. Branch B (loan little): the store held what it held last spring, and the watchmen said it was the toll of the autumn laid for the riders' spring collection and that they had said so to the riders in the autumn.

What the watchmen said of the trains. She asked what the trains said of the raised toll, and whether any turned back or took another road. They said the trains of the river kingdom complained loudly at their door, openly, and paid, as they had last year but with more heat; that they had not seen any train turn back from their door, and that none of the masters had spoken of another road to them. They said the trains they missed were the river's, and that they knew the faces: the trains of other peoples had come as before, and the river's had come fewer. They said that a train that does not come does not pass, and that a toll house cannot count what it does not see. The keeper said it with no hurry, as the watchmen have always said it, and with his hands open.

The Guest looked at what the house held of grain, as she said she would, and said no word of what she thought. The Marshal went with her through the house and said nothing at all. (K, nothing among the Sarrow turns on what she made of it, except the Marshal's standing in Branch B.)

The party came back to the winter city before the sitting below. The Marshal, asked by Kessan at the hall what the farthest door had been like, said that a woman stood at it with salt and a key, and the house stood as it did. He said he had walked it himself this year, with no word sent, and had not been told to.

### Month 8: the sitting, the star

The Marshal's riders and the toll watchers had brought the cords of the first trains of the thaw to the hall. The far houses' cords showed fewer trains than last spring's by about a third. The riders and the watchmen said the same. The ford's cord was read as well. The watchers said that the trains of the river kingdom had come and paid the raised toll with open complaint, loud at the ford and at the far houses, and that trains of other peoples had come as before. The Steward read the cords aloud at the hall.

The three sat with the cords: Kessan, Idris Veld and the Guest. The Steward, the Marshal and the Mother of the Hearth were called in to read the knots and the counts and to be heard, and were not at the table. Kessan spoke first. He said what the cords said: that last spring's trains had paid a half more and the hall had laid up about three quarters of the old cords; that this spring the trains were down by a third at the far houses and the ford's own tally showed no gain, so that a half more on two thirds of the trains came to no more than the old toll had taken on all of them, and the raise had bought nothing this spring that the old toll would not have. He said the stores needed what the raise bought and the stores were no better. He did not say he would unmake it. Idris said the cords did not say why the trains fell, and that the traders had complained of the raise and the watchmen said it was the river's trains that were missing. She said the sill took one toll and not two, and that the Guest had said in the hall in the autumn that if the trains fell by more than the raise bought it came down to what the cords bear, and that the cords did not say which, so the sill would take the middle of it. Kessan said the middle was a half of the raise given back, and that he would give it back to the traders at the ford and the far houses from the trains of the next month, the grain first order standing, and that the raise would be sat on again at the first trains of next spring. The Steward said the stores did not stand on a half of a raise. The Mother of the Hearth said the carriers who are Sarrow would feel the half more than the traders would, and that she would carry it to them. Kessan said that it was not made by one mouth. The Guest spoke at the table. What she said is hers. (K, not in my record. If her word differs from the mortals' middle, the Keeper settles it; what the two mortals settled stands as written.)

So the toll came down by half of the raise, to a knot and a quarter the beast, from the trains of the ninth month, and the Marshal sent it to every toll house by rider with the reason. The grain-first order stood. The loan, if any, was repaid in kind from the autumn toll, as the Guest had said.

The star dimmed in the middle of a clear night this month. The riders on the road told it. The households said it dimmed because the toll was settled, and the Doorwardens counted the dimmings against the blessings, which were now six.

### Month 9: the columns go up

The columns went up with the herds to the high grass. Kessan led the going up as he has for fourteen years, and was glad to, though the herds were small and the band was thin. The Guest did not go with the lower columns this year. She went up with the columns she had not travelled with, the upper columns, at their pace, which is the pace of a people with its old, its young and its herds. She told the Marshal and Idris at the hall, before she left, that she would sit at their fires and tables.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Months 10 and 11: the grass, the cairn, Marda, the road

In the tenth month, with the upper columns on the high grass, the Guest went up with them to the old cutting at the high gates. The blue pass flower was not there again. The oldest herders of the upper columns, summoned by the keepers of rite at her asking, said what the lower columns had said: that the line of the good grass goes up, and that the high gates were green in their grandfathers' time. She told them what she had found last year and that she did not know what it meant. At the old cutting and at one other, the foot of a valley below the gates that the oldest woman of an upper column said was summer grass for her grandfather and was cut for hay now, the herders set a cairn at the foot of the line of the grass and cut a notch in a stone beside it, as she asked through the Mother of the Hearth and the keepers of rite at those fires. They were told to cut a second notch at the first snow of the heights, and to tell the keeper of rite what the line had done, so that it was counted year on year. A herder asked at the old cutting what the stone was for. She said it was to count the line and not to hold it. He said it was the first time anyone had asked a herder to count the grass.

At each new fire she asked once what the fire meant by "as Marda held it" and by Sill, and held each answer as that fire's. The upper columns said: that Marda was the first Doorwarden of the high rite, who took the first toll and gave the oath its last words; that Marda is the name of the sill itself and no woman at all, since a sill is a thing you stand on and is held; that it is a woman who stood at Marle Ford with her hands open and the river took her, and that is why the oath ends there; that no one at the fire knew and the oath is said as it is said; that Marda is what the walkers who come once in a generation sing at the ford, and the fire had it from the counted road. One fire said it would say nothing it did not hold. She asked once at each, and no one was sent anywhere. The keepers of rite noted that she asked the same thing again at every fire.

No train of the river kingdom came over the road in the summer. The Marshal's riders told it at the hall by the end of the tenth month, since the river's trains cross the road at the thaw and through the summer and none was seen at the ford or at any of the houses, and the watchers at the ford said the same. Trains of other peoples came as before. The watchers brought the Guest's standing order for strangers three ordinary travelers, a salt peddler of the plain, two sisters walking to kin and an old man who sang at the sill, and none was placed.

What was said of it. The older watcher said at the hall that no train of the river kingdom had come to the ford in the summer and that he had not known a summer without one. The Marshal said that a king who sends a letter in the month the toll is raised, and keeps his trains home in the summer the toll is lowered, has watched the ford. The Steward said that salt had come with the river's trains and that none would come now, and that the salt in the store would not last the winter at the ration. The Mother of the Hearth said that the households would hear it from her. Idris Veld said that the sill hears a claim made in person and that no one had come to make one. Kessan said it was the letter, or the raise, or the poor year the traders speak of, and that he did not know which, and that he would not send his riders down the road beyond the houses to find out without asking. He sent a rider with the question up to the column where the Guest was. (K, what she says or does not. The question is below. Nothing was answered while she was away. No party was sent out.)

The star dimmed twice more in the tenth and the eleventh months, a night each, clear. The herders high in the grass saw it as they sat at the fire. The people at the fires looked at the Guest's face when the blue deepened, as they have, and what her face showed they tell differently.

The question of the stores went the same way. The Steward asked by the same rider, at Kessan's side, what the hall should do if no river train came before the first snow and the salt ran short. It met silence. The Marshal's rider came back with nothing, and Kessan said nothing of it. The Mother of the Hearth, in the eleventh month, put to a keeper of rite for the Guest whether the households were to be told the salt would not last the winter at the ration. It met the same silence, and she told the households herself, plainly, that it would be short.

### Month 12: the notch, the order to come down

The first snow of the heights lay on the cutting in the twelfth month, a week before the columns came down. The herders cut the second notch at both cairns and went to the keeper of rite and told him what the line had done: it stood where the oldest say it stood last year, no higher. The oldest woman of the upper column said it was the first year anyone had known the line stand, and the oldest man said a line that stands one year has gone up forty. The herders were not made easy by it, and said so. They brought the word down to the hall.

The star dimmed in the twelfth month, once, high in the grass, clear.

Orders went out for the year's end by horn and smoke, by the Marshal's riders and by the keepers of rite at every column's fire: every column down at the winter city by first snow, the Marshal to confirm each by rider. The Guest came down with the upper columns and stood at Marle Ford before the snow and took the sill again. I write nothing of her words there. The counted road did not come this year.

## What the year did to the people

The stores held or failed by the branch above, and the people are thinner in both. The raise of Year 3 stood through the spring and was halved at the sitting, since the cords showed that the trains had fallen by what the raise bought. The river kingdom's trains, which paid with complaint in the spring, did not cross at all in the summer, and the hall does not know why. The folded word left the ford with a train master of the river kingdom and nothing more is known of it. The far houses were asked for a loan and sent, or sent little, by the branch above, and the farthest door was marked with the Guest's own hand before the keeper's eyes. The herders set a cairn at the line of the grass at two places and the line stood. The star dimmed seven nights, the most yet, and the cranes went over. The Guest was with the upper columns all the summer and the hall's questions to her waited. The captains have eaten last three winters running, and the youngest of the eight said it aloud.

The quarrel between the Warlord and the Doorwardens stands, with a king's letter laid across it and a folded word gone after it. Kessan has now been asked to unsay a raise by the cords and did it, half, before the hall; the captains have not forgiven him for that and the households have, a little.
