# The Sarrow, Year 3

Months are counted from the year's opening. Month 1 is the first month of autumn. The people reckon from first snow to first snow, so in their own telling this year began in the third month and has not yet ended. This file keeps the game's count.

Where a thing turns on a power, or on an outcome the Keeper rolls or prices, it is marked (K), and `years/003-report.md` says what is asked. Where the year runs two ways, both are written briefly and marked Branch A and Branch B.

The year's world, as the Keeper gave it: an ordinary year of weather, first snow at its usual time, the passes open at the thaw in the ordinary way, stores short of the thaw, the war band's order in month 6 raising the toll by half from the thaw, the ford's tally down a little again, a letter from the river kingdom's chancery in month 7, the blue flower missing again, cranes in month 3, six dimmings of the star.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the ford, the gate, the hall

The columns came down the road as every year, and Kessan and Idris Veld were in the winter city to receive them. Nobody said it was chance this year. The captains said it was habit and the Doorwardens said it was custom, and the argument over the word was shorter than last year's.

The Guest came to Marle Ford [f61] first, on foot, from up the road, with the staff and the key, and stood in the crossing. The older toll watcher gave her the count of who had crossed since she was last there, from the knotted cord: the trains as many as last year or near it, shorter again, fewer beasts to the train. He told it plain and was not scolded. She asked how the trains had come and he said as before, and that the traders say the river had another poor year. He had no way to check it. (K, what the blessing gave her of the crossings. Nothing turns on it among the Sarrow.) The toll house fed the two travelers who came that day, and she saw it done.

She walked the two miles to the gate and was bidden in by the gate guard without a held breath. The senior of the sworn guard said only, "Guest," and stood aside. At night the gate is kept in pairs with a Doorwarden's boy at each post, and nobody on it sleeps. The first dimming of the year fell this month, one night, the blue deepening for about an hour. Those awake saw it. The city said it was the star greeting her, and the Doorwardens said they would count them against the blessings.

Idris had the hall set. At the hall, with Kessan, Idris, the Steward, the Marshal and the Mother of the Hearth present, the Guest answered three things aloud.

To the Steward, on the account for the dead herder's household: it was hers, and it would be paid from the offerings brought to the winter hall and the rite fires, the bread, salt, grain and milk given to the Guest, and the amount to draw was what he entered on the separate cord, no more. Idris Veld entered it and stood witness. The Steward said he had carried that cord for a year without being told how it was paid, and that he was glad to be told. He asked aloud what he was to do if the offerings in a thin year were thinner than the cord. No answer was given in front of him, and Idris said the cord was to be drawn as entered and that she would hear it again if it fell short.

To the Mother of the Hearth, for the herder who had asked at a rite fire in the tenth month whether the cold is coming down: she did not know, she would go up and look in the summer, and the Mother was to carry that to him. The Mother said she would carry it that day, and did, and the herder said at his fire that it was the first answer anyone had given him about the cutting.

To the Steward again: that she did not call his count a lie, and that she would have a short true count over a kind one. He said she had said it last year. The Mother of the Hearth laughed once, short, which the hall had not heard in two years, and the Steward did not.

### Month 2: the matter of grass, at the sill

The columns were down. In the second month, at the standing house of the lower column in the winter city, the Guest sat at the sill table with the younger Doorwarden of the lower columns, the one who had asked it in the last month of Year 2, and the two households present, a household of the upper hillside and one of the lower, who had quarrelled over one hillside's grazing. She ate at the sill table, as the households knew she would, and they knew by that that what she said would be true or silence. The captain of the column sat to confirm. She heard both households through, the older woman of the upper household first, by the sill custom of the one who stands first at the door.

The line she set is hers (K, not written by me), and she set it by the sill law as the people keep it, not by any wish of her own, as the Doorwarden had it confirmed by the captain aloud. What the people saw is that she did not use the key, that both households ate before they left, that the lower household's man said nothing, and that the older woman of the upper household said it was fair and that she did not like it. The younger Doorwarden went back to his column with the matter closed and said so at its rite fire. The senior Doorwarden of the lower columns, hearing it told, said that a hillside was settled in a morning that his own fire had argued for four years, and then said that it was settled as it should be, which the Doorwardens of the high rite noted.

### Month 3: the two counts, the cords, the sharing, the snow

The two counts of grain, salt, fuel and dried meat were made before first snow, the Steward's and the hearth mothers' of each quarter, and came to the Mother of the Hearth in the same hour. This year the Steward gave a hard number and gave it first. He said, flat, that the stock would fall short of the thaw by about twelve days at the full ration, and showed the sacks. The hearth mothers' count said about twenty days short. The two differed by about eight days. The Steward explained six of the eight: the damp in the low granary again, which he had had the roof of mended too late for, and the hay of the band's horses again counted as fuel. Two days' worth he could not explain, and said so. The Mother set the two side by side before the Guest and said the mothers' was nearer, as it had been last year, and that this year the gap was small enough to live with.

The Steward and the Mother of the Hearth told the Guest together that the stores would not last to the thaw at the ordinary share, and that no sharing by hearth would change it, only spread it. Salt: the trains had brought less again, and the Steward said what was spare, a measure smaller than last year's, and she took it.

By her word of the first month, the stores were shared by hearth from the first snow, the poorest first, the war band's houses last. She told the captains herself in the hall before they heard it elsewhere, and thanked them for the last winter's patience. The captains had little to say. Two said they had no argument. One said that a band eating last for two winters in a row was a band that would sleep on the road. Kessan told them it would hold the passes on it, and that if it did not, he would be the one to say so.

The cords. The Marshal's riders brought every toll house's cord for the year to the winter hall by first snow, the far houses' too, as the Guest had asked through the Marshal. The cords were laid out on the long table in the winter hall, house by house, and Kessan sat by at her asking, and Idris Veld, and the Marshal, and the Steward, who read the knots of each aloud as he is trained to, and the senior of the Marshal's riders who had carried the far cords. The mortals at the table saw that the cords of the houses agreed with one another by season, and agreed with what had been laid up at the hall. The Steward said so, and said it twice, as if he did not trust it. Kessan said the far houses' cords were as neat as any, and was quiet. The Marshal said, "As I told her." Nobody said whether he said it with pride or with fear. What the Guest made of them against a count of her own is hers. (K.) The Guest did not say, at the table.

The cranes went over for some days toward the plain, in flights, as every year. The old women said they were counting the doors, and a few said now that they were counting the stores.

First snow came late in the month, at its usual time. The seat was set, the door of the warlord's house left unlatched, the bowl filled and set on the sill. Kessan sat at the head of his table with the seat at his right set and empty. The decisions give her no step for that night, and I write nothing of whether she came. If she came, the god's file says.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the senior Doorwarden at the hall door, the right hand

The Guest stayed in the winter city through the cold and did not step. Word went by Idris Veld that the senior Doorwarden of the lower columns, who had asked for the oath a year ago, was to come to the hall door and be heard again, with Idris sitting beside the Guest. Before he sat, the Guest told him in the open, in front of Idris, that she would use the Reading on him once, because what she taught would let him lay a mark in her name, and that he might refuse the table. The words are in the god's decisions and the Keeper has them. What the people saw is that he stood a long while at the door, an old man in his best felt, and that he said a rite held by four was a rite held by four and that he had said it at a fire and would say it at a table. Then he sat and ate. (K, the Reading, what it showed, and whether the oath was taught; what Idris and the gate guard saw is that the door was shut after, that the two were alone with the Guest and Idris for the length of a meal, and that he came out and walked home on his own feet and told no one in the hall what had passed. I do not write it.)

The senior Doorwarden said at the rite fire of the lower columns that night only that he had eaten at the Guest's table and been read in front of the woman who keeps the high rite, and that it was done as it ought to be done. The lower columns, who were cold to the high rite, were less cold. Some of the Doorwardens of the high rite said it was a favor given to a proud man for his pride. Idris said it was not a favor, and that if she were to hold the sill alone she would sleep worse.

Kessan sat at the Guest's right hand at the hall table, as before, and was told before the hall that the Doorwardens' slight at it was theirs and not his, and that it would stay so. He took it as a man takes a thing settled and said nothing. The Doorwardens' murmur went on and was quieter.

### Month 5: the cords read, the stores

By the fifth month anyone who could read the Steward's cords could see that the stock would not last to the opening of the passes at the ordinary share. The Steward did not need telling. He read them aloud at the hall without being asked, and with the Mother of the Hearth, and said the number: the stock would run out about twelve days before the thaw at the full ration, and the mothers' count was nearer, by a week of the days.

What the people did. The Mother of the Hearth and the hearth mothers cut the household share by a fifth, the poorest hearths cut by less, the band's houses by more, and told every hearth why in the low quarter before it was done. The Steward drew the seed barley down by a third and said that if the thaw came late the seed would be short for the spring sowing, and said it to Kessan and the Mother together. The herds were culled, the weak stock first, far more than last year, and the meat dried and eaten in the quarter that made it. The old herders objected. They said a herd culled to the bone in the cold has nothing to give in summer. The Mother said that a household with no meat has nothing to give in summer either. Kessan sent riders to hunt in the snow, as in Year 1, and they came back with three ibex and a deer, little against the need, and with frozen hands. The war band's horses were wintered thinner than ever, and the hay was counted as hay, not as fuel, and the hearths kept their own.

The Doorwardens of the high rite said, as in the year before, that the Guest's sharing was a law of hunger and that a people fed by hearth were fed by her. The Mother of the Hearth said it was the households' own custom and that she would say it every month, and the Steward said the stores would not know whose custom.

The star dimmed twice in this month, a pair, on two nights some days apart, both clear. The households said the second was the star saying it again for those who slept through the first. The old women said it was counting the stores. The Doorwardens counted three dimmings so far this year and said at last the number was rising faster than the blessings.

The old and the very young ate least. By the end of the month the first of the old in the low quarter had died of the cold and the thin food. The count of the dead is below in both branches.

(K, the outcome. Branch A, the stores last to within a few days of the thaw: the ration is cut, the last barley goes in the last week, and a few more of the old and the youngest die than in Year 2, a hundred or two over, and the people are thin and quiet at the thaw. Branch B, the stores fail some weeks before the thaw: the last fortnight is eaten on dried meat, the herds, and the seed, hunger sits in the low quarter, and the old and the under fives die beyond the ordinary, some hundreds over, and the spring sowing is short. In both branches the toll raise below is the same, since the war band's order comes in month 6, before the outcome is known.

### Month 6: the toll raised

In the sixth month, with the stock plainly short and the passes still shut, Kessan gave the order in the hall that the toll at the ford and the passes would be raised by half from the thaw, to fill the stores. He said he had said in Year 1 that he would do it if the stores failed, and that it was the thing a warlord does when the stores fail. He did not ask the Guest's leave and did not say he was not asking it. She was in the hall, at the table, and gave no word on it that I have in my record.

What was said in the hall before and after.

Before: Kessan told the captains and the offices what he meant to do, in the hall, on the day before the order. The captains said it was right, and two said it should be doubled. The Steward said the stores needed it, and that the toll was the only part of the stores the host could move at will, and that a raise on the traders paid for itself only if the trains kept coming. The Marshal said the trains would pay it with complaint, as they had paid the last raise a generation ago, and that the far houses had their own cords to gather it. The Mother of the Hearth said that the toll fell on the small trains and the packers as hard as on the great ones, and that the small ones were the households' own kin, the carriers who are Sarrow, and that raising it by half raised their debt to the toll. Idris Veld said that the sill law set the toll by the measure of the house that stood, not by the host's need, and that the order was the host's to give and the sill's to hear, and she would hear whoever came to the sill with a complaint.

After: when the order was given, the captains struck the table. The Marshal said he would send it to every toll house by the thaw, and that a toll house that cannot say why the toll is raised is a toll house that will be asked. The Steward said it was done. The Mother asked, with the Guest in the room, whether the toll was the host's to raise or the sill's. She was asked as one asks a mother who sits at the table, and the Guest gave no answer in words that I have. Kessan said, after a time, that the toll was the host's and that the sill's part was to hear the complaint. Idris said, to him, that that was the sill's part and she would hold to it. They did not quarrel. They were both polite, and the captains did not like it.

The Doorwardens of the high rite said, outside the hall, that the Guest had said nothing because the Guest was waiting. The households said she had said nothing because it was Kessan's to say. The old women said that a house that raises the toll in the year the Guest is among it will be judged on what it does with it.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Months 7 and 8: the thaw, the far houses, the letter

The thaw came at its usual time and the passes opened. Branch A: the last barley went in the first days after the opening and the people came out thin. Branch B: the last barley had been gone some weeks, and the people came out of the winter hungry, with the low quarter marked. In both, the raised toll was in force at the ford and the passes from the first train, and the trains of the river kingdom came as last year and paid it after the thaw with complaint, loud at the ford and quieter at the sill. One trader asked at the sill whether a toll could be raised by half in a year the ford was thinner. Idris heard him, ate with him, said the sill's part was to hear him, and sent him on.

The Guest travelled from the thaw with the lower columns, as her decisions had it, and did not walk the road to the far houses. The Marshal and a captain walked them.

The walk to the far houses. At the hall, once the cords had been laid out, Kessan and the Marshal had been told together by the Guest that the Marshal was to walk the two farthest houses and the two nearest himself, and a captain of Kessan's naming was to walk with him, and neither was to send word ahead. They were to count stores, beasts and roofs and ask each watchman where the new horses were bought and with what, and bring back what they found and not what they thought she wished to hear. She told the Marshal she did not accuse him. The Marshal said nothing at the hall and said at his own fire that it was the first time in his life he had been told to walk his own road.

Kessan named the first of his eight captains, an old, plain, exact man who had never been to the far houses and had never liked the Marshal's riders. He chose him for that, and said so to the captain. They set out in the first weeks after the thaw, on foot, with two riders to carry the horses' fodder and no word sent. They set out for the two farthest toll houses on the road to the northeast, by the road, and the two near houses on the way back. No distance is in my record and I give none. The Keeper prices the walk. (K.) I have them back in the winter city before the columns go up in month 9.

At each house they did the same: arrived unannounced at the hour the watchmen eat; counted the beasts in the pens, the sacks in the stores, the racks in the smokehouses and the roofs by eye; looked at the cord and set it beside the riders' tally, and asked each watchman where the new horses were bought and with what. At each house the watchmen met them with courtesy and fed them at once, and said they were glad to see the Marshal. At the two farthest houses they saw what was there last year: the new roofs of split timber, the good horses in the pens, the smokehouses full.

Branch A, the houses are as the watchmen say: the roofs mended by the carpenters of the trains, the horses toll in kind from the Aldermark traders, the racks full for the night's feeding. The count of the beasts and the sacks agrees with the cord to within a few, and agrees with the hall's cords. The captain says so at the hall flat and plain, and the Marshal says the thin toll is the trains' and the roofs are the houses'. The Marshal keeps his office, and his riders say in the low quarter that he walked the road and was cleared. The captain says only that he saw nothing he could name wrong, and that he would not walk it again alone.

Branch B, the houses are not as the watchmen say: the count of the beasts and the racks is more than the cords show, or the roofs are paid for with more than lodging, or the watchmen answer the captain's question about the horses differently at the second house than at the first. The captain says so at the hall flat, and the Marshal is asked what he knew. The Marshal's office and the thin toll then stand differently, and Kessan is asked what he knew; Kessan said last year he never counted the far houses. The Marshal says in his own defense that a Marshal has riders. I write the year the first way where it matters, and note that the second moves the hall's standing of the Marshal down and the Warlord's up.

The letter. With the first train after the thaw, in the seventh month, a letter came from the chancery of the river kingdom, addressed to whoever holds the ford. A clerk of the train carried it, a young man with ink on his fingers, and gave it at the ford to the older toll watcher, since the watcher is the one who keeps the sill there. The older watcher can speak the river speech a little and cannot read. No one at the ford can read: the Sarrow keep count by knots and by the moon. The clerk read it aloud at the sill in the river speech, slowly, and the older watcher understood the heart of it and the younger watcher the half. It said that a yearly share of the toll of the ford and the passes was owed to the king as heir of the old power whose wardens had kept the road, that it had gone unpaid within living memory, and that it named a figure in a measure no one at the ford had heard. The clerk did not know what the measure came to in the Sarrow's own, and said so, and said he did not know whether the king's chancery meant to be paid or to be answered.

The watcher fed the clerk at the sill, as the custom is. The younger watcher ran to the hall with the letter's heart as the older had it, since a letter cannot be left. At the hall Kessan took it first and said, flat, that whoever holds the ford is the sill and that the sill is not in debt to a king it has not met. He was angry that the letter had come to "whoever holds" and not to him. Idris, hearing it, said the recital names the wardens of a league in the lowlands whom the Sarrow once guided for, and that the claim called the king their heir, and that the Doorwardens' recital said nothing of a share owed. She said it was strange that the claim spoke of "old wardens who kept the road" and that the Sarrow had been the ones to keep the road within living memory, and that the recital said the Sarrow's own first toll taker was Marda Sill. She did not say what she thought it meant.

The Steward heard the figure from the clerk and could not turn it into a measure of grain or salt, and said only that whatever it came to, the hall's toll had nothing to spare this year, which was the one thing every mortal in the hall agreed on. The Marshal said the river kingdom's traders had paid the raised toll with complaint and had said nothing of a share, and that a king who sends a letter in the same month the toll is raised has watched the ford.

Whether it is brought to the Guest: the Marshal's rider carried the letter, with the clerk's words as the watchers had them, up to the lower column where she was, in the eighth month, and it was read to her at the rite fire by a keeper of rite who has the river speech from carrying, in front of the column. What she said, if anything, is hers. (K, not in my record.) What the people did: Kessan sent back, by the same train's clerk at its next crossing in month 9, the answer in his own mouth, that the Sarrow owed no share to a king they had not met, that a guest of the king's chancery would be fed at the sill and heard like any guest, and that the toll was the host's. Idris said her word to the clerk alone, that the sill would hear a claim made in person and eat with the claimant. The clerk said he would carry both. The Marshal says no more word has come.

The strangers. The toll watchers brought the Guest five this year, fewer again, since the newness is gone: a carter with one arm, an Aldermark trader's wife who spoke the river speech well and the Sarrow speech not at all, two plainsfolk brothers with a sick ox, an old man who sang at the sill, a smith with a burn scar. All ordinary by my reckoning. I place none.

The star dimmed twice in the seventh month and once in the eighth, a pair and a single, on clear nights. The herders on the road up said they watched it dim the way a man watches a lamp in a window.

### Month 9: the columns go up

The columns went up with the herds to the high grass. Kessan led the going up, as he has for thirteen years, and was glad to, though the herds were smaller from the winter's cull. The Guest was with the lower columns, who were among those going up. The senior Doorwarden of the lower columns went up with them.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Months 10 and 11: the cutting, the herder, Marda Sill

In the tenth month the lower columns went up to the old cutting at the high gates with the Guest with them. The blue flower was not there again. She stood at the cutting and looked at it herself, and asked the oldest herders how the line of the grass had moved in their lives and whether this was a bad year or a long one. What they said:

The oldest man of the lower columns, who is past seventy, said the flower came down to the foot of the cutting when he was a boy and was thick enough to cut for the colour, and that it has gone up the slope in his life as the grass has, and that in his father's day the high gates themselves were green in summer where now there is rock and thin turf. The oldest woman said the line of the good grass has gone up the hill every year she has watched it and that the herds are now put where the old herders put them in the third week of summer, in the first. A younger herder, a man of the age to have children, said it is not a bad year, since last year was not either, and that it is a long one, and the old have it right. Several said the valleys below the gates that were summer grass for their grandfathers are cut for hay or left. They said it as a matter of course and not as grief. They said it had been going for years and nobody listened, and some said it with the Guest at the fire.

The herder who had asked her in Year 2 was there. She told him what she found, plainly, and said she did not know what it meant. He said it was something to be told.

At each fire she asked once what the lower columns meant by "as Marda held it" and by Sill. Each fire answered for itself and she held each as that fire's answer. Some of what they said: that Marda was a woman who stood in the ford and was never seen to leave it, and that "as Marda held it" is a promise to hold a thing the way a person holds a door against the wind; that Marda was the name of the river's first crossing and not a woman at all; that Sill is the name of the stone a person stands on to be counted, and that Marda Sill is the one who counted; that Marda is older than the Sarrow's speech and that no one at the fire knew what it meant; that it is a name said at the close of the oath because one must say it and not because one knows. One fire said nothing and said it did not wish to say a thing it did not hold. She asked once at each and no one was sent anywhere. The people note that she asked the same thing at every fire.

The star did not dim in these months. The herders high in the grass said it was resting, and that it would be worse for it.

### Month 12: the order to come down

Orders went out for the year's end as before, by horn and smoke, by the Marshal's riders and by the keepers of rite at every column's fire: every column down at the winter city by first snow, the Marshal to confirm each by rider. The Guest came down with the lower columns and stood at Marle Ford before the snow, and took the sill again. I write nothing of her words there. The counted road did not come this year.

## What the year did to the people

The stores did not last to the thaw at the ordinary share, and the people knew it from the fifth month. The sharing from the first snow held the households together, the cull was heavy, and the war band ate last for a second winter and is tired. The toll was raised by half from the thaw by the Warlord's order, the traders paid with complaint, and the hall's laying up is better than it was and not yet what it was a generation back. The far houses were walked by the Marshal and a captain, and the roofs, the horses and the smokehouses are as they were, and what the walk found is in the report. A letter came from the river kingdom, and the Warlord's answer and the First Doorwarden's answer are not the same answer. The senior Doorwarden of the lower columns sat at the Guest's table in the open and is quieter. The herders saw the cutting bare again and know the line of the grass is going up. The star dimmed six nights, the most yet. The old women are quieter than they were.

The quarrel between the Warlord and the Doorwardens stands as it did, with a king's letter now laid across it, and the lower columns are, for the first time, a little less cold to the high rite.
