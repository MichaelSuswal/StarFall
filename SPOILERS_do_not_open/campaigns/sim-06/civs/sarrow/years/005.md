# The Sarrow, Year 5

Months are counted from the year's opening. Month 1 is the first month of autumn. The people reckon from first snow to first snow, so in their own telling this year began in the third month and has not yet ended. This file keeps the game's count. Days are the days of the moon's month, as the people count them.

Where a thing turns on a power, or on an outcome the Keeper rolls or prices, it is marked (K), and `years/005-report.md` says what is asked. Where the year runs two ways, both are written briefly. The winter stores run as Branch A and Branch B. The riders beyond the houses run as Branch R1 and Branch R2. The day at the farthest door runs as Branch D1 and Branch D2. The three do not depend on one another except where I say so.

The year's world, as the Keeper gave it: an ordinary year of weather, first snow at its usual time, the passes opening at the thaw in the ordinary way. The seed of last year came through whole, the sowing was a full one, and last summer's harvest was ordinary. Salt is short at the turn of the year and trains that carry it are few on every road: two came over before the snow, small, of other peoples, and their masters asked a high price. In autumn trains of other peoples crossed as last autumn. From the thaw in month 7 trains of the river kingdom came over the road again, about half as many as before the toll was raised, and their masters say the toll is not what kept them; some of their draught beasts coughed, a dry cough, and two trains came short of beasts. No envoy of the river king came. No stranger out of the ordinary came by the road. The blue pass flower did not come up, and at the cairns the line of the grass stands where the first notch was cut, by the herders' eyes. The star dimmed three nights (months 2, 2 and 7), and the cranes went over once, in month 3.

My numbers start from the Keeper's line on Year 4, which takes my Year 4 branches this way: the stores lasted to within days of the thaw and the seed seal was whole at the sowing (my Branch A), about a hundred and fifty died over the ordinary, the old and the youngest; the loan was the nearer far house's two days and words only from the farthest (my Branch B), so the Marshal stands under question again over the farthest house; the word for the river king was given into a clerk's hand at the chancery in the second month, and no answer and no envoy came; the toll stands at a knot and a quarter the beast from month 9; no river train crossed in months 10 to 12; the dimmings are not known to be anyone's doing. The people stand at the opening of this year at about 17,550.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the ford, the gate, the hall, the riders

The columns came down the road as every year, the herds small yet, and Kessan and Idris Veld were in the winter city to receive them. Nobody argued the word for it this year. The captains said nothing and the Doorwardens said nothing, and an old woman of the low quarter said it was now simply the year's turn.

The Guest came to Marle Ford [f61] first, in the first days, on foot, with the staff and the key, as she has each autumn. The older toll watcher gave her the count from the knotted cord of who had crossed since she was last there at the close of the year: trains of other peoples as last autumn, shorter, fewer beasts to the train; no train of the river kingdom since the tenth month of last year, which she knew; no train yet that carried salt. He told it plain and was not scolded. He told her too that the word she had sent the king was given to the master of a river train and that nothing had come back, which she knew.

At the toll house table that day a master of a train of another people, not the river's, stood bound out with his beasts penned. She ate bread with him, and asked him over it, plainly, whether he had heard why the river kingdom's trains did not come this summer and what his people say of the star, and told him she asked for no more than his telling and would note it as his. The watchers stood by. They say he answered at some length and slowly, that he looked at the key, and that he stopped twice. What he said she wrote on a cord of her own. The Sarrow at the table had no part of it and I have none. (K, his telling is another people's and the Keeper's.) She did not use the Reading on him; nothing in his hand frightened her.

She walked the two miles to the gate and was bidden in by the gate guard with a single word, and at the hall that day, with Kessan, Idris Veld, the Steward of Salt and Stores, the Marshal of the Road and the Mother of the Hearth present, she spoke first of what she had left unanswered. She said that three of them had asked her in the summer, that she had been on the grass and they were not answered, and that this was her doing and not theirs. Then she answered each aloud.

To Kessan, who had asked whether he might send riders beyond the houses to learn why the river's trains did not come: yes. The terms were given in the next breath, as set down under the riders below, and she said they were his riders and his choosing and that she held him to the terms. Kessan said only that he was glad to be asked to choose. The captains, told that night at his fire, said it was the first thing she had given the Warlord that was plainly his own.

To the Steward, who had asked what to do for salt if none came before the snow: the order for salt stood for the whole winter and he need not ask her again. It was given in the open, with Idris witness, and its words are under the salt below. The Steward heard it standing. He asked, aloud, what was to be done if a master asked more than the hall would pay. She said he was to name the price, and that the hall bought only at the price he named. He said that was an order a steward could carry.

To the Mother of the Hearth, who had asked whether the households were to be told the salt would not last the winter at the ration: yes, tell them, and tell them what the Steward's number was, what the hall was doing, and why the summer's questions went unanswered. The Mother was to tell it at the hall fire on the first night of the cold, before the hearth mothers, and the Guest said she would stand beside her and say her own part of it, the part about the grass. The Mother said that was more than she had asked, and that a host that says it is short before the pot says it is the thing she had wanted a host to do for twenty years.

At the same sitting she said, unasked, how it would stand if she had to be away from the hall at any time that year, by the mountain or the road, so that no one in the dark should have to guess. While she was away Idris Veld might answer in her name any question that touched the rite, the households or the stores, and Kessan any question that touched the road, the passes or the riders. A question that touched both, or that the two would answer differently, waited for her. Idris and Kessan each said they would hold to it. The Marshal said it was plain. The Steward said nothing, and wrote it on the cord.

The riders beyond the houses. The next day, before the offices could speak of anything else, Kessan named his captain, Idris her keeper of rite, and the Marshal his riders. Kessan named a captain from the middle of the eight, a man of about forty winters who holds a column on the road, who was not at the far houses with the first captain and who is hard to anger, and said to the captain before the hall that he chose him for that. Idris named the keeper of rite who had read the river king's letter aloud to the lower column in the river speech, since he has the speech from carrying. The Marshal named four riders, the senior of his riders first, the one who had carried the far cords to the hall and the loan to the far houses, and three younger, and named a guide, an old packer of the lower columns who as a young man went with trains down the road north-east and along the river. All were named by their offices and not by name. The captain would bring the party home.

The Steward counted out of the hall's stores what the Guest had asked for, toll cloth, iron and felt, no more than the hall could spare, which was little, four laden beasts of it: felt on two, bar iron on one, toll cloth on one, with fodder for the horses. He said at the count that the iron was the hall's tools and that he gave it for salt and for nothing else, and that a man who sold the toll to get salt would be a man he remembered.

At the hall, with the five by, the Guest gave the captain her word for the king, and said it aloud so that no one should have to take it second hand. The words were: "To the one who wrote to whoever holds the ford. A word of the Sarrow went to you in the autumn of last year, by a train master, and no answer came. Since the tenth month no train of your kingdom has crossed the road. The toll was lowered at the ninth month, and the road is open to you as to any guest. I do not charge you with the absence. If it is the toll, say what would bring your trains. If it is another thing, say so. The sill is the same as it was. A claim is heard in person, at Marle Ford, and nothing is owed or refused until it is heard. I have no name to sign. They call me Guest, and Mother of the Door." The captain was to give it into the hand of the first officer of the king or clerk of the chancery who would take it, and wait for an answer as long as a guest may be asked to wait.

Her terms for the party, given to Kessan before Idris and heard by the captain: no blood, no seizing of any train or house, no taking of any thing unasked, no oath sworn in her name beyond the guest oath of the table. They say whose riders they are if asked, that it is the Sarrow of Marle Ford, that they keep the sill, and that they came as guests. They do not say her size, her place, her need or what she can do. If they are turned away they do not press and they turn back. If they are held they ask for a guest's meal and a hearing at the sill and send word by anyone who will carry it. They are back before the deep snow, or they winter where they are given guest right and send word by the first road open in the thaw. If they find a house that sells salt on the road they buy only as much as they can carry and bring it to the Steward, and they do not sell her toll to get it.

Their leave, then, is the Warlord's and the Marshal's and the First Doorwarden's, each for his own part, and hers over all of it, as above. The party set out from the winter city in the first days after the goods were tied, by the road north-east and the river, as the guide would choose it, for the river kingdom if the road runs there. I give no days for the ride. They left the farthest toll house on Pass Road, the last house of the Sarrow on their road, on the morning of the twelfth day of the month, and passed beyond it. (K, the price of the ride to that house and the whole of what lies beyond it.) At the farthest house they were fed at once by the watchmen and the keeper's household, as any party of the Marshal's is. The keeper asked whose riders and by whose leave. The captain said the Sarrow of Marle Ford's, the Warlord's riders, by the Guest's leave and the Marshal's, going beyond as guests. The keeper asked what they carried and the captain said toll cloth, iron and felt for salt. The keeper said he knew nothing of the road past his own door but what the trains say, and that he hoped they would be fed as they feed. Then he wished them the road. What anyone sees from outside at the farthest house is as it was before: the new roofs of split timber, the good horses in the pens, the smokehouses full. The senior rider, who has seen it three years running, looked at it again and said nothing.

The salt. The order the Guest gave the Steward and the Marshal, and which the Steward carried out with Idris witness, was this. The winter stock of grain, salt, fuel and dried meat is counted twice before the first snow, the Steward's count and the hearth mothers', and the ration is set from the first day at the lower of the two. The seed grain and seed barley is sealed under two marks, the Steward's and the Mother's, as before. If the stores fail the herd goes first, then the dried meat, then the seed. Salt goes first to the meat that must be put by for the winter, as much as the stock can bear, then to the hearths' bread and the pot, and last of all to the toll houses and the rite. All the salt offerings brought to the winter hall and the rite fires, which had been entered as the Guest's debt, go this winter to the common stock on the separate cord. The Guest gave the Steward her own small bag of salt, all but one handful, in the open, before Idris; the handful she keeps in case a mark must be laid again; she said she lays no blessing this year that she can help. The Steward weighed the bag in his hand, said it was less than a poor household's, and entered it. Any train that carries salt is passed without toll this winter, and the hall buys the salt in iron, felt or the toll cloth at the price the Steward names. The Marshal told this in the open at the ford, to every master of another people's train who stood there that week, so that it should be known on the road, and sent it by rider to every toll house, with the order that every house send word of any train that carries salt or grain. The grain first order stands.

The first salt train came on the seventeenth day. It was small, of another people, a dozen beasts and salt on about a third of them. The master, told the order, asked for a bar of iron and a length of felt and more to each beast's load, about twice what the Steward named. The Steward, who had come down to the ford himself, named his price again and did not move. The master took it for the first two beasts and held the third a night, and in the morning the Steward paid the master's own price for that third and said so to Idris and the Mother, who had asked him why he gave way. He said a beast of salt held back in October is a pot of salt less in March. The train crossed without toll, as the Marshal had said, and the master said at the toll house door that he had not been told the hall paid and did not toll, and that he would tell it.

The star dimmed on no night of this month. The people noted it and said nothing of it yet.

### Month 2: the counts, the second train, the star

The columns were down. The Steward and the hearth mothers of each quarter began the two counts, the Steward by tally and the mothers by the bin. The seed grain and seed barley were counted apart from the first day.

The second salt train came on the ninth day, smaller still, of another people, eight beasts with salt on about half. This master had heard at the ford of the hall's price, and asked it, and the Steward bought at it, every beast. The train crossed without toll. The master asked at the toll house whether the Sarrow were in a famine, and the older watcher said no, they were in a winter. Two trains in all had come with salt, and by the Marshal's word from every house, no third was seen on the road before the snow.

The star dimmed twice this month, on two nights some days apart, both clear, each for about an hour in the middle of the night. The households said the pair was the star saying it again for those who slept. The Doorwardens counted the two against the six blessings and were not easy about the number.

### Month 3: the two counts, the ration, the seed, the captains, the dead, the first snow

The two counts came to the Mother of the Hearth in the same hour, taken after both salt trains. The Steward gave a hard number first and said that at the full ration the stock of grain, barley and dried meat would fall short of the thaw by about ten days. The hearth mothers' count said about sixteen. The two differed by about six. The Steward explained four: he had counted the dried meat as the cull and the hunt would make it, with salt at about the old measure, and the mothers had counted only the meat hung and salted; the band's horses' hay still sat in some bins as fuel in the mothers' count; and the low granary's damp had taken a little in the rains though the roof had held. Two days he could not explain, and said so as he has for four years. The Mother set the two side by side before the Guest and said that the mothers' was nearer again, as it has been every year, and that this year the gap was small enough to be only a number.

Salt, counted the same hour. The store at the first count in autumn had stood at about a quarter of a winter's salt at the old measure. With the two trains' salt, the Guest's bag and the offerings given to the common stock, the Steward counted it at about half. The meat to be put by took the first of it. The pot and the bread were to have what was left, a thin measure, and the rite last of all. The Doorwardens of the high rite said they would use the salt at the first snow and the oath and no more; the senior Doorwarden of the lower columns said that in his columns the rite fire would take its pinch from the household's own bag or go without, and that a rite held without salt was not a lie if the salt were named aloud. Idris Veld said the sill is not the salt, and said it once.

The ration from the first snow was set at the lower of the two counts, as ordered: cut by about an eighth, the poorest hearths by about a tenth, the band's houses by about a fifth. The Mother told every hearth in the low quarter why before it was done. The seed was counted apart, the grain and the barley, and sealed under the Steward's mark and the Mother's together on a knotted cord clayed over in the old granary beside the hall, with Idris Veld witness. The Steward said aloud, flat, the order the stores would fail in, the herd first, then the dried meat, the seed last of all. The Mother said she held her half and would not give it up for anyone's asking. The Steward said that she had said so last year and that he had not asked her to.

The captains. Before the snow the Guest told the eight captains herself in the hall, before any could hear it elsewhere, that the stores were shared by hearth from the first snow, the poorest first and the war band last, and why, that this was the fourth winter, that she thanked them for three and asked for the fourth. Four said they had no argument. One said that a band that eats last four winters running is a band that will ask something of the host one day, and that he said so to the Guest because she had said she would rather a true word than a kind one. The youngest of the eight said nothing this year. Kessan told them the band would hold the passes on it, and that if it did not he would say so.

The first snow came late in the month, at its usual time. That night, at the rite fire of the winter hall, with the seat set empty as at every first snow and the households round the hearth, Idris Veld said the names of the winter's dead, hearth by hearth, as the Mother had brought them: about a hundred and fifty, the old and the youngest, fewer than the three hundred of the winter before. The Guest stood through all of it and did not sit and did not eat. The warlord's door was left unlatched, the seat set and the bowl filled and put on the sill, as the people do, and by the decisions the Guest set the seat and unlatched the door herself, beside Kessan's household, which the households who saw it did not know what to make of, a Guest who sets her own seat. I write nothing of whether she came to the table that night.

The Mother's telling. On the first night of the cold, at the hall fire, in front of the hearth mothers of every quarter, the Mother of the Hearth said the Steward's number and the mothers' number, that the ration was cut by an eighth and why, that the hall had bought salt from two trains and given all the offerings to the common stock, that salt would be short and the pot would taste of it, and that three of the offices had asked the Guest a thing in the summer and been given no answer. The Guest stood beside her, as she had said, and said her part: that in the summer she had been on the grass at the cairns and had not been where a rider could reach her, that the question had gone up and met nothing, and that this was her doing and not theirs. The Mother said afterward that she had not asked it of her, and that she was glad it was said at the fire where the old women could hear it, and not at the hall. The old women, who argue the Guest every winter, argued it the length of the night. Some said a host that says it is short before the pot says it is the Guest's way. Others said it was the hearth mothers' own custom and that the Guest had been shamed into it by a count. Nobody said she lied.

The cranes went over in flights for some days toward the plain, in the later weeks. The old women said they were counting the doors and the stores and the dead and now the riders.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the tables, the herders at the hall

The Guest stayed in the winter city through the cold and did not step. She sat at the households' tables in the low quarter, one hearth a week, chosen by the Mother of the Hearth, the first a household of one of the winter's dead. She ate at each, and the households tested the bar on lying as they have learned to, and she answered true or was silent. Kessan sat at her right at the hall table. The Doorwardens' murmur was quieter. No envoy of the river king came before the thaw.

In the same month, at her asking through the Mother and the keepers of rite, two herders of the upper columns who cut the notches came down to the hall and said, before Kessan, Idris Veld, the Steward and the Marshal, what they had said at the cairns: that the line of the grass stood where the oldest say it stood last year, no higher; that the blue flower did not come; and that a stone cut to count a line does not hold it. The elder of the two said it flat, in his felt, and the younger said that the households say the stone holds the line and the herders have told the households they are wrong and are not heard. The Guest told the hall she did not know what it meant and that she did not wish the hall to think she did. The Steward asked what a hall was to do with a line it could not hold. Kessan said the hall had heard it, and that it was more than the hall had done in forty summers. The Doorwardens of the high rite said it was the toll. The herders went back up in the thaw.

### Months 5 and 6: the stores

By the fifth month anyone who could read the Steward's cords knew what the ration was for. The Mother and the hearth mothers held the quarter cut and told every hearth again why. The seed stayed in the old granary under the clay, the cord whole, and the Steward went to look at it every week and told the Mother each time that it was whole. The herd was culled less than in the cold years because there was less herd to cull, and the meat was put by with the salt the Steward gave it first, and dried and eaten in the quarter that made it. The pot had little salt and the bread less. The households, who have tasted a thin year, said the pot tasted of the Steward's face. Kessan sent riders to hunt in the snow as in the other years, and they came back with an ibex and two deer, which were shared by hearth and not by the band.

A matter at the sill in the fifth month. A household of the middle quarter was found by the weekly count, by a hearth mother of its own street, to have kept back a bag of salt, not a great bag, from the autumn trade at the ford, under a sack of dried fruit. The nearest Doorwarden sent it to the hall door, as the custom is for a thing that touches the stores, and Idris Veld and Kessan heard it together, the household's woman and the hearth mother who found it, and the Guest sat by. The woman said she had meant it for her sick daughter's broth. Idris said by the old sill table that the bag was to go to the common stock and the household was to be fed from it with the rest, and the hearth mother who found it was to be thanked at the fire. Kessan said it was just and that the band would have been fined harder. The Mother said it was the first time anyone of the low quarter was heard at the hall door on salt, and that it would not be the last. The woman's daughter was given the broth from the common bag in front of the hearth mothers. The household is not shamed in the quarter, they say, though the quarter is not easy.

The old and the very young ate least. By the end of the sixth month the first of the old in the low quarter had died of the cold and the thin food. The Doorwardens of the high rite said again that the sharing was the Guest's law of hunger. The Mother said again that it was the households' own custom. The Steward said that the stores did not know whose custom, and that in this winter they did not know whose salt.

The star did not dim this winter.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Month 7: the thaw, the sowing, the river's trains, the star

The thaw came at its usual time and the passes opened. Branch A: the last barley went in the last days before the opening. Branch B: it had been gone some weeks.

The sowing. On the first day of sowing the Steward and the Mother broke the seed seal together at the hall door in the open, with Idris Veld witness, and counted what the sowing had. Branch A: the seal was whole and the seed as counted at the first snow, and the sowing is the full sowing, as much as the hands and the oxen of the columns can put in. The Steward said he had broken a seal he did not fear for the second spring running. Branch B: the seal was broken in the last week and the seed on that part was eaten; what was left is the sowing, short again, and thinner than the one before. The Guest told the households the count herself, in the open, by the number the Steward gave.

The Guest stayed at Marle Ford through the thaw and did not step. She told Kessan, Idris Veld and the Marshal at the hall, in the open, that she might on any day, without word, stand at a toll house door on a train day, and why: that the cords say one thing and the door may say another, and that a cord can be true and still be short of the trade, and that she wished to see what the cord does not say. She said she would not use the Breaker's Hand on any door, smokehouse or store, whatever she found. Kessan said that the far houses were the Marshal's trade and that he had never counted them, and that he said it now so that no one should think he had hidden it. Idris said she would carry it to the Doorwardens as it was said. The Marshal said it was one more thing he had not been asked, and then said he would send it to every toll house alike by his riders in the same hour, so that no house would think it was named. He did so. This was his own act, taken on himself, and he said so to Kessan. The ford, the two houses nearest it and the two farthest all had the word by the same riders at the same time, that the Guest may stand at a toll house door on any train day without word.

The river's trains. From the thaw trains of the river kingdom came over the road again, about half as many as before the toll was raised, by the ford's tally and the cords of the near houses. Their masters paid the toll, which stood at a knot and a quarter the beast, and said at the ford and at the far houses that the toll was not what kept them. The older watcher asked a master of the first train of the thaw why, since the hall had thought it was the toll. The master looked at the sill and said it was not. Some of their draught beasts were coughing, a dry cough, and the master said so plainly, as a man names a debt, and showed the older watcher one, a mule with its head down, and said it had been coughing since the high road and would not be fit for a season. Two trains in the first weeks came short of beasts and short-handed, with loads they could not carry over the passes. The ford's watchers, and later the near houses', took no toll on the loads the beasts could not carry, by the old sill custom that a load not carried is not tolled, and the masters of the two trains hired Sarrow carriers from the ford to take the rest over Stair Pass for a share in kind, which the carriers took, and the Mother of the Hearth said it was the first good news the carriers had had.

The same master told the older watcher that the master who had taken the Guest's word to the chancery in the autumn before had given it into a clerk's hand there in the second month, and that no answer was given him and he had heard none since. The older watcher told it at the hall at the next sitting.

What the watchmen said of the cough. The ford's watchers said the coughing beasts came in with the trains and should be penned below the ford where the river bank is wide and not at the near pens by the herds. The watchmen at the far houses said they had seen the cough on the river's beasts at their doors too, and that it had not come to their own horses, and that they had kept their pens apart. What the Marshal said. The Marshal asked the Guest at the ford, in front of the older watcher, whether the river's beasts that coughed were to be penned apart from the herds and the Sarrow carriers' beasts. The decisions give her no word on it, and I have none. The Marshal said at the ford that the order was his own, and had the coughing beasts penned apart on the bank below the ford, with a watcher to feed them and no Sarrow beast in the pen, and told the herders of the upper columns, who go to the high grass in the ninth month, to look at their own beasts each week. The Marshal said it was a small order and a sensible one, and that a Marshal had a road to keep and would not wait for a god to tell him to put a sick mule in the lower field. He also said, to Kessan and the Steward, that a king who keeps his trains home in the summer and sends half in the spring, with sick beasts, may not have watched the ford at all, and may have a trouble of his own. Kessan said it might be either, and that he would hear it from the captain.

What the hall said. The Marshal's reading changed from the autumn's. Kessan said that if the toll was not what kept them, then the raise the hall had given back had not been the cause either way, and that he would sit with the cords at the sitting and not unmake anything. The Steward said the cough was one more thing the hall could not turn into grain. The Mother of the Hearth said the households would hear it as the river's beasts being sick, and that she would say it that way. The Doorwardens of the high rite said it was the cold walking down, since the beasts were coughing and the flower was bare. The households said it was the salt on the ford. The captains said the king had told his masters to bring half. None said they knew.

The star dimmed once this month, in the middle of a clear night, as the passes were opening. The riders on the road told it. The people counted three for the year and said the number had fallen by more than half, and the Doorwardens, who had said it dims when a door is blessed, said no door was blessed this year and the star dimmed three times, and did not say what that meant.

### Month 8: the sitting, the farthest door

The Marshal's riders and the toll watchers brought the cords of the first trains of the thaw to the hall. The river trains were about half. The trains of other peoples were as before, short, with few beasts. The ford's tally and the far houses' cords agreed on the half. The Steward read the cords aloud.

The three sat with the cords, as last year: Kessan, Idris Veld and the Guest, with the Steward, the Marshal and the Mother called in to read knots and be heard. Kessan spoke first. He said the toll stood at a knot and a quarter the beast and that the masters had said before the whole ford that it was not what kept them, and that he would not raise it on half the trains for the stores, since a raise on half the trains came to less than the old toll on all of them had, and the cords showed it. Idris said the sill takes one toll and not two, that the cords did not say why the trains fell and the masters said it was not the toll, and that she would have the toll stand where it stood. The Steward said the stores did not stand on the toll this year, they stood on salt and on the seed. Kessan said that the salt order, which was the Guest's for the winter, ended with the thaw, and that salt was still short, and that he and Idris would keep it to the first snow, so that a master who carried salt at any time of the year would pass free, until a train came with more than the hall could buy. Idris said the sill would have it so. The Marshal sent the word to every house by rider with the reason. The loan: the Steward said that the nearer far house's two days were owed and that he had repaid them in kind from the first grain toll of the thaw, and that the farthest house had sent words only and was owed nothing, and that he had nothing on any cord from it that was not the cord's own. The Guest spoke at the table. What she said is hers. (K, not in my record. If her word differs from the mortals' settlement, the Keeper settles it; what the two mortals settled stands as written.)

The farthest door. On a train day in the second half of the month, on the nineteenth day, at first light, the Guest came down to the crossing at Marle Ford, as she has each first morning, with the staff and the key, and stood in it. The older watcher was at his post. He saw her stand in the water with her hands open and speak, and he did not hear what she said. Then she was not in the crossing. He said to the younger, who had come out, that she went into the ford and did not come out the far bank, and the younger ran for the hall, since a thing like that cannot be kept. At the hall Kessan, Idris Veld and the Marshal heard it at the same table and said it was what she had said she might do. Kessan sent two of the sworn band to stand at the ford house that night with the watchers, saying it was his road and the ford was a gate. The older watcher kept the ford that night as he keeps it every night, and said at the hall in the morning that nothing came by the ford that he did not count. (K, what a power did in the ford and what it showed the watcher. Whether the ford stood open that night to the Guest is the Keeper's.)

At the farthest toll house on Pass Road, at the hour after first light, a train stood at the door. (K, whose train, what it carried, and everything the Guest saw that day.) The keeper of the house, a man of the age to have grandchildren, who had been told by the Marshal's riders in the seventh month what every keeper was told, was at the door with his watchmen, counting the train in the way they count it, by the beast and the load and the head, with the cord. A tall woman who stoops stood in the threshold, with a staff and a key, where a moment before there had been nothing but the road. His wife, who had come to the door with a pot, set it down on the step. The younger of the two watchmen took a step back and the older did not. The masters of the train, were silent and some of them went to their beasts. (K, what the masters did. I give only what the keeper's household did and said.)

The keeper kept his hands open. The Guest told him, as she had said she would, to his face and the moment she stood in his door: that she had come to see with her own eyes what is carried in at his door on a train day, that she accused no man, that she asked nothing but to stand in his threshold and count, that she would stay the day, and that if he bade her to his table she would eat knowing that she could not speak a lie until she rose from it. He said it was a house of the Sarrow and that a guest might stand in its door unasked, as he had said the year before, and that she was welcome in it. He said the cord of the house had nothing in it the knots did not say, and that she might count against it. He said that for a house to keep a train day it needed its watchmen at their tasks, and that he would have her stand where she saw best, and that he would not hurry a master for her. She said she wished it kept as any train day, and he said it would be kept so.

He bade her to his table at midday, with his wife, his younger son's wife and her two children, and the older watchman, as a house sits for a guest at the end of a morning. The keeper, as host, broke the bread first and ate first, and the Guest ate. The younger of the two children, a girl of about six, asked her what she had had for her dinner, which is a game the children of the low quarter play and the road's children have heard, and the Guest answered true or was silent. What her answer was is hers. The keeper's wife told the older watchman afterward that she had not been afraid and that her hands had said otherwise.

She asked him at the table what she had said she would ask: whose goods are carried in at his door, by what trains, and for how long they stay. The keeper answered as the house answers. The goods are the trains' own, they come over the road and go over it, a night's feeding is given to any master who asks it and the smokehouse is for that feeding, and the cord holds the toll by the beast. He said the trains of the river complained loudly, as before, and that none turned back at his door and none had spoken to him of another road, and that a toll house cannot count what it does not see. He said it with no hurry. (K, whether the keeper's answer is the whole of it. What she saw at the door and in the pens, and what the cord for the same day held against what she counted, are the Keeper's. I write the keeper's answer as the record gives it to him and no more.)

She stayed the day in sight of the door, as she had said, and the household said afterward that she looked at everything and said nothing, that she did not touch any door or store, and that once her hand moved and she said aloud that it was moving and put it down. (K, the Breaker's Hand: not used. What her hand did and why she said so aloud is the Keeper's.) At dusk the keeper's household set a bed for her by the fire, as a guest's, and she did not lie in it. At first light the next day, after a full day, she stood in the threshold, thanked the keeper for the bread, and was not in it. The keeper's wife said she was in the door and then she was not, and that the bed had not been touched. (K, what a power showed, and whether she stepped back through that door to the ford. The Keeper rules whether the second step from a blessed threshold that she holds stands the ford open a second night, as she asked.)

When she came back she told what she found at the hall table to Kessan, Idris Veld and the Marshal together, before she said it to anyone else, as she had said she would. What she said is hers and not in my record. What the mortals did turns on it. (K.)

Branch D1: what she found is that the door and the cord agree, or that what she saw does not call for a sill. Nothing is laid before the sill. The Marshal's standing, which was under question over the farthest house, is eased a little and no more: the riders say he was named as honest in the far houses and was right, and the low quarter says the roofs were mended by carpenters after all. Kessan says to his captains that he never counted the far houses and has now been shown he need not. The keeper of the farthest house is told by the Marshal's rider that the Guest thanks him for the bread. The Doorwardens say the Guest saw what the cord does not say and that the cord had nothing more to say.

Branch D2: what she found is that there is a trade at the door that the cord does not say. She tells the three, and Idris Veld and Kessan together set it for the sill, as the Guest has said it would be, with the keeper heard and her view given last. The keeper is bidden down from the farthest house by the Marshal's rider, with the Guest's word that he is a guest of the sill until heard, and comes on foot with one of his watchmen. He is heard at the hall door, standing, with his hands open, and says, as the record gives him to say, that it is a house of the Sarrow, that a master leaves what he leaves for the roof and the pen and the smokehouse as a guest's gift and not as toll, that the cord counted the toll and nothing else, that the Marshal's own order said to feed a traveler a night and the house fed him, and that nothing he took was taken from the hall's toll. Idris says the sill takes one toll and not two, that a guest's gift is the host's to keep only if it is the house's and not the hall's, and that she would price it by the old sill table and not by a man's mouth. Kessan says the toll is the host's and that whatever the house took in the host's name is the host's, and that he will take the good horses in the pens as the hall's by toll in kind, since the watchmen said the horses were toll kept for the hall and the riders. They agree on the price. The keeper is fed at the sill as a guest until the judgment and then pays by the old table, and goes back to the farthest house under a second keeper whom the Marshal names from his riders, or is kept from it, as the Keeper sets the size of what was found. If it is small, he keeps his house under the second keeper and the horses are the hall's. If it is large, he is kept from the house and the second keeper holds it, and the household goes with him or stays by the hall's leave. The Marshal asks before the five whether he is to be relieved of the road; Kessan says the road needs him; Idris says he walked the farthest house himself last year without being told; neither says what the Guest thinks. The Guest gives her view last, and it is hers. (K, whether it comes to a sill at all, and the size of what was found.) The captains of the band say the Lintel took the horses and the Doorwardens say the sill took the keeper; the low quarter says it was the roofs after all.

In either branch, what anyone sees from outside at the two farthest houses is what was there before: new roofs, good horses, full smokehouses, except that in Branch D2 the horses of the farthest house are the hall's.

### Month 9: the columns go up

The columns went up with the herds to the high grass. Kessan led the going up, as he has for fifteen years, and was glad to. The herds were small. The herders of the upper columns, told by the Marshal to look at their beasts each week, did so and told the keepers of rite what they saw. The Guest did not go up with them. She stayed within two days of the hall, at the ford, the gate, Stair Pass and Far Notch with the Marshal, where trains cross, as she had said. In the Marshal's riders' telling she was seen at Stair Pass on three train days, sitting with the toll watchers at their table, and said nothing and was fed.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Months 10 and 11: the high grass, the cairns, the questions

The high grass was ordinary this summer. The herds fattened as they could. The herders put no new beast to the grass above where they had put them last year, by the notches. The river's trains crossed through the summer, about half as many as before the raise, and the carriers who are Sarrow carried for the short-handed ones for a share. The pens below the ford held coughing river beasts through the heat, apart. No cough was brought to the Marshal from the Sarrow's own herds by any herder of the upper columns or lower, by the end of the eleventh month. (K, if the cough is to come to the Sarrow's beasts, it is the Keeper's.)

No envoy of the river king came. No stranger out of the ordinary came by the road. The Marshal's standing order brought the Guest a few ordinary travelers in the course of the summer, a boy walking to kin with a potter's tools, a woman carrying a loom on her back, an old man who sang at the sill, and a pair of carters from the plain. Each was fed a night and sent on. None was placed.

In the eleventh month, at midsummer, the Guest went up to the cairns once, as she had said, and came down in a week. Before she left, at the hall, aloud and before the five, she said again the standing answers: Idris Veld might answer in her name any question that touched the rite, the households or the stores, and Kessan any question that touched the road, the passes or the riders, and a question that touched both or that the two would answer differently would wait for her.

At the old cutting at the high gates the blue pass flower was not there again. The herders of the upper columns, at the two cairns, said what the line had done. They said it stood where the first notch was cut, by their eyes, at both. She asked what the line had done since she had seen it, and they told her. She told them what she found, as she told them last year, that she did not know what it meant. A herder said to her, in front of the fire, that it was the first time a god had come up twice to a stone and said she did not know, and that he did not know whether to be glad. The oldest woman of the upper columns said a line that stands two years has gone up eighty. The herders cut no new notch at midsummer for the Guest, but the herders say they cut a notch at the first snow of the heights in the twelfth month at both cairns, and the line stood.

The questions while she was up. Three came to the hall in that week.

The first touched the households and the stores. The Mother of the Hearth, by a keeper of rite, asked whether the households were to be told that the Steward's tally showed the salt would not reach the first snow at the ration, and that the thin pot of the winter was to be thinner. It went to Idris Veld under the standing answers. Idris said in the Guest's name that the Mother was to tell it, plainly, with the Steward's number, as at the first snow of last year, and the Mother did, in the low quarter, hearth by hearth. She said afterward that she had said it herself and that Idris had said it in the Guest's name and that it was the same thing said twice.

The second touched the road. A rider of the Marshal's, by Kessan, asked whether the herds of the upper columns might stay a week longer at the high grass, since the lower grass of the winter valley had been grazed hard by the carriers' beasts and was slow. Kessan answered it under the standing answers, that they might, to the first snow of the heights and no later, and that the order to be down by first snow stood.

The third touched both. A rider of the ford's watchers, by the Marshal, asked whether a master of a river train, who had come with coughing beasts and would not leave them penned apart from his own men because he said they were all he had, was to be turned from the sill. Kessan, asked, said that the beasts were to stay below the ford and the man was to be fed at the toll house and sent on without his beasts, since the herds were the host's and a sill that feeds a man does not owe his mule a pen by the herds. Idris, asked, said that the sill turns no guest from the door and that a guest's beast is part of the guest. The two did not quarrel, and said so to each other, and said the question was for the Guest. It waited. The Marshal, who was at the ford, took it on himself: he had the master fed at the toll house, the beasts penned below the ford with the others, and a Sarrow watcher put to feed them with a cloth over his mouth, and he told the master he did it by his own order and not the Guest's, and told Kessan and Idris the same. The master said it was a courteous order. The question stayed pending until she came down, and what she said when she was told is hers.

The Guest came down from the cairns in a week, as she had said, and was at the ford before the snow. She laid no seventh blessing. She did not step again this year.

The star dimmed on no night of these months. The herders high in the grass said it was resting again. The Doorwardens said it had dimmed three times in a year and that the Guest had blessed no door in the year, and did not say what followed.

### Month 12: the notch, the order to come down

The first snow of the heights lay on the cutting in the twelfth month, a week before the columns came down. The herders cut the notch at both cairns and went to the keeper of rite and told him what the line had done: it stood where it stood, at the first notch, no higher, by their eyes. The oldest man said that a line that stands two years has gone up eighty, and the oldest woman said she would be gone before anyone said so. They brought the word down.

Orders went out for the year's end by horn and smoke, by the Marshal's riders and by the keepers of rite at every column's fire: every column down at the winter city by first snow, the Marshal to confirm each by rider. The ford, the hall door, the city gate, Stair Pass, Far Notch and the farthest door are the six, and the Guest took the sill at Marle Ford again before the snow. I write nothing of her words there.

The harvest. Branch A: the full sowing gave an ordinary harvest in an ordinary year, brought in by every hand in the last weeks of summer, and laid up at the winter hall at about what a sound year gives, as last year. Branch B: the short sowing gave a thin harvest, and the winter hall laid up less than the year before.

The riders, in the summer. Branch R1: the party's word is in, and the spring and summer have been shaped by what it was. Kessan has not sent another party. Branch R2, word came by a master in spring or not at all; if not at all, no party has been seen and no word of it, and the captain's household and the riders' households say they will set the empty seat for them at the next first snow, as for the Guest. (K, the whole of the riders.)

## What the year did to the people

The stores held or failed by the branch above, and the people are thin in both. The salt was bought, a little, from two small trains at a price the Steward set and gave way on once, and the Guest's bag and the offerings went to the common stock; the pot was thin of it all winter. The hall told the households before the pot told them. The seed was sealed under two marks again. Kessan sent riders beyond the houses, as guests, with the Guest's word for the king. The river's trains came back at half, with sick beasts, and said the toll was not what kept them. The Guest stood at the farthest door on a train day, unwarned, and was fed at the keeper's table. The herders were heard at the hall, and the line of the grass stood a second year. The star dimmed three nights, fewer than last year and the first fewer for three years. The cranes went over.

The quarrel between the Warlord and the Doorwardens stands with a king's letter laid across it, a folded word gone after it, a party of the Warlord's riders and a keeper of rite gone beyond the houses together, and the salt between them. Kessan sent the riders and Idris sent her keeper, and each says it was the other's idea to go together. The households think it was the Guest's.
