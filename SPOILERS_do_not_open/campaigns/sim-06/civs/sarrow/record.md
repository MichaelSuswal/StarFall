# People: the Sarrow

The agreed record of god-thresholds's people, written by the Keeper on creation Night 3a, 2026-11-24, from the god's own description as confirmed in two rounds. Quoted blocks are the god's words. This record holds what the people are, know and believe; what they believe of their past may be wrong.

Named by their god: the Sarrow, which they say means those who keep the sill. Their winter home is unnamed (unnamed winter city [f60]) until a mortal names it in play.

## Who they are
> Mortal folk of the common kind: broad, weathered, quick to cold and slow to warm, dark of hair and eye, good at being hard to see on a hillside. They are a host of herders, carriers and guides who live in the high broken country and who hold, by long custom, the passes and fords they move through. Their days run with the season: the herds up to the high grass in summer, down by the first snow, the road and the war band between, and the winter spent behind walls at the one place they keep. What fills a day is feeding the herd, mending harness and roof, carrying other peoples' goods for a share, standing the watch at a crossing, and keeping the fire and the table. This is my wish for them and their own belief about themselves. The world decides.

## How many
About 18,000, with a winter city (unnamed winter city [f60]) holding about 4,000 in the cold months and the rest wintering in the valley round it. Larger than the usual number; not priced against a weakness. The practical costs agreed on creation Night 3a, which the Keeper applies in play: (1) food and fuel: the country does not feed this many from its own grass and barley; they live in part on tolls in kind and on carrying, a closed pass, a bad summer or a lost season of carrying is felt in the stores sooner than a smaller people would feel it, and the winter ground must hold fodder and fuel for all from first snow to thaw; (2) moving: the whole people with its old, its young and its herds goes at about half a walker's pace, in columns stretched over days of road, and cannot turn or gather quickly; (3) growth: slow, and a hard winter takes more than it gives back for some years; (4) grass: the herds strip a valley, so the host cannot stay long in one place outside the winter ground.

## How they live
> They are a close band on the move. From the thaw until the first snow the host is spread in columns along the road between the high grass and the winter ground, each column with its herds, its captain and its keeper of rite. At the first snow they come together in the winter city and shut the gates against the cold, and the passes are theirs to hold. In country like this they live by the pass and the ford: they know where the river can be crossed and when, they cut steps where the rock needs them, they carry loads where mules cannot go, and they take a toll in kind for safe passage, which they give. They eat meat, curd, barley and dried fruit, they keep goats, hardy cattle and horses, they weave wool and felt, they work wood and stone, they smelt and forge iron in the old way, bloomery iron, no steel. They keep count by the moon and by knots on a cord, and they reckon a year from first snow to first snow.

## Their code
> They honor the guest and the host. A stranger who comes to a Sarrow hearth is fed, seated, and left in peace until he has eaten and slept, and no blood is spilled at the sill. They honor a toll fairly paid, and a word given at a threshold. The worst of them would still not break the sill: not strike a guest at the hearth, not turn a freezing traveler out into the snow to die, not sell a guest. These are oaths. An oath can be broken, and some will break it; I do not ask for them to be unable to.

## Skills and culture
> Ordinary and free. They are good guides, packers, stonecutters and cut-timber builders, and know their country's weather. They keep a table custom: the host breaks bread first and eats first, to show the food is clean. They keep the empty seat at the table and the unlatched door at first snow, for the Guest, as set out below. They sing at the sill when someone crosses it. They teach their children the names of fords and passes as other peoples teach the names of kin. The women hold the hearths and the stores; the men and women alike may carry the spear. They are not gentle, and they are not cruel for sport.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. The god proposed no edge and none was priced; no weakness is owed. The habits and prides above are tempers, able to fail. The force under Helpers is ordinary by the measure in the ruling ledger (L-0030).

## Their magic
Salt wives, a few among all the Sarrow: one lays a line of salt across a sleeping place, a door, a ford edge or a fold and speaks the sill rite; while the line holds and she is within a day's walk, twenty miles, she wakes when anything crosses it and knows only that it was crossed and by how many. She does not sleep deep while any line holds, goes thin and grey, and ages faster the more lines she holds. Ordinary in this world; no edge; no part of the god's powers. No one can make another by telling.

## What they believe about gods
> They say that gods are guests who have not yet been fed. A god is one who comes to the door at the end of the year, in a shape a little wrong, and the house is judged by what it does. They say a Guest is expected at the first snow, a tall woman, stooping, some say, with a key; others say a man, or a wind. They set the empty seat and leave the door of the warlord's house unlatched through the first night of snow, and fill a bowl and leave it on the sill. They do not agree on what the Guest is, and the old women argue it every winter. They say a house that turns the Guest away is cursed to a bad winter. This is their belief, as I wish it.

## Rule and worship
> Rule: as my lean, a warlord. A mortal ruler under me rules by force through the war band and its captains, and one ruler answers to me and rules for me. I add nothing to this and I give no reason to depart.
> Worship: as my lean, a priesthood. The keepers of rite, called Doorwardens, have standing among the people and keep the sill rite, the salt, the oaths and the table law. Offerings are brought to the rite fire at each camp and to the winter hall: bread, salt, a measure of grain, a bowl of milk. There is no tithe by law. They do not pray to a god by name: they set the table and open the door, and the rite is the worship.

## Hierarchy
- The Warlord, Kessan, called the Lintel: the host and the road, the sworn band of about three hundred and sixty under eight captains, the passes; about three thousand five hundred more carry a spear when the host calls.
- The First Doorwarden, Idris Veld: the rite, the table law, the sill courts and the keepers of rite (Doorwardens), who carry word between the columns.
- The Steward of Salt and Stores: granaries, salt, winter stock.
- The Marshal of the Road: scouts, guides, riders, and the toll houses at fords and passes; the riders and the toll watch are keepers of a trade, posted in ones and twos.
- The Mother of the Hearth: speaks for the households.
- Disputes: the nearest Doorwarden at the sill, confirmed by the column's captain; the hard ones to the First Doorwarden and the Warlord together.

## Helpers
Served from landing (creation Night 3a, 2026-11-24, the god's own choice): the offices under Hierarchy know the god on sight for the one they awaited and for whose they are, and serve it from the first night. Knowing whose they are is who the office holders are, never what they will do; each can fail the god in play.

The offices are those under Hierarchy. Orders travel by horn and smoke for the plain words, by the Marshal's riders along the road, a day's ride between relays, and by the keepers of rite to every column's rite fire.

## How the god meets them
They are waiting. They set an empty seat and leave the Warlord's door unlatched on the first night of snow for a Guest, a tall woman who stoops, with a key, and the households have been taught to bid in the first one who stoops at the door. They know the god on sight for that Guest and for whose they are. They know no name for it and not what it can do. Served from landing.

## Neighbors and their trouble
Neighbors: the Aldermark [r10], whose traders pay their tolls and resent them; plainsfolk of the Ansel Plain [r04]. Their trouble: carrying over the passes has thinned for some years and the tolls with it, so the stores are tighter each winter; the Warlord and the Doorwardens each think the other holds too much; and the high grass was poor last summer.

## Named mortals
Kessan, called the Lintel, the Warlord: fifty winters, heavy, scarred, careful with his word, short in temper, eleven years over the war band; respected, not much loved; afraid he will not be ready. Idris Veld, the First Doorwarden: an old woman, patient, shrewd, well liked, thirty years in the rite. Each is who they are, never what they will do.
