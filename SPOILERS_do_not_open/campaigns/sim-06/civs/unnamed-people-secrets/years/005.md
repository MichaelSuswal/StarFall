# Year 5: the unnamed people of god-secrets

Written by the civ agent. The year opens in autumn. Month 1 is the first month of autumn, month 4 the first of winter, month 7 the first of spring, month 10 the first of summer. Months run about thirty days. The people do not know the stranger is a god and have no name for themselves or the town. Office holders are filed by office. The weather was ordinary: the harvest ordinary, the winter wet, the river open at its usual time. Where the year turns on something that is not the people's to settle, it is written both ways, briefly, and set out in the report. No moment is held; the whole year is written.

Where the Keeper's line on Year 4 differs from my Year 4 file, the Keeper's line stands. The stranger went on from the landing at the river's head by the drovers' track onto the plain and was back on the twentieth day of month 11, before the day he named. The town's talk of the dim nights of months 10 and 11 had him on the river; he was on the plain, away from the town, and the town knew only that he was away. The dredging stands as I wrote it. The dimmings of the star are not known to be anyone's doing.

## How the year opened

The stranger came back on the twentieth day of month 11, two days before the day he had named to the Clerk, and went first to the Clerk's house and gave him the day he came and the day he said, as he had told him he would. The stool at the foot of the table had stood empty for three sittings and the Boat Master had asked after him twice. He walked the store with the Warden the day after, and the far run stood behind its four marks until it was drawn out in months 11 and 12. The old stock the Warden carries into the new harvest is 6,860 sacks, all in the near bins, by the count at the door. The far run stands empty and swept. The count has been cried at the Warden's stone three times, and the town speaks of it as its own.

The town has had a second winter of talk about the sky, and it has had the cellars, and it has had the dredging paid for in part by debt. The Speaker is the head of the tanners' house, who lent the boat houses their levy shares. The second tallies stand.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**Month 1, the third day, in the evening.** The carts are along the granary yard wall, the reaping done and the first sack to go in at first light. The stranger comes to the yard at the end of the afternoon. He has sent no runner this year; he says to the watchman at the yard gate that he is there to speak to the Warden, and the watchman fetches him. The Warden says the Clerk may stand by, and the Clerk, who has been sent for by the Warden's own boy and not by anyone else, comes.

The stranger says, plainly and not at length, that the count is the Warden's this year and he will not stand at the door. He says he asks nothing new. He asks only that it be made as it was made the last two autumns: counted aloud at the door; the far run filled first by the sack, with a tally-keeper of each of the two families cutting a stick at the bins; the near bins counted the same way; the whole store squared from the first sack. He asks the Clerk to set this year's sticks beside the last two and the old roll. He asks that the Crier cry the closing count at market as the Warden's, and that the Warden take it to the council himself. He says he will not come to the yard on the days of the carrying in, and that he will be on the bench in the market street, where anyone who wants him can find him.

The Warden hears him to the end. He says that for four autumns the man has sat on a bench by the door and said nothing, and that he is now to sit on a bench further off and say nothing, and that the Warden will count the same. He asks why the man tells him this at all, the evening before, and not the morning. The stranger says that a count made three times by the town is the town's, and that a custom that wants him at the door is his and not theirs, and that he would sooner see which it is. The Warden is quiet a while. He says that is a man asking to be shown he is not needed, and that it is not a trader's reason, and that he does not know what to call it. He says he will not take it as a kindness. The stranger says it is not meant as one. The Warden says he will do it as he has done it and in his own way, and that if the count comes right it will be his, and if it comes wrong it will be his, and the man may sit where he likes.

The Clerk says that setting sticks side by side is his office and wants no asking, and that this year he will set four sets side by side, the roll's, the Year 3 autumn's, the Year 4 autumn's and this one, since the roll no longer carries a bin the Warden has not walked and the third column is his to keep. The Crier, told by the Clerk the next morning, says he will cry what the Warden gives him.

The Warden does not go himself to the two heads this year. He sends his sister's son, now about seventeen, to each door at dusk with a word, that the keepers are wanted at the bins at first light on the fourth. The heads send the same four as last year. The cooper head sends the widow, his second tally keeper, to the far run, and his nephew to the near bins. The east fields head sends the grandson to the far run and the younger grandson to the near bins. Neither head says anything to the boy. Neither has spoken to the stranger, and the stranger does not go near either.

**Month 1, the fourth to the twenty-sixth day.** The carrying in, twenty-three days of cart work. The stranger is on the market bench from first light on the first day, with his scales on his belt and nothing in his hands, and is there, or at the gate, or at a stall, on every day of the carrying in. He does not go to the yard. The Warden stands at the door himself in the place where the stranger's bench was, with the eight sack-counters crying every sack and a stick each, and the Clerk's boy marking the cry on the Clerk's stick. Two witnesses stand by turns, a farming head and a boat headman. The far run is filled first. The widow and the east fields grandson cut their own sticks at every bin. The far run takes 3,460 sacks by the evening of the sixth day, which is its roll entry. The widow's stick agrees with the Warden's to the sack on every day and she says nothing at the bin but the count. The east fields grandson's stick is right every day this year, and he says to the boat headman on the fifth day, loud enough to be heard, that he has learned to count in his head and not on his fingers. In the near bins the younger grandson is out by one sack on the eleventh day and the Warden's counters set him right; the cooper nephew, who was corrected twice last year, is not corrected.

On the ninth day a carter, passing the market bench with an empty cart, stops and asks the stranger whether the Counter has left the door because the count is wrong or because it is right. The stranger says he does not know, that he is not at the door, and that it is the Warden's count. The carter says that is a thing to say, and drives on. On the fourteenth and fifteenth days rain stops the carts; the count resumes on the sixteenth with the sticks stood in the Warden's own house in between. Nothing goes in uncounted. On the seventeenth day a cart of the west fields house reaches the yard gate after the counters have stood down for the night, and the Warden's lad sends it back to stand till morning; it goes in on the eighteenth with the first cart.

The sacks counted in over the twenty-three days come to 31,760. The harvest is ordinary. The lads' sticks at the damp corner stand as before.

**Month 1, the twenty-seventh day: the closing count.** The Warden has the whole store counted aloud by parties, the Clerk marking and two witnesses at his elbow, from the door to the far wall. It reaches the far wall and squares. The near bins stand at 35,160 sacks (the 6,860 of old stock and 28,300 of the new), the far run at 3,460. The whole store is 38,620. The Warden has the far run sealed with wax by himself, the Clerk and both keepers, as before, and orders the near bins drawn first and the far run last, by the sack, with both keepers present. He has the count on a stick in his own mark. The count has been made the same way as the last two autumns, and nothing in it was changed because the stranger was not at the door.

**Month 2, the fourth day: the council.** At the first sitting after the harvest is in, the Speaker, the head of the tanners' house, calls the Warden. The Warden stands and gives the count himself: the store at 38,620, the far run counted in by the sack and sealed, the near bins counted by the same stick. He says he has nothing to add to what he said in other years, and sits. The Clerk lays four sets of sticks side by side, the roll's and the three autumns', and says they agree to a few sacks in the whole.

A boat headman asks the stranger, from his stool at the foot, what he makes of the new count against the old. The stranger says what the sticks say and whose sticks they are: the Warden's crew's, the four keepers', the Clerk's. The east fields head rises, old and slower than he was, and says once more that his house was robbed and that the carters of the Warden's uncle's day know it, and that he will not live to see it found. The Speaker says the table has the sticks and moves on. No one seconds anything. The cooper head looks at his hands.

The table turns then to the river. The Clerk has the levy's accounts to read, as at every first sitting, and a farming head says across the table that the farming houses paid for the boat houses' river, and a boat headman says the boat houses paid for it with silver they had to borrow from this table. The Speaker does not look up. A farming head asks the stranger whether he has a figure. The stranger says only what the Clerk's sticks hold: that the bar was dredged in the dry season by the council's own rule, that it took fourteen hundred and twenty days of hired work and the hire of two boats, and that the groundings of the three springs of soundings cost about two hundred and thirty worker-days of hired labor between them. He says he does not know who ought to bear it. He says a town that argues from one book argues shorter than a town that argues from two. The table is quiet at that, and the cooper head and the Speaker are the two who do not look at one another. The stranger asks the Speaker, through the Clerk, that the Clerk set the dredging's cost, the groundings' cost and who paid each onto one stick for the council, and says he carries no figure of his own beyond what the Clerk has. The Speaker says the table has heard the man's liking for one book, and orders it. The Clerk says he will lay it at the next sitting.

**Month 2, the sixth day: the market.** The Crier cries the closing count at the Warden's stone with the Warden at its foot: the near bins, the far run by itself, and the whole store, 38,620. The market hears it as it hears the weather. A woman of the fish stalls says the count came right without the Counter at the door. The old woman at the tanners' end says it came right because he was not at it, and her son does not hush her.

**Month 2, the eighth day: the Clerk's house.** The stranger goes to the Clerk's house at the end of the afternoon and sits with him over a cup, as idle talk. He says that the star dimmed seven times last year and twenty-one since he came, and that the town sets cups on the river steps and calls it manners. He asks whether the Clerk would care to cut a stick for each night, with the month, the hour the blue deepened and the hour it passed, kept by the night watch on the walls, who are awake. He says he does not ask it for his own sake, that a count the town keeps for itself tells the town what the star does and does not tell it that he does it, and that he does not know what it is. The Clerk, dry, asks whether the man means he does not know that it is not himself. The stranger says he does not know that either. The Clerk says that is the third time he has heard the answer and that it has not changed, and that he will cut a stick for each night as the watch gives him the hour, and that the sticks will be the council's and not the man's. The stranger asks whether the Gate Warden would have the watch on the wall note the hour on the nights it dims, and says that if the Clerk says no he will not ask again this year.

**Month 2, the ninth and tenth days.** The Clerk goes to the Gate Warden at the gate, with his boy, and puts it. The Gate Warden says the watch is awake on those nights in any case and a notch on a candle costs nothing, and that the watch will mark the hour the blue deepens and the hour it clears, by the candle the watch keeps on the wall and the changing of the watch. He says the watch will mark also whether the Counter is seen in the town on such a night, since a count should be whole, and that he does so on his own account and not for the man. The Clerk carries this back. The stranger says that is the Gate Warden's to do.

**Month 2, the eleventh night: the star.** The first of the year and the twenty-second since the stranger came. The blue deepens about an hour, the star stands dim about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it is as before. The watch on the walls marks the hours on a stick for the first time. The Gate Warden's watch captain notes that the Counter is on the granary yard bench from the first deepening to the clearing, looking up as everyone does, and saying nothing. The Crier says at market in the morning that it is the first of the year.

**Month 2, the twenty-fifth night: the star.** The second of the year and the twenty-third since. The watch marks it. The Counter is seen on the market bench at the deepening by the fish stall woman, who has her boy up, and later in the yard. The marks on the two sticks agree with each other to within the width of a finger of candle, and the middle of the dim falls on the turn of the middle watch on both. The bone-setter at the marsh edge says nothing of her hands; a woman who finds strayed beasts says hers were dull for a day. They do not say it together.

**Month 2, the twenty-eighth day: the sitting.** The Clerk lays the one stick of the river on the table: the dredging, fourteen hundred and twenty worker-days and two boats; the toll chest, five hundred and twenty days of it, paid in coppers by loaded boats and carts at the gates, three parts in five by the boats and two in five by the carts; the levy, nine hundred days and the hire, paid by the farming houses at about three hundred and forty days in grain and carting days, and by the boat houses at about five hundred and sixty days, of which about a hundred and sixty in carriage days and about four hundred in silver. On the stick, in the Clerk's mark, stands what was said at the table in month 9 of last year and no more: that the boat houses' silver was lent them against next season's carriage by the tanners' house and the cooper house. The Clerk says that by the sticks the boat houses paid about eight hundred and ninety days of the fourteen hundred and twenty and the farming houses about five hundred and thirty, and that the groundings' cost fell about two parts in three on the boat houses and one in three on the cargoes of farming houses.

The argument runs most of the morning. The farming heads say a thing borrowed is not a thing paid until it is repaid, and that the boat houses have paid in promises. The boat headmen say that they carry the lenders' hides and barrels next season for less than the market's price and that this is a payment too, and that the stick shows the boat houses bore the larger share, which is more than the farming houses have said in four years. The Speaker says the stick is the Clerk's and that his house lent at a fair price. A boat headman says it is a fair price today. The Speaker lets that stand. No motion is put. The stranger says nothing at the sitting and is asked nothing.

The Gate Warden tells the table that the watch has cut two sticks of the star, with the hours, and that the Clerk has them. The Speaker says the table does not sit on the sky, and that it will hear the Clerk's sticks, the watch's, when the Clerk wishes to lay them.

**Month 3.** The first frost. The cranes go over in flights for some days toward the plain, as they do. The old say the star dims when the cranes go over, and this year it does not. The old woman at the tanners' end says the star keeps away from a thing that is being watched. The Crier cries the two nights of the year and the watch's sticks, between the price of salt and the day of the next market. The cups on the river steps are fewer than last year, and the fish stall woman says it is because the cups did what was wanted. The lads' sticks at the damp corner go on.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The stranger stays inside the walls and talks, and sits on the granary bench, at the gate and on the market bench as before. He asks the Gate Warden for nothing and does not change the figure he gave for the watch's rations. He does not go into the cellars. No star night falls in the winter.

**Month 4.** Nothing of note beyond the market. A farming head asks at the end-of-month sitting whether the cranes and the stars are done, and is told by the Speaker that the table does not sit on the sky.

**Month 5.** The winter fever comes through the narrow streets near the river, as in other winters, heavier among the small than among the old. About two hundred and ten of the small ones take it, and twenty-three of them die; about a hundred and eighty die in the year in all. The hedge-workers are busy and two go to bed after. The wet winter fills the lowest cellars again, and the second-lowest, and the Clerk's boy carries the barrels up for the fourth time. The mothers of the boat families' row and the tanners' end say that the small ones who slept over the wet cellars took it worst, and the market says it, and does not know why.

**Month 6, the fifth day.** After the water has gone down in the cellars, the stranger goes to the Clerk of the Tally in his house, and then to the Boat Master on the quay, and asks each as a trader asking after his own trade. He asks whether the Clerk would set down by household how many cellars took water this winter and in the three before, and how many of the small ones took the fever, and in which streets. He asks for the count and not for a cause. The Clerk says that is a count he has half already, in his boy's carrying of barrels, and that he will have the rest by going door to door. The Boat Master says his crews' wives know the boat families' row better than the Clerk's boy does and that his runners will go with the boy. The Boat Master asks what the figures are for, and is told that a town that talks all winter should have its figures when it talks, and that he may take it or leave it. He takes it. The stranger says that he does not go into the cellars and will not, and that he will bring the count to the council only if he is asked, and will say whose count it is.

**Month 6, the seventh to the twenty-sixth day.** The Clerk's boy and two of the Boat Master's runners go door to door in the three lowest streets and the second-lowest rows, and the hedge-workers and the bone-setter give their counts of the fevers from memory. The Clerk sets the counts down by household, in four winters.

By the Clerk's sticks: the cellars that took water this winter number thirty-six households, against thirty-one last winter, twenty-four the winter before and nineteen the winter before that. Fourteen of the thirty-six are in the lowest rows, standing in water over a hand's depth for weeks, in the river steps lane, the tanners' end and the boat families' row; twenty-two are in the second-lowest rows, with seep, and the barrels carried up. Of the small ones who took the fever, a hundred and twenty-eight were in the three lowest streets and eighty-two elsewhere. Of the households where a small one took the fever, about one in thirteen had a wet cellar; of all households in the town, about one in twenty-eight has one. The same ratio, a little under or over, stands in each of the four winters. The Clerk writes that the count shows the two together more often than not and says nothing of a cause, as he was asked for none.

The hedge-workers say what hedge-workers say: that the fever is easier eased in a dry bed.

**Month 6, a night.** The second tally keeper of the cooper family goes to the Warden's door by night for the third winter and stands in the doorway. She says her house's second tally now carries the levy lent to the boat houses, at a price she thinks the head set above the Speaker's house's, and that the second tally and the open tally together still lack a sixth, or a little over. She says she does not know whose it is. The Warden says he will carry only what he has counted, and that she may carry her own to the Clerk, who keeps a private book and keeps what he is told. She says she will think on it. He puts a fourth stick behind the lintel. She goes home.

**Month 6, the twenty-eighth day: the sitting.** A boat headman asks the stranger whether he has a count of the cellars. He says it is the Clerk's and the Boat Master's, whose runners went, and that he has nothing of his own. The Clerk lays the stick. The table is quiet at the figure of the fevers by the lowest streets. The Speaker, the head of the tanners' house, whose own end of the town is one of the three, says that the cellars are the town's old trouble and that the table has no silver for them this year. The east fields head says the lowest cellars are the old league's and the league's dead lie under them. The Boat Master says the count says nothing of the dead. The table orders two small things: that the Clerk keep the count of the wet cellars and the fevers each winter and set it beside the last, and that the Crier cry at market that the small ones be taken up out of rooms over wet cellars while the wet lasts. The Crier cries it, and some do and some cannot, since there is no upstairs to take them to.

In the same month the Warden sets his lads at the damp corner for the fifth winter, and the stick stands at 810 tails for the three months and 40 sacks pulled spoilt since the harvest. He still holds that the rats take more than the tails show.

The Clerk spends the long evenings of the winter, with his boy sent to bed, laying the league weights that have come to him by masons' hands beside his scales, and the marks on their faces beside the marks on the tallies in the chest. He tells no one. See the report.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

**Month 7.** The ice goes from the river on the fifth day, at its usual time. The Speaker changes with the opening of the river. The new Speaker is the head of the second of the two large boat families, a man of about fifty-five, slow and heavy and no speaker, who says on taking the chair that the table has had the soundings and the cellars on sticks and will go on having them so. On the sixth day the Warden walks the whole store bin by bin, aloud, with the Clerk's boy and the two keepers cutting sticks. The far run stands as sealed, four marks whole. The whole store stands at 22,320: near bins 18,860, far run 3,460. It squares with the winter's drawing to a few sacks in a hundred. The Warden has it cried at market.

On the evening of the sixth day the stranger goes to the Boat Master on the quay and asks, as a trader, whether his crews will sound the channel at the gate a fourth spring, at low water and at high, as they did, by the crews, with the depths put on sticks by the Clerk beside the last three springs. The Boat Master says he would have done it a fifth year unasked, and asks whether the man means to be at the gate. The stranger says he will not go down to the gate on the day, and that the Boat Master may set the first loaded boat to it himself. The Boat Master laughs and says the boat houses can find their own bottom, and that the man may keep his bench.

**Month 7, the ninth day: the sounding.** The Boat Master sets the first loaded boat to the gate himself, a boat of the cousin's family, and the crews sound beside it with the notched poles. The stranger is not at the gate; he is on the market bench, and hears of it at noon from a child of the boat families. At the spring high water the crown of the bar, which is the cut, is three feet and ten inches, a hand shallower than the crews found the cut left at the lowest water of last year. Above and below it the channel is about two feet, a little under last spring's two feet and two inches, along about eighty paces on each side of the crown. A loaded boat draws about two feet. It crosses the cut with a good margin, and at the lower shoal the steersman feels the bottom and the crews pole her through with six sacks lightered into a skiff. The sticks are made and the Clerk takes them.

The Clerk lays the four springs' soundings in a row, the crown at three feet and two inches, three feet, two feet and ten inches, and now three feet and ten inches on the cut. He says the crown cannot be read against the others, since it was cut in between.

**Month 7, the fourteenth night: the star.** The third of the year and the twenty-fourth since the stranger came. The watch marks the hours on a stick. The Counter is on the market bench at the deepening, and is seen there by two of the watch going their rounds. The marks agree with the two of the autumn within a finger of candle. The bone-setter at the marsh edge says her hands were dull for two days after, not one.

**Month 8.** In the second week, after a dry spell, the crews sound the channel at low water. The crown, the cut, stands at two feet and four inches, a hand under the two feet and eight inches it was cut to. Above and below it the channel is one foot and eight inches, two inches under last year's one foot and ten inches. A loaded boat cannot cross the shoals without lightering. The Boat Master, who has watched the shoals narrow and the crown hold, has his crews lighten the loaded boats by a third into skiffs before the shoal this dry spell, and four boats cross so. Two boats ground in the dry spell all the same, both at the shoals and not on the cut: a boat of the poorest boat family's headman, which sits three days till a rain lifts the river, and a farming house's hired boat, lightered by hand in a day. The cost in the Clerk's sum is five boat-days and a fifth of a load wetted, about seventy worker-days of hired labor, against a hundred and ten last year and seventy-five the year before.

The Clerk lays the four springs and the groundings beside the dredging's cost.

**Month 9, the sitting.** The Speaker puts the rule the council made: the sounding at the crown in the dry spell is two feet and four inches, over a foot and a half, and the rule does not call a dredging. A boat headman moves that the rule be read at the shallowest sounding in the channel and not the crown, since a loaded boat can no longer cross at the shoals; the farming heads say the rule was made at the crown and that the crown is open, and that the table does not change a rule because it is sore. The Clerk, asked, says from the sticks of the cut that the two shoals together would take about six hundred worker-days and the hire of two boats to cut by six inches, since they want less taken off than the bar did. The motion is put and lost, four to five, with the new Speaker not voting. The council orders only that the Clerk have the crews sound the shoals each dry spell and lay them with the crown, and that the toll go on to be gathered; the chest stands at about five hundred worker-days of hired labor. The boat houses say the river is closing at the shoals. The farming houses say the boat houses made the shoals.

The Clerk, at the end of the sitting, lays the watch's three sticks of the star beside the Crier's count: three nights this year, five, six and seven in the three years before, and three in the first. The Gate Warden says the watch has the hours, and that on all three nights the Counter was in the town. The Speaker says the table has heard it.

The summer markets open. The drovers of the plain come in. The coasting boats come up. The Gate Warden asks the stranger at the market bench what he means to do this summer. The stranger says he means to go down to the boat families' holdings on the marsh edge, for about ten days, and to be back. The Warden says that is a thing that has something to count in it, and that it is not the sensible thing he was counting.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

**Month 10.** At first light on the ninth day the Boat Master's string of three sets out from the town quay for the mouth on the ordinary run, with grain, reed work, salt fish, leather and beer. The steersman of the first boat says the old words to the water, "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's fortune." On the thirteenth day the cousin's family's two planked boats set out up the river on the ordinary run, with salt, salted fish, beer and reed work, the steersman the cousin's eldest son, now about thirty-three, on his eleventh run up. He says the old words at the quay. The stranger is not aboard either and is in the town; he is on the market bench at the hour. The boats come back on the ordinary run, the string about the end of month 10 and the upriver crew in the second week of month 11. What they took on at the mouth and at the head is theirs.

No dredging is made. The levy debt of the boat houses stands at about the same, since two of the headmen have borrowed again against next season's carriage, and the boat houses have carried the lenders' hides and barrels all summer at the lenders' price.

**Month 11, the third day.** The stranger goes to the Clerk of the Tally and tells him that he is going down to the boat families' holdings on the marsh edge, by water, in a boat house's boat, and that he will be back on or before the sixteenth day. The Clerk writes the day, the water and the room in the guest book, paid ahead as before. The stranger goes then to the Boat Master and asks, as a trader, which boat house would carry him and sit him at its table. The Boat Master says that the poorest boat family's headman has lost his three days in the dry spell and would be glad of two weights of scrap silver, and sits a decent table. The stranger goes to the headman and pays him two weights. The headman asks why the man wants to sit at the boat houses' tables. The stranger says that he has heard the farming houses' side of the river's cost and the offices', and would hear the boat houses' in their own words, over a meal, and that he asks for nothing and will carry nothing to the council unless he is asked. The headman says that is more than the table has done for him. The stranger says nothing to that.

**Month 11, the fifth day.** At first light, from the town quay, the headman's boat sets out, the stranger aboard, the headman steering and a crew of three. It is the first run of this boat's summer. As it shoves off the headman says the old words to the water, "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's fortune." It goes down the river and by the water to the holdings on the Iss Marsh [f36] edge; I give no distance or time. The stranger goes as the man, with the scales on his belt, and does nothing with the key.

**Month 11, the marsh edge.** The stranger is sat at the tables of the marsh-edge houses. He sits at three: the poorest headman's own, and the tables of two other headmen of the marsh-edge houses, one an old man of about seventy and one of about forty. The meals are eel, fowl, reed-flour bread and small beer, and the talk runs from the supper to the dark.

The old headman says that the boat houses did not make the bar, the river did, and that the town's grain goes down their water and the town calls the water theirs. The forty-year-old headman says the boat houses paid the river twice, once in silver borrowed and once in carriage at a price the market would not give, and that he does not know which is the dearer. The poorest headman says the dry spell grounded him on a shoal the cut did not touch, and that the levy is the council's and the debt is the lenders'. He says the count the Counter began is the first book that has the boat houses in it, and that it has them in it as the ones who owe, and that his people do not know whether to thank the man for it. He says it to the man's face over the table. The stranger says what the sticks say if he is asked, and he is asked, and says it: the Clerk's stick, who paid what. He says he does not know who ought to bear it, and that he asks for nothing. The forty-year-old asks whether he is the farming houses' man, since it was his count that showed the boat houses carry the greater part of the grain. He says he is no house's. The old headman asks whether he is the one who dims the star. He says he does not know. The old headman says the marsh is loud on those nights, as the Boat Master has said, and that the marsh edge does not say why, and the stranger does not ask.

On the second evening an old man at the second table begins a telling. The stranger says, before it is done, that he will not hold it. It is begun and stopped. Nothing more is said of it at the table, and the talk goes to eel.

Written both ways, briefly, for the return:
- If he is back on or before the sixteenth day, he comes up the river and in at the river gate, and goes first to the Clerk's house and gives him the day he came and the day he said. The stool at the foot of the table is empty for one sitting.
- If he is back after the sixteenth but before the harvest, the Clerk keeps the room, the stool is empty for two sittings, the Boat Master asks after him once, and he goes first to the Clerk and gives him the two days.

In either case, on the day he comes up through the river gate, a number of the boat families on the quay look up, and the watch at the gate looks up, as the Gate Warden has long had it do for anyone. The Clerk, from his door, and the Gate Warden, at the gate, each note who looked up and who did not. The poorest headman's wife runs to the granary yard and tells the Warden's lads that the Counter is back, as a child did last year. Of the farming heads in the market, none looks up. The fish stall woman does. The Boat Master, on the quay, looks up and says nothing.

**Month 11 to month 12, the weeks before the harvest.** The stranger stays at the market bench and the foot of the table and the gate, and does not go up the river or out onto the plain. He is seen to be in the town, the first summer since he came in which he goes no farther than the marsh edge and is home for the weeks before the harvest. The Gate Warden says to the watch captain that he has no number for it, that for four summers he has counted the man's sensible acts and that he does not know whether a man who stays is the fifth. The town notes who comes to find him and who does not.

The boat families come, more than before: the poorest headman twice, the new Speaker once, a boat headman's wife with a question about her cellar. Two farming heads come on separate days to ask what he made of the stick. The Gate Warden comes once, at the watch's hour, and says nothing and goes. The Clerk comes to say the Gate Warden's watch has cut the third stick of the year, and goes. The cooper widow passes the bench three times and does not stop. The east fields head does not come, nor the cooper head, nor the Speaker of last year, nor the Warden, who is at the yard.

**Month 12, the eighth day.** The reed cutter's daughter comes to the granary yard bench, where he is sitting, and does not sit. She says she has heard he sat at the marsh edge, and that her father's house was at the edge. She asks whether it is time she told what her father began. The stranger says the same as before: that he would rather carry nothing he would not be glad to carry, and asks whether it is hers to give and whether she means someone to carry it. She says she does not know yet. He says then she is to keep it until she does, and that he will be on the bench. She stands a while and goes home. She tells no one.

**Month 12.** The reaping begins in the last weeks and the town is busy. The far run is drawn down in months 11 and 12, the last, by the sack, with both keepers at the doors and the four marks broken before witnesses, and the old stock the Warden carries into the new harvest is 6,780 sacks, all in the near bins. The widow is at the bin and does not meet the Warden's eye. The Warden has the carting begun on the same footing: the count aloud at the door, both keepers' sticks, the Clerk's boy marking. No star night falls from the seventh month to the end of the year.

The year closes with the harvest coming in and looking ordinary. The river is shallower at the shoals and the cut is a hand shallower than when it was cut. The cellars are worse by five households. The second tallies stand.

## What did not move

- The second tallies. Two large houses still keep them. The cooper keeper has told the Warden a third time and the Warden has told her she may carry her own to the Clerk. No one else is told.
- Where the grain went. Nobody knows it as a body. The Clerk's winter of the uncle's sticks found no draw; the Warden's four sticks behind the lintel say what he was told and no more.
- The delta stick. Known to the Clerk and the stranger only. The Clerk has not carried it to the council.
- The far run's seal and the four marks. Whole, and broken before witnesses in months 11 and 12.
- The helpers. No office has taken the stranger's word as a god's. Nobody acts in his name. See the report.
