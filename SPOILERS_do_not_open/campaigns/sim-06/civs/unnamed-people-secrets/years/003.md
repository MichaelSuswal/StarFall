# Year 3: the unnamed people of god-secrets

Written by the civ agent. The year opens in autumn. Month 1 is the first month of autumn, month 4 the first of winter, month 7 the first of spring, month 10 the first of summer. The people do not know the stranger is a god and have no name for themselves or the town. Office holders are filed by office. The weather was ordinary: the harvest ordinary, the winter wet, the river open at its usual time. Where the year turns on something that is not the people's to settle, it is written both ways, briefly, and set out in the report.

Where this file differs from my Year 2 file, the Keeper's line stands. The closing count of last autumn did not reach the far wall and could not be squared, and the cry was held for the council. My Year 2 file wrote the cry as made at market; it was not. The town knew only that the Warden's count had been held back, and said so through the winter (see `lore.md`).

## How the year opened

The Warden, the Clerk and the stranger have known since the end of last summer what stood behind the facing of the far bins. In the two months between, the Warden said nothing to the council and little to anyone. The Clerk wrote it in his private book and kept to his house. The stranger sat on the granary bench as before, took no figure to anyone, and was not asked for one. The Warden was quiet with him through those weeks, in the way of a man who has been shown his own floor.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**Month 1, the second day.** The stranger sends a runner to the Warden the evening before, as last year, and the Warden answers that he will hear him and that the Clerk may stand by. In the granary yard, with the carts waiting along the wall, the stranger speaks to the Warden plainly and not at length. He says the far bins are hollow behind their facing and the near bins are full, and that the Warden and the Clerk and he have all seen it. He puts one proposal and no blame. The Warden is to carry the new harvest in and count it aloud at the door as the first sacks go in, as last year, and to fill the far bins first, by the sack, with a keeper of the tally from each of the two families who keep that end standing at the bin and cutting a stick of their own as every sack goes by. The Clerk is to set those sticks beside the old roll. And the Warden is to bring the gap to the council himself, in his own words, before the Clerk speaks and before the stranger does.

He says to the Warden that he does not ask where the grain went and will not guess. He says a store named short in the autumn can be mended by the winter. He says he will carry no figure of his own to the council on this: he will carry the Warden's, or none.

The Warden takes a long while. He asks whether the stranger knows where the grain went. The stranger says no, that he does not ask and will not guess. The Warden asks why he is not taking the figure to the council himself, since he has it. The stranger says that a loss a man is allowed to name and mend is a loss he will go on finding, and a loss another man names for him is one he will hide. The Warden says that is the second kindness this man has handed him that costs him, and that he does not like how well they work. He says he will do it, in his own way. He will tell the two heads himself before a sack moves. He will take the facing down himself with their keepers watching. The stranger is to sit on the bench by the door and not go near the far run. The gap is to go to the council in the Warden's mouth, and the Warden says he will say a thing the stranger did not ask him to say: that the roll was his to walk, and that he carried it forward without walking it. The Clerk, asked, says he will set the sticks beside the roll as is his office and wants no asking.

**Month 1, the third day.** The two families who keep the far end are the cooper house, who store the barley for their brewing there, and the oldest of the farming families, the family of the east fields, who have kept the first bins by the wall since before the Warden's uncle's time. The Warden sees the head of each in the evening, separately, at his own door, and says it in a few words. The head of the cooper house is smooth. He says the roll is wrong, that his house put its barley in that run every autumn before witnesses, and that he will send a keeper with pleasure. The head of the east fields family is old and loud. He says the roll is wrong and the Warden's uncle's carters know why, that he will send his brother's grandson to stand at the bin, and that he wants it understood at the council that his house has been robbed or its grain misentered, and which of the two is the Warden's to find. Neither says he knows where the grain went. The cooper head sends, as his keeper, the second tally keeper of his house, the widow who keeps his books, since refusing to would be remarked; he does not look at her when he tells her. The east fields head sends the grandson, a young man of about nineteen who counts on his fingers.

**Month 1, the fourth day.** At first light the Warden has the facing taken down, bin by bin, along the whole far run of twelve bins. Present: the Warden, the Clerk's boy and then the Clerk himself, who comes after the first bin, the two keepers, two witnesses (a headman of a boat family and the head of the west fields farming family), the Warden's two lads, the eight sack-counters. The stranger sits on the bench by the door and does not come down the store. The facing is one course of sacks across each bin mouth, two deep at the corners, a hundred and forty sacks in all, counted aloud on the floor as they come down. Behind each lies bare floor, old chaff and the dung of mice. Nobody speaks for a time. One of the sack-counters says rats. The Warden says rats do not face a wall in sacks. The widow keeper says nothing and her face does not change. The grandson says a name of the Warden's uncle under his breath and is told by the boat headman to hold his tongue. The Warden has the hundred and forty sacks set by on the floor, and says the carts will come in from midday.

**Month 1, days 4 to 26.** The carrying in. The first carts run through the near end to the far wall and the far run is filled before any near bin takes a sack. Each sack is cried at the door by the eight counters with a stick each, and at the bin by the two keepers, who each cut a stick of their own, and the Clerk's boy marks the cry on the Clerk's stick. Four sticks at the far run, then, and two witnesses by turns, a farming head and a boat headman, as last year. The far run takes five thousand eight hundred and sixty sacks of the new harvest by the evening of the ninth day, with the hundred and forty facing sacks set back in the front courses, so that the run stands at six thousand by the count. On the sixth day the east fields keeper's stick runs two sacks short of the other three at one bin. The witnesses have the sacks of that bin counted again on the floor and the Warden's crew is found right; the grandson goes red and his head, hearing of it that night, says the boy is no counter. The cooper house keeper's stick agrees with the Warden's to the sack on every day. The widow does not speak to anyone at the bin but to say the count.

The rest of the harvest goes into the near bins. In all the sacks counted in over the twenty-three days come to 31,520. The harvest is ordinary. The lads' sticks at the damp corner stand as before.

**Month 1, the nineteenth night: the star.** The first night of the year. The blue deepens about an hour, the star stands low about an hour, and then it is as before. The Warden is on the yard wall and sees it, and the sack-counters sleeping in the yard are woken to see. The Warden says to the Clerk next morning that the town has a star that goes dim and a granary that was hollow in the same year, and the Clerk says one has nothing to do with the other. The Crier says at market that it is the ninth since the Counter came, counting Year 1's three and Year 2's five.

**Month 1, the 27th day: the closing count.** The Warden has the store counted aloud as last year, by parties, with the Clerk marking and two witnesses, bin by bin from the door to the far wall. It reaches the far wall and squares, since the far run was counted in by the sack. The near bins carry old stock of 4,010 sacks by the count, against 3,950 in the roll, a few sacks in a hundred and put down to the old sticks' rounding. The far run stands at the six thousand, which is its roll entry. The whole store stands at 35,670 sacks: near bins 29,670, far run 6,000. The roll, read out by the Warden on the first day of the carrying in, had the old stock at 9,950 sacks. Against the 4,150 that stood, the gap is 5,800 sacks, by the Warden's own count. He has this on a stick in his own hand's mark. The far bins' doors are shut and sealed with wax by the Warden, the Clerk and both keepers, so that no one draws from them without the four marks broken. The Warden orders that the near bins be drawn first and the far run last, and that whoever draws the far run does so with both keepers present, cutting sticks.

**Month 2, the fourth day: the council.** At the first sitting after the harvest is in, the Speaker, who is still the head of the large boat family, has been asked through the Clerk by the stranger that the two families who keep the far end be heard before any word of blame is said, and has agreed. The Warden speaks first, standing, before the Clerk and before the stranger, who sits on his stool at the foot. He says what he has counted, and gives the figure for the gap, 5,800 sacks, by his own count of the far run against his roll. He says what the stranger did not ask him to say: that the far run was entered on the roll in his uncle's hand and in his own after, that he carried it forward each year without walking it to the wall, and that this is his to answer. He says he does not know where the grain went and will not guess before the table. He says he saw the facing come down with his own eyes and that he will say what he saw to anyone who asks. He sits.

The two heads are heard. The cooper head says the roll is wrong and that his house is a house that counts. He says he will lay his house's sticks of the far run before the council, which are the open tally, and that the council should look to the old Warden's time. He regrets, in a smooth voice, that the Warden has been served so by his uncle. The east fields head says the roll is wrong and that his house has been robbed, or its grain drawn and not struck, in his father's day or the Warden's uncle's, and that he moves the council search the Warden's house and his uncle's old house and the carters' yards. The motion has no second. The cooper head, who might have seconded it, looks at his hands. The table is quiet. The Clerk, asked by the Speaker to lay the sticks, lays the three sets side by side, the Warden's crew's, the two keepers' and the Clerk's own, and says they agree to within four sacks in the whole, which the witnesses settled. He says that no stick he has shows a draw from the far run in his time or his father's for the sum in question, and that he will go through the old sticks of the Warden's uncle through the winter. He says it is not a promise to find.

A headman of a boat family across the table asks the stranger what he makes of the gap, since he has sat on the stores for two years. The stranger says what the sticks say, and says whose sticks they are: the Warden's crew's, the two keepers', the Clerk's. He says no more. Then a farming head, down the table, says that a Warden who walked nothing for nineteen years should be asked to stand down. The stranger says that a town that hangs a man for a short bin will not be told the next one is short, and that if the table turns to blame he will leave it. The Speaker, who has heard the line, strikes the table and says the table will hear the sticks and will not hang a man on a count of his own, and that the Warden has done what no Warden before him has done in her memory and put his own floor on the table. The table does not turn. The stranger does not leave. The council orders that the Warden keep his roll by walking it, bin by bin, every spring at the opening of the river and every autumn at the harvest, aloud, with the Clerk's boy, and that the closing count be cried each harvest as the Warden's, with the figure for the far run by itself.

**Month 2, the sixth day: the market.** The Crier cries the closing count at the Warden's stone, with the Warden standing at its foot. The near bins, the far run by itself ("counted in this autumn from its facing, six thousand sacks"), and the whole of the store, 35,670. He does not cry the roll's figure, but the town has it from the council's doors by evening. It does not take the market an afternoon. A man at the cooper stall says the Warden has lost a fifth of a harvest. A woman of the boat families says the Warden has found a fifth of a harvest. The Warden stands through the cry with his hands folded and goes back to the yard.

**Month 3.** The first frost. The Warden's lads' sticks go on at the damp corner. The cranes pass over in flights for some days, toward the plain. The stranger sits on the granary bench and the gate and the market bench as before. The town talks of nothing else for a month and then talks of it less.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The stranger stays inside the walls and talks. He sits and listens. He keeps the one kept thing as he keeps it. He asks the Gate Warden for nothing and does not change the figure he gave for the watch's rations.

**Month 4.** Boys in the street have a rhyme about the hollow bins, which the Warden's sister's son hears on the yard wall. He knocks one of them down. The Warden, told, has the boy of fifteen brought to the yard and says nothing for a long time and then says the boy is right to be angry and wrong to hit, and that the rhyme will wear out. It does, in about three weeks.

The Gate Warden comes to the stranger at the gate in the middle of the month, at the watch's fire, and asks him, in front of the watch captain, where the grain went. The stranger says he does not ask where it went and will not guess. The Warden says that is an answer a man gives when he knows, or a man gives who truly does not, and the two sound alike. He says no more. The stranger does not say more either.

**Month 5.** The winter fever comes through the narrow streets near the river. It is heavier among the old than last winter's. The hedge-workers are busy and two go to bed after. The reed cutter of the marsh edge, about seventy, who began a telling on the granary bench last winter, dies of it in the second week. His daughter keeps the house.

In the same month the star dims on two nights, five days apart, the eighth and the thirteenth. Many are awake with the fever. The cups on the river steps are more again. A bone-setter at the marsh edge and a woman who finds strayed beasts say their hands were dull for a day after the second. No one else says it. The Crier says it is the tenth and eleventh since the Counter came.

On the fourteenth day of the month, at the end of the Clerk's season of quiet, the stranger goes to the Clerk's house and sits with him over a cup. As idle talk, he asks once more whether he might sit an evening with the league's tallies in the Clerk's house and count the notches and not the meaning, on the Clerk's own terms. He says again he does not ask for the list of names and will not ask for it, and that if the Clerk wishes him gone at any hour he goes. The Clerk, who has spent the autumn with his own sticks of the far run laid out beside a roll that was wrong, is long quiet. He asks why the stranger put the figure to the Warden and not to the council. The stranger says what he said to the Warden. The Clerk says that a kept book shown by the one who keeps it is better than one taken, and that he has heard the saying before from this man and had not understood that he meant it. He says yes, one evening, on his terms.

His terms: his house, with his boy present, on a night of his choosing. The stranger is not to touch a stick except with the Clerk's hand on it. No copying of a notch, on clay or wax or stick or ground. No sound of reading. Three tallies of the Clerk's choosing and no more. And the Clerk will count each himself, and the two counts are to be set side by side after. If they agree, the Clerk will think about a second evening next winter.

**Month 5, the twenty-second night.** The evening is held, in the Clerk's house, by lamp. The Clerk opens the chest himself, with his own key, and takes out the three. He and the stranger count notch by notch, the Clerk's hand on each stick as the stranger passes his finger above it without touching the notches, and the boy sets down the numbers on the Clerk's stick. The Clerk's counts and the stranger's agree on two of the three. On the third they differ by one notch. The Clerk counts again and the stranger is right. The Clerk is quiet. He says the notch was worn and he had taken two for one. The stranger does not say anything about it. The evening ends at the second watch. The stranger is not shown the names and does not ask. The Clerk writes it all in his private book that night, and writes also that the man did not read, and that he paused once at one stick longer than at the others and did not say why. The Clerk says in the morning to no one that the man counts better than he does and that he finds he does not mind it. The Clerk's boy says to another boy, who says to his mother, that the Counter was in the Clerk's house with the chest. By the fourth day the market says it.

**Month 6.** The reed cutter's daughter comes to the granary yard bench in the first week and sits down by the stranger. She says her father began a thing to him last winter and did not finish it, and asks whether the stranger will hear it from her. He says he would rather carry nothing he would not be glad to carry, and asks whether the thing was hers to give and whether she means someone to carry it. She says she does not know. He says then she should keep it until she does, and that he will be on the bench. She is quiet a while and goes home. She tells it to no one, so far as anyone knows.

In the same month the second tally keeper of the cooper family goes, one night, to the Warden's door. She was at the far run for twenty-three days and she saw it filled, and she has a sum that will not close. She says to the Warden, standing in his doorway, that the second tally of her house has lines for barley lent out against the next season's carriage, that she cannot tell him they account for what stood behind the facing, and that she can tell him the lines are in it. She says she will not say it at the table. She asks why she should be believed. The Warden says he did not ask her to be. He says he will carry only what he has counted, and will not carry what is hers. He says she is safe from his mouth. She goes home. The Warden tells it to no one, writes it on a stick in his own mark, and puts the stick behind the lintel.

In the same month the Warden sets his lads at the damp corner for the third winter, and the count at the close of winter stands at 810 tails for the three months and 38 sacks pulled spoilt since the harvest. The Warden's own reckoning is still that the rats take more than the tails show.

Also in the month a child of the boat families, a girl of nine, asks the stranger on the quay whether he is the one who makes the star go dim. He says he does not know. She says her grandmother says he does and her uncle says he does not. He says they may both be right, and she is satisfied.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

**Month 7.** The ice goes from the river on the sixth day, at its usual time. The Speaker changes with the opening of the river. The new Speaker is the head of the west fields family, a man of about fifty who is no one's creditor and says on taking the chair that he will hold the table to counts and not to names. On the ninth day the Warden walks the whole store, bin by bin, aloud, as the council ordered, with the Clerk's boy and the two keepers, who cut their sticks. The far run stands as sealed, the four marks whole. The whole store stands at 19,880 sacks: near bins 13,880, far run 6,000. This squares with the winter's drawing as the Warden's sticks have it, to a few sacks in a hundred. The Warden has this cried at market.

On the same day the stranger goes first to the Boat Master and asks for the first loaded boat that comes to the gate. The Boat Master gives him a boat of the cousin's family and its steersman, as the string of three is not yet loaded. The crews sound the channel with notched poles, at the gate, at the spring high water. The boat is brought over the bar loaded, with the crews sounding beside it. Three feet at the crown of the bar, about two feet and four inches a few paces up and down, along a channel of about eighty paces. A loaded boat draws about two feet and crosses clear, with a hand to spare. The sticks are made and the Clerk takes them.

In this month the star dims on two nights, the fifteenth and the seventeenth. The Gate Warden says at the council that the two nights are the fourth and fifth of the year, the twelfth and thirteenth since the Counter came, and that the nights are coming faster. The Speaker moves to the next matter. The Boat Master says that the marsh was loud the second night, and no one asks what he means.

**Month 8.** In the second week, after a dry spell, the crews sound the channel at low water: one foot and seven inches at the crown, with about two feet a few paces up and down. This is three inches shallower than last year's grounding sounding. A loaded boat cannot cross without lightering. Two boats ground on the bar in the same week: a cousin's family's and a boat of a farming house's hire. Both are lightered by hand and one sits two days till a rain lifts the river. The cost in the Clerk's sum is six boat-days and a fourth of a load wetted, about seventy-five worker-days of hired labor by the stranger's rate of last year, against last year's one grounding at about forty-five. The Clerk puts the sticks of the two springs side by side on his table, as the stranger asked him to last spring, and the two groundings' costs beside the dredging's, which stands at about thirteen hundred worker-days and the hire of two boats.

On the twenty-fourth night of the month the star dims. The sixth of the year, the fourteenth since he came.

At the sitting at the end of the month the new Speaker asks whether anyone has figures, and the Clerk lays out the two springs. The stranger is asked by a farming head whether he has anything to add and says that the sticks say what they say, that they are the Boat Master's crews' and the Clerk's, and that he says nothing of what the council should do. He sits down. The argument runs most of a morning and the council argues, for the first time in years, from two springs. The farming heads say the shallowing is three inches in a year and the bar will not be dredged for a three-inch loss. The boat heads say the three inches are the margin by which a loaded boat now cannot cross at low water at all, and that the river has been closing a little every year and the groundings are not the whole of it. The Clerk says that if the bar comes back at the rate of the sticks, it is at four to seven years from the last dredging, which was eleven years back, and has therefore overstayed, and that the sticks show only two springs and he will not call a trend from two. The council settles this: a toll of one copper bit on every loaded boat and cart through the river and land gates, from the start of the next year, to be kept in a separate chest by the Clerk for the dredging; and a rule, put by the Speaker and carried seven to two, that if the sounding at the crown in a dry spell next spring is under a foot and a half, the council dredges in the dry season of that year, with the levy shared by the farming and boat houses by the weight of grain each sends down the river. The boat heads take the rule because it is a count. Two of the large houses do not like it and do not say why.

**Month 9.** The summer markets open. The drovers of the plain come in. The coasting boats come up. The town is quiet. The Gate Warden asks the stranger at the market bench, as he passes, what he means to do this summer. The stranger says he will go up the river, not down it, on a crew, for about a month, to see what lies on the water above the town. The Warden asks why he should go where there is nothing to count. The stranger says he does not know that there is nothing. The Warden says that is the third sensible thing the man has done, and that he does not like to count it.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

**Month 10, the first week.** The stranger goes to the Boat Master and asks, as a trader asking after his own trade, for a berth as a weigher and counter on a crew going upriver, paid in scrap silver for passage and keep. The Boat Master asks why. He is told he may take it or leave it, and that the stranger means to see what lies above the town on the water, who keeps it, who trades on it and what comes down it. The Boat Master takes three weights of scrap silver, one more than for the run to the mouth since the keep is longer, and gives him a berth on the upriver crew of the cousin's family: two planked boats, the first steered by the cousin's eldest son, a man of about thirty-one who has made the run up eight times. The Boat Master tells the Clerk, and only the Clerk, that the stranger means to be back before the harvest and leaves nothing in his hands. The Clerk writes it in the guest book with the day and the river. The room is kept, paid ahead.

**Month 10, the sixteenth day.** At first light, from the town quay, the upriver crew sets out: two planked boats carrying salt, salted fish, beer and reed work, with the stranger aboard the first and a crew of four to each boat. As the first boat shoves off the steersman says the old words of the boat families to the water, "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's fortune", as every boat of the families does on its first run of the summer. It is 39.7 miles up the river to its head: a little over three days going up by pole and tow, and less than two coming down. The crew's ordinary run is to make the head in four days with a day lost to the current, sell the cargo at the families' landing there and lie by it for about three weeks while the upper market runs, take on what the landing offers, and come down in two. The run is about twenty-eight days. What lies at the head, who keeps the landing, who comes to it beyond the boat families, and what the stranger sees and does there, is the Keeper's.

Written both ways, briefly:
- If he returns with the crew, on or about the thirteenth day of month 11, he comes up from the quay and goes first to the granary yard, as he said he would, and asks the Warden to let him look at the store as it stands. The stool at the foot of the table was empty for one sitting.
- If he returns later but before the harvest, the stool is empty for two sittings, the Clerk keeps the room, and the Boat Master asks after him twice. He comes in by the quay, and goes first to the granary yard.
- If he does not return before the harvest, the Clerk keeps the room as paid, some say he has gone for good, and the Crier says a man who pays a season ahead means to be back.

**Month 11.** At the lowest water of the summer the Boat Master's crews sound the channel at the gate for the second summer: one foot and one inch at the crown, with a little under two feet a few paces up and down. A loaded boat cannot cross without lightering. The Clerk puts the depths to stick and lays them in a row with the others. The toll is not yet gathered and the chest is empty. The stranger, if he is back, says nothing of it.

If the stranger is back, on the day he returns, or the day after, he goes to the granary yard first and asks the Warden to let him walk the store and take it as it stands. The Warden walks it with him, and with one of his lads and the Clerk's boy. The near bins are drawn down as the Warden ordered, from the door end. The far run stands behind its four marks, whole, and is not yet drawn. The Warden's sticks and the keepers' sticks stand beside the roll, and the roll no longer carries a bin the Warden has not walked. The Warden says nothing of what the stranger thinks, and the stranger says that it is well kept, and says no more. The Warden says afterward to the Clerk that the man looked at the far wall for the length of a long breath, and that he does not know what he looked for.

**Month 12.** The reaping begins in the last weeks and the town is busy. The far run is drawn down in months 11 and 12, the last, by the sack, with both keepers at the doors and the four marks broken before witnesses, and the Warden has the draw cut on the sticks as it goes, so that the old stock he carries into the new harvest, 4,380 sacks by the count at the door, is the first old stock in his years that he has counted in himself. No star night falls in the summer. The cooper head's keeper, the widow, is at the bin and does not meet the Warden's eye. The Warden has the carting begun on the same footing as last year and as this: the count aloud at the door, the far run's keepers at the bins, the sticks in two hands, the Clerk's boy marking.

The year closes with the harvest coming in and looking ordinary. The dredging is not settled. The rule is made.

## What did not move

- The dredging. Argued from two springs now, with a toll chest and a rule that turns on a count next spring. Not done.
- The second tallies. Two large houses still keep them. The cooper keeper has told the Warden of lines for barley lent out of the far run, and no one else.
- The cellars. Took water again in the wet winter.
- The helpers. No office has taken the stranger's word as a god's. Nobody acts in his name. See the report.
- Where the grain went. The Warden, the Clerk, the two heads and the cooper keeper each have a part of the answer or a guess and none has the whole. The people as a body do not know.
