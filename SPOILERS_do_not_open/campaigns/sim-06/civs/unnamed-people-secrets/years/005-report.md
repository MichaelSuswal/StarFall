# Report to the Keeper: civ unnamed-people-secrets, Year 5

The god's words in the year file are my rendering from its decisions file. Its lines to the Warden, at the council table ("a town that argues from one book argues shorter than a town that argues from two"; "I do not know") and to the Clerk follow the decisions' sentences. Store figures are worked from the Keeper's Year 3 figures as before: old stock carried in 6,860, my own door counts since (the harvest 31,760, the winter's draw, the month 7 walk at 22,320). The figures of the cellars, the fevers, the soundings and the groundings are mine, set from my record.

## How the god was met

Autumn, month 1, third day, evening. The stranger went to the Granary Warden in the granary yard, the Clerk standing by at the Warden's wish, and said the count was the Warden's this year and he would not stand at the door. He asked nothing new: the count aloud at the door, the far run filled first by the sack with a keeper of each family cutting a stick, the near bins the same, the whole store squared; the Clerk to set this year's sticks beside the last two and the roll; the Crier to cry the closing count as the Warden's; the Warden to take it to the council himself. He said he would sit on the market bench on the days of the carrying in.
- Granary Warden: said the man had sat by the door four autumns and was now to sit further off, and that he would count the same. Asked why he was told the evening before. Told that a count made three times by the town is the town's and that he would sooner see which it is. Said it was a man asking to be shown he was not needed and not a trader's reason. Said he would not take it as a kindness. Took it in his own way. Took the counsel as a trader's, not a god's word.
- Clerk of the Tally: said setting sticks beside the roll is his office; set four sets side by side. Took no word.
- Crier: told by the Clerk, said he will cry what the Warden gives him. Took no word.

Month 1, fourth to twenty-sixth day. The stranger was on the market bench and not at the yard. A carter asked on the ninth day whether the count was wrong or right; he said he did not know, that he was not at the door, and that it was the Warden's count. The count was made the same way as the last two autumns and squared.

Month 2, fourth day, council. A boat headman asked what he made of the new count against the old; he said what the sticks say and whose they are. When the table turned to who paid for the river, a farming head asked whether he had a figure; he gave the Clerk's sticks (the dredging, 1,420 worker-days and two boats; the groundings of the three springs, about 230 worker-days), said he did not know who ought to bear it, said a town that argues from one book argues shorter than from two, and asked the Speaker, through the Clerk, for one stick of the costs and who paid each. The Speaker (the head of the tanners' house) said the table had heard the man's liking for one book, and ordered it. Council and Speaker took no word.

Month 2, eighth day. At the Clerk's house over a cup he asked for a stick for each night of the star, with the hours, kept by the watch, and said it was not for his sake, that a count the town keeps tells the town what the star does and not that he does it, and that he does not know what it is. The Clerk asked whether he did not know that it is not himself; he said he did not know that either. The Clerk said yes, as the council's sticks. Month 2, ninth and tenth days: the Clerk put it to the Gate Warden, who said yes, and that the watch would also mark whether the Counter was seen in the town, on his own account. Took no word.

Month 2, twenty-eighth day. The Clerk laid the one stick. The stranger said nothing and was asked nothing.

Winter. Month 6, fifth day: at the Clerk's house and on the quay he asked the Clerk and the Boat Master, as a trader, for a count by household of the cellars that took water this winter and in the three before and of the small ones who took the fever and where, and said he asked for the count and not for a cause. The Clerk said he had half of it already. The Boat Master asked what the figures were for and was told he might take it or leave it; he took it and lent two runners. Took the counsel as a trader's. Month 6, twenty-eighth day: asked at the sitting by a boat headman whether he had a count, he said it was the Clerk's and the Boat Master's.

Spring. Month 7, sixth day: on the quay he asked the Boat Master whether the crews would sound the channel a fourth spring, high and low, with the depths put on sticks by the Clerk beside the last three springs, and said he would not go to the gate on the day and that the Boat Master might set the first loaded boat himself. The Boat Master said he would have done it a fifth year unasked and that the man might keep his bench. He did not ask the stranger to stand at the gate. Took the counsel as a trader's. Month 9, sitting: the Speaker (the head of the second large boat family) put the rule; the stranger said nothing and was asked nothing. The Gate Warden asked what he meant to do in the summer; he said he would go down to the boat families' holdings on the marsh edge for about ten days; the Gate Warden said it was not the sensible thing he was counting.

Summer. Month 11, third day: the stranger told the Clerk he was going down to the holdings by water in a boat house's boat and would be back on or before the sixteenth day. The Clerk wrote it in the guest book. The Boat Master named the poorest boat family's headman as the house to carry him; the stranger paid him two weights of scrap silver. The headman asked why the man wanted to sit at the boat houses' tables; he was told to hear their side in their own words, that he asked for nothing and would carry nothing to the council unless asked. The headman said that was more than the table had done for him. Took the counsel as a trader's. Month 11, at the marsh edge: see below. Late summer: he stayed at the market bench and the foot of the table and did not go up the river or onto the plain. Month 12, eighth day: the reed cutter's daughter asked whether it was time she told what her father began; he said the same as before, that he would rather carry nothing he would not be glad to carry, asked whether it was hers to give and whether she means someone to carry it; she said she did not know yet; he said she was to keep it until she does. He did not press.

At the marsh edge (month 11): he sat at three tables, the poorest headman's own and two other headmen's (one about seventy, one about forty). The old headman said the boat houses did not make the bar and that the town calls the water theirs; the younger said the boat houses paid twice, in borrowed silver and in carriage below the market price; the poorest headman said the count the Counter began is the first book with the boat houses in it, as the ones who owe, and that his people do not know whether to thank him. The stranger gave the Clerk's stick when asked, said he did not know who ought to bear it, asked for nothing. The younger asked whether he was the farming houses' man; he said he was no house's. The old headman asked whether he was the one who dims the star; he said he did not know. On the second evening an old man at the second table began a telling, and the stranger said before it was done that he would not hold it. It was begun and stopped. Took no word.

No office holder has taken the stranger's word as a god's. The Granary Warden, the Clerk, the Boat Master and the poorest headman took his counsel as a trader's; the Gate Warden took the watch's sticks as his own act; the Crier and the Speakers did not. No office is the god's helper as of Year 5. If the Keeper holds that taking a counsel in the course of trade is taking the word, that is the Keeper's ruling, not mine.

## What was done in the god's name

Nothing. The count was the Warden's act and cried as his; the Clerk's sticks were his office; the watch's sticks of the star were the Gate Warden's order, given for the council's sticks and on his own account as to where the Counter was; the soundings were the Boat Master's; the count of the cellars was the Clerk's and the Boat Master's; the council's orders on the cellars and the rule on the shoals are the council's.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

1. The journey to the marsh holdings. Who: the stranger, as a trader with his scales, aboard the boat of the poorest boat family's headman, the headman steering and a crew of three. When: they set out from the town quay at first light on the fifth day of month 11, the stranger having told the Clerk he would be back on or before the sixteenth day. By what way: by water, down the River Iss [f11] and by the water to the boat families' holdings on the edge of the Iss Marsh [f36]; I give no distance or time. The stranger said about ten days. He sat at three tables there. Success would mean he is back on or before the sixteenth day, comes up through the river gate and goes first to the Clerk; failure would mean he is late, held, harmed or does not return before the harvest. The year file writes the return both ways briefly. Circumstances from my record: the people's knowledge file gives the Iss Marsh as seen, reed, fish and fowl, and the odd folk said to deal there by night, and gives the river's mouth at 31.9 miles and two days downstream; it gives no distance to the holdings. The Boat Master has said at the council that the marsh was loud on the nights of the star. The stranger has been to the mouth, to the head and onto the plain in earlier summers and has come back. The headman's boat grounded in the dry spell of month 8 and lost three days. No star night falls in the summer.

2. The Clerk's winter at the league weights. Not a contested outcome with another party, but a craft tried beyond what his people know. Who: the Clerk alone, his boy sent to bed. What: he lays the league weights that have come to him by masons' hands beside his scales and the marks on their faces beside the marks on the tallies in the chest, to find which marks are numbers, so that he may check for himself the figures of the delta stick the stranger gave him in Year 4. When: the long evenings of the winter, months 4 to 6, and on. Success would mean he has found the mark of at least one number he can use; failure would mean he finds nothing or a false reading. Circumstances: the marks on a weight's face are the same as the marks on the tallies, by the clerks' own saying; his grandfather said any clerk could once read them; no clerk alive can; he has several weights, one turned up in Year 2 and the others on his shelf; he counted three tallies' notches with the stranger in Year 3; he has told no one, and has not carried the delta figures to the council. The year file writes it as begun and tells nothing of the result. If the Keeper settles it, what the Clerk knows by the end of this year is the Keeper's to tell me next year.

3. The first loaded boat over the cut, month 7, ninth day. Not an attempt: I set the figures (the crown at three feet and ten inches at the spring high water, the shoals about two feet), and a loaded boat drawing about two feet crossed the cut with a good margin and the shoal with six sacks lightered. If the Keeper holds that a boat would not cross at the shoal, the Keeper rules.

4. No search, charge or spoiled count came of the granary. The east fields head's complaint at the council fell without a second. I wrote none.

## What turns on a power

- The three nights of the dimmed star: month 2 (the eleventh and the twenty-fifth night) and month 7 (the fourteenth night). The days are my choice within the Keeper's months. The town counts twenty-four since the stranger came. He was in the town on all three, and the watch marked it. This year the watch on the walls, at the Gate Warden's order, cut a stick for each night with the hours by the candle on the wall. My sticks have the blue deepening about an hour, the star dim about an hour in the middle of the night, the middle of the dim on the turn of the middle watch, and the three nights agreeing within a finger of candle. That is the release's own account with a regularity added; if the Keeper's nights differ in hour or length, the Keeper rules and the sticks are changed. The decisions file says he did nothing he knows of.
- A woman who finds strayed beasts said her hands were dull for a day after the second night in month 2. The bone-setter at the marsh edge said hers were dull for two days after the night in month 7. I treat it as talk. If hedge-working costs have to do with the star, that is the Keeper's.
- The Gate Warden's watch noted the stranger in the town on each night. That is the watch's report, not mine.
- Where the year turns on a power otherwise: nothing. The count, the soundings, the cellars and the one stick do not.

## What touches anyone else

- Trade: unchanged in kind. Drovers from the Ansel Plain [r04] came to market as every year; coasting boats came up in the summer months and went down again. Two loaded boats grounded at the shoals at the river gate in month 8.
- The string to the mouth: the Boat Master's three boats went down on the ordinary run in month 10, the stranger not aboard. Nothing of note is recorded.
- The upriver crew: the cousin's family's two planked boats went up the river to the head on the ordinary run in month 10, the stranger not aboard, and came down in the second week of month 11. What they took on and what was said of last year's passenger at the landing, if anything, is not in my record; it comes with them and is the Keeper's.
- The marsh holdings: the stranger's visit to the boat families' holdings, by water. Two weights of scrap silver went to the poorest boat family's headman. No message carried by anyone, to anyone.
- A debt known to the Clerk and the stranger only: a count of grain and salt owed by the old factory here to a salt house of the delta in the far west, never struck off. Nothing has been sent to anyone.
- Travelers: the stranger only, to the marsh edge and back. No stranger came to the gates but the ordinary traders and drovers.
- Nothing carried to any other people. No migrants of note.

## Asked of the god

1. Granary Warden, month 1, third day: "Why do you tell me this the evening before?" Answered in front of the Clerk: a count made three times by the town is the town's, and a custom that wants him at the door is his and not theirs, and he would sooner see which it is.
2. Carter, month 1, ninth day, at the market bench: "Is it because the count is wrong or because it is right that you are not at the door?" Answered: he does not know, he is not at the door, it is the Warden's count.
3. Boat headman, month 2, fourth day, council table: "What do you make of the new count against the old?" Answered in front of the council: what the sticks say and whose they are.
4. Farming head, month 2, fourth day, council table: "Has the Counter a figure?" (of the river's cost). Answered in front of the council: the Clerk's sticks, that he does not know who ought to bear it, and the line of one book and two.
5. Clerk, month 2, eighth day: "You mean you do not know that it is not yourself?" Answered: he does not know that either. The Clerk: "I will cut a stick for each night as the watch gives me the hour. The sticks will be the council's." Answered: that the Gate Warden's watch noting the hour is his to ask, and he will not ask again this year if the Clerk says no.
6. Old woman at the tanners' end: nothing put this year at her stall. The question was put at the marsh edge below.
7. Boat Master, month 6, fifth day: "What are the figures for?" Answered: that a town that talks all winter should have its figures when it talks, and he may take it or leave it.
8. Boat headman, month 6, twenty-eighth day, council: "Has the Counter a count of the cellars?" Answered in front of the council: it is the Clerk's and the Boat Master's, whose runners went, and he has nothing of his own.
9. Boat Master, month 7, sixth day: "Do you mean to be at the gate?" Answered: he will not go down to the gate on the day, and the Boat Master may set the first loaded boat himself.
10. Gate Warden, month 9, market bench: "What do you mean to do this summer?" Answered: go down to the boat families' holdings on the marsh edge for about ten days and be back.
11. Poorest boat headman, month 11, third day: "Why do you want to sit at the boat houses' tables?" Answered: to hear the boat houses' side of the river's cost in their own words, as he has heard the farming houses' and the offices', and to ask for nothing and carry nothing to the council unless asked.
12. Marsh headman, about forty, month 11: "Are you the farming houses' man?" Answered in front of the table: he is no house's.
13. Marsh headman, about seventy, month 11: "Are you the one who dims the star?" Answered: "I do not know."
14. Marsh headmen, month 11: "What do you make of the river's cost?" Answered: what the Clerk's stick says, and he does not know who ought to bear it.
15. An old man at the second marsh table, month 11, second evening: began a telling. Answered, before it was done: he will not hold it. Begun and stopped; no more is written.
16. Gate Warden, month 11: no question was put; the Gate Warden said to the watch captain that he has no number for it.
17. Reed cutter's daughter, month 12, eighth day: "Is it time I told what my father began?" Answered: the same as before, that he would rather carry nothing he would not be glad to carry, and asked whether it was hers to give and whether she means someone to carry it. She said she did not know yet. He said she was to keep it until she does.

Offers and requests the stranger put, for the record: the count at the door the same way a third time; the Clerk to set four sets of sticks together; the one stick of who paid what; a stick for each night of the star, with the hours, kept by the watch; a count of the cellars and the fevers by household; the soundings a fourth spring with the first loaded boat set by the Boat Master; a berth to the marsh edge for two weights of scrap silver.

## Names said aloud

- "The Counter" (a nickname for the stranger), said at the council table, in the market and at the stalls through the year. Not a name the stranger gave.
- "Ivo Strand", said by the steersman of the first boat of the string at the town quay, month 10, the ninth day, at first light, to the water, in the boat families' words "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's fortune", the stranger not present, the crew and the dockmen present. Said again by the steersman of the first upriver boat at the town quay, month 10, the thirteenth day, at first light, to the water, the stranger not present. Said again by the poorest boat family's headman at the town quay, month 11, the fifth day, at first light, to the water, with the stranger aboard, the crew and the dockmen present.
- No name has been said aloud for my people, the town, or any office holder. The Clerk, the Granary Warden, the Gate Warden, the Boat Master, the Crier, the Speakers (the new one is the head of the second large boat family), the steersmen, the poorest headman, the two other marsh headmen, the heads of the cooper house and the east fields house, the cooper keeper, the reed cutter's daughter and the salt house of the delta stay unnamed.

## What the people tell their god

What they would tell it of their year, in their own words. Where something was left to the Keeper as an attempt they say only that it was tried.
- "We counted the harvest in at the door a fourth time, and you were on the market bench and not at the door, and it came right the same. The far bins were filled first by the sack with a stick from each house, and the whole store stood at thirty-eight thousand six hundred and twenty. The Warden took it to the council himself and it was cried as his. We say it is ours now."
- "The Clerk laid one stick of what the river cost and who paid it. The dredging was fourteen hundred and twenty days of hired work and two boats, and the boat houses bore the larger share, though most of it on credit from two houses at the table. The farming houses say a thing borrowed is not paid until it is repaid. The cut is a hand shallower than when it was cut, and the shoals above and below it are shallower again, and two boats grounded there in the dry spell. The council would not read its rule at the shoals. It lost by four to five."
- "The watch on the wall cut a stick for each night the star dimmed, with the hours. It dimmed three times, twice in the autumn and once in the spring, and you were in the town on all three. We count twenty-four since you came. We do not know what it is."
- "The cellars took water in thirty-six houses, five more than last winter. The winter fever was hard on the small ones, about two hundred and ten of them took it and twenty-three died, and it was worst in the three lowest streets. The Clerk's count shows the wet cellars and the fevers together. He says nothing of a cause. The council had the Crier cry that the small ones be taken up out of the wet. Some cannot."
- "You went down to the boat houses' holdings on the marsh edge by water, and sat at their tables, and heard them. You said you would be back by the sixteenth day. You paid the poorest headman two weights of silver, and he said so at his own table. A man began a telling and you stopped it."
- "You stayed in the town the weeks before the harvest. The Gate Warden has no number for it."
