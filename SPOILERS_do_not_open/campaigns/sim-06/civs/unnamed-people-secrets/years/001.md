# Year 1: the unnamed people of god-secrets

Written by the civ agent. The year opens in autumn. Months are counted from that opening: month 1 is the first month of autumn, month 4 the first of winter, month 7 the first of spring, month 10 the first of summer. The people do not know they are watched, do not know the stranger is a god, and have no name for themselves or the town. Office holders are filed by office. Where the year turns on something that is not the people's to settle, it is written both ways, briefly, and set out in the report.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**Month 1, the first day.** The harvest is in and ordinary. At sunrise a sentry on the east wall, walking out the night's watch, sees a figure standing on the rise a mile away beside the Iss Track [f37], in a brown coat, not moving, facing the town. He takes it for a drover waiting for the gate and thinks no more of it. The figure stays on the rise for the whole of the early morning. Smoke goes up from the town, carts and baskets move on the track, a boat or two goes out on the River Iss [f11], and the figure turns its head at each of these in turn, as a man learns a room.

Mid morning the man walks down the track and comes to the land gate. He gives the Gate Warden's watch the guest's call openly. The watchman on the gate, asking the usual questions, is told the man is a trader with scrap silver and brass weights, that he came by the track from the south-west, and that he has no name for the book. He says it plainly and smiles a little. The watchman, who has never been told a man has no name and does not like a book with a hole in it, sends for the watch captain, who sends for the Clerk of the Tally. The watchman says to the next man on the gate, "The Counter has come," because of the scales on the man's belt and his way of looking at the cart tallies as they pass. The word goes down the market street before noon. It is a joke and a nickname, not a name.

The Clerk of the Tally comes up to the gate in his own time, hears the man out, and enters him in the guest book with a mark of his own drawing, a pair of scales over a short stroke, and the day. The guest is fed three days and left unharmed, by the town's code. The man pays in scrap silver for his food and for a bed in a house near the market street that lets rooms to traders, so that he owes nobody. He is given a place on the bench in the granary yard when he asks for one, since benches there are used by carters waiting to unload and nobody minds one more.

**The two dim nights.** Two nights in the first month, some days apart, the star dims. The blue deepens for about an hour, the star stands low for about an hour in the middle of the night, and it comes back. The town has seen such nights in other years and does not rise from its beds for them. Those awake on the walls and on the boats see it and mention it in the morning, as they do. This year the market has more to say, because the stranger came in the same month. See `lore.md`.

**Month 1, days two and three.** On the second day the man asks the Granary Warden, with the Clerk standing by, to let him see the books of the harvest and the store, since he is a trader and a counter and would like to see how a town this size keeps its count. The Granary Warden does not like it and says so. The Clerk, who has the books of the council in his keeping, and who has not in his life met a stranger who asked to see a granary's tallies and offered something in return, says guest right does not stretch to the ledgers but that a count made in the open, on the granary floor with witnesses, is another matter. The man says he would ask nothing better. He is shown the tally sticks and the clay and wax books of the harvest and the bins as the Warden's people keep them.

The man says little. Where a figure is plainly wrong he says so in a small voice and with no weight on it, and he says only what the figure is and not what it means. The harvest tally by the Warden's book and the harvest tally by the sticks differ by a few measures in a hundred. He says it twice, once to the Warden and once to the Clerk. The Warden answers that the difference is rats and damp in the low bins, and that every year says the same. The man says that is likely, and that a count made aloud on the floor would show how much, and that a count cried at the market is believed in a way a book is not. He asks whether the Clerk and the Crier would cry the count to the council when it sits. He asks it as a favor in a bargain, not an order.

On the third day, in the late afternoon and in the evening, he sees the other office holders one at a time. In each case the offer is the same: a count, a weighing, a second pair of eyes on a ledger, for a hearing.
- The Clerk of the Tally, first. He hears the man out, says he will think on it, and that evening sends a boy with a message for the man's room, asking whether the man reads. He does not read, the man says, only counts; the Clerk takes this for modesty or a lie and writes neither in the book.
- The Granary Warden. Stiff and civil. He takes the offer of a second pair of eyes for the bin count and no more. He will not show the stranger the council's own books.
- The Gate Warden. Cool and short. He says the man is a guest for three days and a guest after that is a matter for the council. He wants to know who the man's people are and why a trader walks in with no goods. The man answers that he trades in counting and the Warden does not like the answer. The Warden orders a quiet watch kept on the room, as he would for any stranger.
- The Boat Master. Easy and curious, and the only office holder who asks what the man has seen on the river. The man says what he saw from the rise: which boats were out, which way they carried what. The Boat Master finds it exact and a little uncanny and takes up the offer of a weighing of a cargo of grain for the south, since the Boat Master's scales and the buyer's differ every season.
- The Crier. Amused. Says it is his office to cry what the council sets, not what a guest sets, but that a count by the Clerk is the Clerk's and he will cry it if the Clerk gives it to him.

No office holder takes the man's word as an order, and none is asked to. They take his counsel as a trader's counsel.

**Months 2 and 3.** The first thing is the count. In the second month, before the first hard frost, the Granary Warden has the bins counted aloud on the granary floor, with the Clerk to mark the sticks, two of the boat families' headmen to witness, and the stranger on the bench by the door, saying nothing. The count comes in a little under a twentieth short of the Warden's book, which is rats and damp in the low bins and some grain which has gone out on a carter's word and not on a stick. The Warden, shamed to have been wrong in front of witnesses, finds he is easier for it, because the missing grain now has a number and a cause, and his book is made right. The Clerk gives the count to the Crier. The Crier cries it at the market on a market day, and the market, which has not heard a store counted aloud in living memory, listens. The count is believed, and it is the first thing for years on which the town has had the same figure in its head on the same day.

The count is cried to the council when it sits in the third month. The council hears it with the stranger on a stool at the foot of the long table. See the council below.

The people are pleased in a small way. A joke goes round that the stranger counts so well the books come right behind him, and an old woman at the tanners' end of the market says, loud enough to be heard, that the old people said a god would come as a stranger who counts, and that you would know him by the way the books came right. Laughter. Her own son hushes her. The Clerk, when the joke reaches him, says that a book comes right when a man counts it and that is all there is to it.

**The council.** Late in the third month, after the three days are long done and the man has stayed on, hiring a bed and paying with counting and weighing for the Boat Master, the Granary Warden and two market stalls, the Clerk brings him before the council as a guest who has done the town a service. He does not ask for a seat or a vote. He asks for a place on a bench to be heard on the granary and the winter's stores. The council divides. Five of the nine are for giving him a stool at the foot of the long table for the winter's sittings, on the stores only, with no vote and no place on the rolls. Four are against: two of the large families who do not like a stranger at their table at all, the Gate Warden, who wants him watched and not heard, and one boat family headman who says that a man who will not say his name is a man to be wary of. The stool is given by five to four. He is not entered on the rolls. The Speaker for the year, who is for him, says the town can decide the rest of it at its leisure.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The man stays inside the walls. He sits on the granary yard bench in the cold with his coat drawn up, at the gate with the watch when they will have him, and on a bench in the market street on the days the market is held. He is a quiet man. People talk to him because he does not talk back much and because he remembers what he is told. The Clerk notes this, and notes also that the man never repeats what he has heard. The Clerk is not used to such a person and watches him the more for it.

The council sits at the long table through the winter on the stores, the dredging quarrel and the cellars. The stranger sits on his stool and is asked, three times in the winter, for a figure, and gives it, and the council goes on. The count of the store cried at market has changed one thing at the table: the boat families and the farming families have a figure for the grain that goes down the river, and the figure shows the boat families carry the greater part of the grain that the town sends out, which makes the dredging bill, in the council's own telling, theirs. The boat families do not like it. The farming families like it very well. The dredging is not settled. Both sides say the stranger's count did it, and neither side thanks him.

The river is low and the silt at the river gate is no better. The winter is ordinary and wet, and the lowest cellars take water again. A winter fever comes through the narrow streets near the river in the fifth month and a number of the old and the very young die of it. The hedge-workers are busy that month, easing fevers and setting a few bones broken on the ice at the gate. Two of them go to their beds for several days afterward, as they do.

**The night the star dims a third time (month 5).** Anyone awake sees it, as in the first month. This time the town has talk of the stranger and the star together, though he has been seen in his room that night by the woman who lets it (she heard him cough through the wall at midnight) and has done nothing anyone can name. The talk is the more for being unfounded.

**The confidence.** In the deep of the winter, on a night when the council has sat long and the frost has settled, a woman who keeps the tallies for one of the large families (the family that coopers and brews) comes to the granary yard bench where the stranger is sitting and begins to speak. She has not asked him. She sits down and begins, in a low voice, to tell him what she has not told the Clerk or the council or her own household's head: that her family keeps a second set of tallies, as does another large family, and why. She tells it to him because he is a counter and a stranger and the sum will not close for her and she cannot ask a clerk. The stranger does not tell her to stop. He listens to the end. When she has finished she asks him whether the sum closes, and he tells her it does not. Neither says anything more. They sit on until the cold sends them in. She does not come again that winter, and says nothing of it to anyone. He keeps what she told him. What the telling was is in `people/second-tally-keeper-of-the-cooper-family.md`.

**The dog.** On a night in the dead of the winter, a brown dog the town does not know goes through the alleys behind the market street and out along the marsh edge. It is seen by the watchman on the river quay, who tells it at the gate the next day as a thing worth telling because the town's dogs did not bark at it, which they bark at every strange dog. A hedge-worker who sets bones, in a house at the marsh edge, wakes in the small hours to find a dog lying across her threshold. She takes it for a stray, gives it a bowl of water, and it goes at first light. A boy seen out late by the tanners' yard sees the dog stop at the doors of two hedge-workers' houses and look at them, and goes home and says nothing of it for a month. In the same night the dogs of the lower town lie quiet. By morning the stranger's door is shut as he left it, and he is slow coming down, short of words and heavy in the tongue, and he is a quiet man in any case. The woman who lets the room says he looked as a man looks the day after a hard night's drinking. She puts it down to that, and the Gate Warden's quiet watch on the room, set in the first month and long relaxed, sees nothing it can name. Nobody enters the stranger's room that night.

The town does not connect the dog and the stranger. The watchman has a story about a dog. The hedge-worker has a story about a dog. They are not told in the same place.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

The ice goes off the river in the seventh month and the town opens the river. The Boat Master's crews go down with the first loads. The silt at the river gate is worse for the winter wet, and the loaded boats ground twice on the bar. The Speaker for the year changes with the opening of the river, by the town's custom, and the new Speaker is one of the large farming families' heads and is neither for nor against the stranger. The stool at the foot of the table stays.

In the seventh month, when the ground thaws, the stranger asks the Clerk, over a cup, as idle talk, whether the town has old things that nobody can fully read, and whether the rolls hold any name he might ask about. The Clerk, who has been waiting for such a question since the first day, answers more freely than he means to. He says the old tallies of the league, the ones from the lowest cellars, are kept in a chest in the Clerk's own house. He says they are the town's pride and its shame, that no clerk alive can read the marks, and that the clerks argue over them every winter. He says also that in the old people's telling there were walkers who came in at the land gate in the days of his grandfather's grandfather, were entered as guests, sang lists of names in the market street for three nights, left on the fourth, and that the clerks of those days wrote down some of the names, and that the clerks of these days argue over what they are. He says the names are in a book that is not the council's and that he will not show a guest, but that he will say the names are not like any names in the rolls, and that there are twenty and some. He asks the stranger why he asks. The stranger says he likes old things that cannot be read, and the Clerk, who likes them too, says he knows the feeling. The Clerk says no more, and after the stranger has gone writes down in his own private book what he has said and to whom.

In the eighth month, once the ground is soft enough, the stranger walks the open ground outside the walls with something small in his hand that catches the light, bronze by its colour. He goes first to the rise by the Iss Track where he stood on the first morning. He walks the verge of the track for a mile each way, slowly, stopping at times. He goes also to the public ground by the river gate and the strip of common between the east wall and the marsh, which the Clerk told him he might use. He stays out of every yard, house and storehouse. The Gate Warden's watch on the wall sees him and sends word to the Warden, who sends a man to walk behind him at a distance. Whatever he does with the thing in his hand, the man sent to watch does not see anything done that he can name.

How it turned out is not the people's to say. Written both ways:
- If the stranger found something on open ground, the watcher sees him stop and stand a long time at one place on the verge of the track on the second morning, say nothing, make no mark that can be seen from a distance, and walk on, quieter than before. The Warden is told that the stranger "stood a long time at a place and then left it." Nobody digs there. The talk in the town that spring is that the stranger is looking for something, and some say it is lost silver and some say the old league's grain-house and some say a grave.
- If he found nothing, the watcher sees a man with a bronze thing in his hand walk the verge, stop and start, and come back to the gate by evening with dust on his shoes. The talk is that the stranger is a surveyor or a tinker or a man who thinks the cellars flood from the ground above.

Either way the Gate Warden has the watch note the places the stranger stopped, which are four, and puts them in the gate book.

The cranes passed over for some days late in the third month, toward the plain. That is in the autumn quarter and was ordinary.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

Summer is fair. The drovers from the Ansel Plain [r04] bring stock to market as they do and the coasting boats come in the summer months and go down again. The market sets its days and the market peace holds.

In the tenth month, early in the summer, the stranger says to the Clerk, and to no one else, that he means to go away for about a fortnight and be back by the harvest. He tells the Clerk because the Clerk keeps the guest book. He says he means to go down the Iss Track to the south-west on foot, as a trader with his scales, to see who keeps the road and what the plain holds. He leaves nothing in the Clerk's hands, no goods and no word, and the Clerk does not ask for any. He keeps the room he has hired, paid a season ahead. He takes the scales, the coat and his purse, and goes out by the land gate at first light on a market day, and the watch notes it. The Clerk writes in the guest book the day and the road. The Gate Warden says it is the first sensible thing the man has done, and he is not sorry to see the back of him for a fortnight.

What came of the journey is the Keeper's to price. The people know only that he set out for the south-west by the Iss Track, and that he said he would be back by the harvest. Written both ways:
- If he is back in the summer, within the fortnight or a little more, the town has missed him a little: the stool at the foot of the table was empty for two sittings, and the Boat Master asked after him twice. He comes in by the land gate, and goes first, as he said he would, to the granary to look at the store.
- If he is not back by the end of the year, the town has missed him in the same small way and the Clerk keeps the room for him as paid. Some say he has gone for good, and the Crier says a man who pays a season ahead means to be back.

In the twelfth month the harvest begins, and the town is busy with it. At the year's end the harvest is coming in. It looks ordinary. The dredging has not been settled.

## What did not move

- The dredging quarrel. Sharper, not settled. The count has shown the boat families what the grain they carry weighs, and they have not agreed to pay.
- The second tallies. Two large families still keep them. One keeper has told a stranger, and no one else knows that.
- The cellars. Took water again in the winter and are no better.
- The helpers. None of the offices has taken the stranger's word as a god's. Nobody acts in his name. See the report.
