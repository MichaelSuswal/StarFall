# Report to the Keeper: civ unnamed-people-secrets, Year 2

The god's words in the year file are my rendering from its decisions file, which gives their shape and in two places their exact sentence (the answer at the council table, and "I do not know"). Where the year file puts other words in the stranger's mouth, they are plain and kept to what the decisions say he would say.

## How the god was met

Autumn, month 1, second day. The stranger went to the Granary Warden in the granary yard, the Clerk of the Tally standing by at the Warden's wish. He offered the bargain in the Warden's terms and asked nothing for it.
- Granary Warden: stiff. Asked what the stranger wanted for it and why a guest would hand him credit. Took the bargain on his own terms (his crew, his sticks beside the Clerk's, the count cried as his, the stranger on the bench by the door). Gave two lads of his household to the damp corner and the rats. Took the counsel, not a god's word; he does not know of a god.
- Clerk of the Tally: stood by, then marked the cried figures on his stick as his own office. Took no word.
- Crier: cried the closing count at market as the Warden's, when the Warden gave it him. Took no word.

Month 2, council. A boat headman who spoke against the stool last year asked who the man is. The stranger answered for himself in the sentence the decisions file gave. The council laughed. The stool was renewed six to three (the Gate Warden, the boat headman and one large family against). The council ordered the Warden's closing count cried each harvest. The Speaker (a farming head) and the others took no word.

Winter. Month 4: the Gate Warden took a cup in the market street in daylight after four days' thought, put his questions, was answered as the decisions say, and did not say he believed it. Month 5: he let the stranger weigh the watch's rations from the common store; the watch draws a thirty-eighth of the store's issue where the council had reckoned a twenty-fifth. He took the figure to the council as the store's own sticks and said he owed no favor for it. Took no word.

Month 5: a reed cutter of the marsh edge began a telling on the granary bench; the stranger stopped it before it was done, saying he would carry nothing he would not be glad to carry, and asked if the man meant someone to carry it. The man said no and went home. By my count the stranger holds one telling, the cooper family's second tally keeper's of Year 1, and was not given another this year. How many he holds is the god's to say.

Spring. Month 7: the Boat Master gave the figure for last spring's two groundings (nine boat-days, a third of a load) and took the idea of soundings; the Clerk set it against the last dredging (about thirteen hundred worker-days and the hire of two boats, the bar back in four to seven years by his sticks, four by the Boat Master's saying). Month 8: the new Speaker (a boat family head) asked the stranger for figures at the council; he gave them and whose they were and said nothing of what to do. Took the counsel, not a god's word.

Summer. Month 10: the Boat Master gave the stranger a berth as a weigher on the first boat of the string for two weights of scrap silver, and asked why; the stranger said he wanted to see who trades on the water below the town. The Clerk was told only that he meant to be back before the harvest. The Gate Warden said it was the second sensible thing the man had done.
Month 12: the Clerk, asked over a cup for an evening with the league tallies (notches, not meaning, not names), said he will think on it and will not answer until the harvest is in.

No office holder has taken the stranger's word as a god's. The Granary Warden and the Boat Master took his counsel as a trader's. The Gate Warden, the Clerk, the Crier and the Speakers did not. No office is the god's helper as of Year 2. If the Keeper holds that taking a counsel in the course of trade, or a bargain put in the Warden's own terms, is taking the word, that is the Keeper's ruling, not mine.

## What was done in the god's name

Nothing. The Granary Warden's count was cried as the Warden's. The Clerk marked it as his own office. The Boat Master's soundings were done as the Boat Master's.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

1. The closing count of the whole store, month 1, the 27th day. Who: the Granary Warden crying it, eight sack-counters in two parties of four, the Clerk marking sticks, two witnesses (the head of a large farming family and a boat headman), the Warden's two lads, the Crier standing by, the stranger on the bench by the door. When: the day the last cart was in. How far down: the Warden means every bin in use from the door end to the far wall, counted bin by bin. Success would mean the count reaches the far wall and shows what is in the store, old stock and new; failure would mean it stops short or cannot be squared. What a count of the whole store would show is the Keeper's. Circumstances from my record: last year's count aloud ran a little under a twentieth short of the Warden's book, which the Warden put to rats, damp in the low bins, and grain gone on a carter's word without a stick; the new sacks were counted in at the door at 31,400 (that figure is mine); the lads' sticks at the damp corner to the year's end are 1,640 tails and 44 sacks spoilt, covering only the damp corner and the tails, and the Warden holds that the rats take more. The year file writes it both ways: squared, or not squared and the cry held for a sitting of the council.

2. The walk through the store, on the stranger's return, month 10, the 28th or soon after if he returned with the string. Who: the stranger, with the Granary Warden, one of the Warden's lads and the Clerk's boy. How far down: the stranger asks to walk the length of the store; the Warden agrees. What the whole length shows is the Keeper's. Circumstances as in 1. Written both ways: it stands as the book has it, or something at the far end does not and the Clerk is sent for.

3. The journey down the river. The stranger took a berth as a weigher on the first of three planked boats, steersman the Boat Master's sister's son, a crew of five besides, and set out from the town quay at first light on the ninth day of month 10 for the mouth, 31.9 miles, two days down. The crews' ordinary run: sell the cargo (grain, reed work, salt fish, leather, beer) at the mouth, lie by the families' landing about a fortnight, take on salt and wool, and come up in three days; nineteen days in all, back about the 28th day of month 10 on the ordinary run. What lies at the mouth, who is met there beyond the boat families, and what the stranger sees and does is the Keeper's. Whether he returns with the string, later but before the harvest, or not at all is the Keeper's; the year file writes the three.

4. The month 11 night of the star and where the stranger was. Written both ways in the year and in lore.

## What turns on a power

- The five nights of the dimmed star, months 2, 6, 9, 11 and 12. The people saw what the release says and have only talk: the stranger brings it, the marsh folk block it, the star is old. The town counts eight since he came. What the stranger did or did not do on them, if anything, is the Keeper's. The decisions file says he did nothing he knows of.
- A bone-setter at the marsh edge said her hands were cold after the night of month 6. No one else said it. If hedge-working costs are anything to do with the star, that is the Keeper's; I treat it as talk.
- Where the year turns on the stranger's absence in month 11 (the third night, with him on the river or not), see Uncertain outcome 4.

## What touches anyone else

- Trade: unchanged in kind. Drovers from the Ansel Plain [r04] came to market as every year; coasting boats came up in the summer months and went down again. One loaded boat grounded at the river gate bar in month 8.
- The string: three planked boats, the first with the stranger aboard, down the River Iss [f11] to the mouth, carrying grain, reed work, salt fish, leather and beer, and returning with salt and wool on the ordinary run. The stranger paid two weights of scrap silver for passage and keep. No message carried by anyone, to anyone. What is met at the mouth beyond the boat families is the Keeper's.
- Travelers: the stranger only. No stranger came to the gates but the ordinary traders and drovers.
- Parties sent out: the string, as the boat families' ordinary run. Nothing else.
- Nothing carried to any other people. A league weight turned up in the tanners' end and was given to the Clerk; it stays in the town.

## Asked of the god

The questions and requests mortals put to the stranger, in their words as near as I can set them, and whether he answered in front of them.
1. Granary Warden, month 1, second day: "What do you want for it?" Answered: nothing, in front of the Clerk.
2. Granary Warden, same: "Why would a guest of a year hand me the credit?" Answered: that a count by the Warden's own people will be believed where the stranger's would not, and that a loss a man finds himself he can mend.
3. Boat headman, month 2, council table: "Who is the man who will not give a name?" Answered by the stranger, in front of the council: "I have none. I had one once, I think, and I have not found it. Call me what you like, and I will answer to what is kindly meant."
4. Gate Warden, month 4, the cup: "Who sent you? What do you want of this town? Why has a trader with no goods stayed a year? Have you people anywhere?" Answered in front of the Warden: no one sent him; he wants the books to come right and the river to open; the town is a good town to count in; no people he can name. Where he could not answer, he said so.
5. Gate Warden, same: asked of the weighing, "What do you want for it?" Answered: nothing; the Warden said he owed no man a favor, and the stranger said he did not ask him to.
6. A reed cutter of the marsh edge, month 5, the granary bench, begins a telling unasked. The stranger, before it was done, said he would rather carry nothing he would not be glad to carry, and asked whether the man meant someone to carry it. The man said no.
7. The old woman at the tanners' end, month 6, at her stall: "Is it you that does it, Counter?" Answered: "I do not know."
8. Boat Master, month 7: "What are the figures for?" Answered: so that the council argues dredging from a count, and that he could take it or leave it.
9. New Speaker, month 8, at the council: "Have you figures?" Answered in front of the council: yes, the Boat Master's crews' and the Clerk's, with whose each is.
10. Boat Master, month 10: "Why do you want to go down the river?" Answered: to see who trades on the water below the town, as a counter would.
11. Clerk, month 12: "Why do you ask?" (of the league tallies). Answered: the figures are his trade, the notches can be counted without being read, and a kept book shown by the one who keeps it is better than a kept book that is taken.
12. Granary Warden, month 10, on the stranger's return: "Why the store first?" Answered: to see how it stands after the summer's drawing, as is his trade.

Offers the stranger put, for the record: a count aloud by the Warden's own people; a weighing of the watch's rations; a count of the cost of the groundings and of the dredging; soundings of the channel; an evening counting the notches of the league tallies.

## Names said aloud

- "The Counter" (a nickname for the stranger), said at the council table, in the market and at the stalls through the year. Not a name the stranger gave. At the table in month 2 he said he has no name.
- "Ivo Strand", said by the steersman of the first boat at the town quay, month 10, the ninth day, at first light, to the water, in the boat families' words "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's luck", with the stranger aboard and the crew and the dockmen present.
- No name has been said aloud for my people, the town, or any office holder. The Clerk of the Tally, the Granary Warden, the Gate Warden, the Boat Master, the Crier, the Speakers, the steersman, the old woman at the tanners' end and the second tally keeper of the cooper family stay unnamed.
