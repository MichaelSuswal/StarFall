# Year 4: the unnamed people of god-secrets

Written by the civ agent. The year opens in autumn. Month 1 is the first month of autumn, month 4 the first of winter, month 7 the first of spring, month 10 the first of summer. Months run about thirty days. The people do not know the stranger is a god and have no name for themselves or the town. Office holders are filed by office. The weather was ordinary: the harvest ordinary, the winter wet, the river open at its usual time. Where the year turns on something that is not the people's to settle, it is written both ways, briefly, and set out in the report.

Where the Keeper's line on Year 3 differs from my Year 3 file, the Keeper's line stands. The old stock that stood at last harvest was 6,630 sacks against 9,950 in the roll (near bins 6,490 and the 140 facing sacks), the gap 3,320, and the far run's roll entry 3,460, filled with 3,320 new sacks and the 140 facing sacks. My Year 3 figures of 5,800, 6,000 and 35,670 are replaced. Every store figure below is worked from the Keeper's figures and my own door counts (the new harvest of 31,520 last year, and the winter's draw as the Warden's sticks had it). The Clerk's evening with the league tallies is as the Keeper has it: the stranger's count was right where the Clerk's was out by one, and he said nothing of the stick he paused at. The journey up the river went as the crew's ordinary run, and he came down with it on the thirteenth day of month 11.

## How the year opened

The far run stands empty and swept, its twelve bins drawn down in the last two months of the summer behind four broken marks. The Warden carries into the new harvest 6,860 old sacks, all in the near bins, by the count at the door: the first old stock in his years that he has counted in himself. The roll he opens is the roll he walked in the spring and again at midsummer. The town has had a count cried at the Warden's stone twice now, and speaks of it as the town's way.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**Month 1, the second day.** The stranger sends a runner to the Warden the evening before. The Warden answers that he will hear him and that the Clerk may stand by, and the Clerk, who has not been asked, comes at the Warden's wish. In the granary yard, with the carts along the wall, the stranger asks one thing and nothing new. The harvest is to be carried in and counted aloud at the door as the first sacks go in. The far run is to be filled by the sack with a tally-keeper of each of the two families cutting a stick as every sack passes. The near bins are to be counted the same way. The Clerk is to set this year's sticks beside last year's and the old roll. The Warden is to take the count to the council himself, and the Crier to cry the closing count as the Warden's. He says nothing of last year's gap beyond what the sticks say, and does not ask where the grain went.

The Warden hears it to the end and says that the council ordered every part of it last year, that he would have done it without the asking, and that he does not see why a man asks for what is already ordered. The stranger says a count made the same way twice becomes the town's way, and then it no longer needs him at the door. The Warden is quiet a while. He says that is a thing he had thought and not said, and that he does not like how well this man says what he thinks. He says he will do it as he did last year and in his own way: his own crew at the door, his own lads, the stranger on the bench and not on the floor. The Clerk says setting the sticks side by side is his office and wants no asking, and that he will set three autumns side by side, not two, since the roll's old figure is the third.

**Month 1, the third day.** The Warden goes at evening to the two heads, the cooper house and the east fields, each at his own door. Since the near bins are to be stick-counted as well as the far run, each house must send two keepers, one for each end. The cooper head is smooth and sends the widow, his second tally keeper, to the far run again, and a nephew to the near bins. The east fields head, old and loud, says his house has been robbed and that he will say it at the council again, and sends the grandson of last year to the far run and a second grandson, younger, to the near bins. Neither head says he knows where the grain went. Neither takes any word of the stranger's, who has not spoken to either.

**Month 1, days 4 to 26.** The carrying in, twenty-three days of cart work. The far run is filled first. Eight sack-counters cry every sack at the door with a stick each. At the far run the widow and the east fields grandson cut their own sticks at every bin, with the boat headman and the head of the west fields house as witnesses by turns. In the near bins the two second keepers do the same at the bin mouths, and the Clerk's boy marks the cry on the Clerk's stick. The far run takes 3,460 sacks by the evening of the sixth day, which is its roll entry. This year the east fields grandson's stick runs one sack out on the third day, and he says so himself before it is found. The widow's stick agrees with the Warden's to the sack on every day, as last year, and she speaks to no one at the bin but to say the count. Her nephew in the near bins is less exact and is corrected by the Warden's counters twice.

The rest of the harvest goes into the near bins. The sacks counted in over the twenty-three days come to 31,640. The harvest is ordinary. The lads' sticks at the damp corner stand as before.

**Month 1, the twenty-seventh day: the closing count.** The Warden has the whole store counted aloud by parties, the Clerk marking and two witnesses at his elbow, from the door to the far wall. It reaches the far wall and squares. The near bins stand at 35,040 sacks (the 6,860 of old stock and 28,180 of the new), the far run at 3,460. The whole store is 38,500. The Warden has the far run sealed with wax by himself, the Clerk and both keepers, as last year. He orders the near bins drawn first and the far run last, by the sack, with both keepers present and cutting sticks. He has the count on a stick in his own mark.

**Month 2, the second day: the Clerk's three sticks.** The Clerk has set the sticks of this autumn beside those of last autumn and beside the old roll. He says to the Warden, in the Clerk's house, that the sticks of the two autumns agree to within the width of a hand in method and that the roll no longer carries a bin the Warden has not walked. He says it is no thing to cry. The Warden says he did not ask for it to be.

**Month 2, the fourth day: the council.** At the first sitting after the harvest is in, the Speaker, still the head of the west fields family, calls the Warden. The Warden stands and gives the count himself, before the Clerk and before the stranger: the store at 38,500, the far run counted in by the sack and sealed, the near bins counted by the same stick. He says what he said last year was his to say and that he has nothing to add to it. He sits. The Clerk lays the three autumns' sticks. A boat headman asks the stranger, from his stool at the foot, what he makes of the new count against the old. The stranger says what the sticks say and whose sticks they are: the Warden's crew's, the four keepers', the Clerk's. He says no more.

The east fields head rises and says again that his house was robbed, and that the carters of the Warden's uncle's day should be named before the table. The cooper head looks at his hands. The farming head who last year asked that the Warden stand down says, to the table, that the question of blame has never been answered. The stranger, who has been asked nothing, says that a town that hangs a man for a short bin will not be told the next one is short, as he said before, and that if the table goes on he will leave it. The Speaker strikes the table before it goes further, says the table has the sticks and has a Warden who put his own floor on it, and moves to the next matter. The stranger does not leave. The east fields head sits down red in the face and says it is not finished. It is not.

**Month 2, the sixth day: the market.** The Crier cries the closing count at the Warden's stone with the Warden at its foot: the near bins, the far run by itself, and the whole store, 38,500. The market listens as it listened last year and less hard. A woman of the fish stalls says the Warden has made the grain come back. A man at the cooper stall says it was always there. Both are spoken of for a day.

**Month 2, the ninth night: the star.** The first of the year. The blue deepens about an hour, the star stands dim about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it is as before. The Warden on the yard wall sees it, and the boys of the damp corner are woken to look. The stranger is in the town; he is seen on the granary bench in the early part of the night by a carter, who says he looked up as everyone did. The Crier says at market it is the fifteenth since the Counter came.

**Month 3.** The first frost. On the twenty-first night the star dims again, the second of the year and the sixteenth since the Counter. Both nights fell in a month of ordinary frost and nothing else. In the same month the cranes go over in flights for some days toward the plain, as they do, and the old say the star dims when the cranes go over, which it did not last year. A bone-setter at the marsh edge and a woman who finds strayed beasts say their hands were dull for a day after the second night. They do not say it together. The cups and crusts on the river steps are more again.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The stranger stays inside the walls and talks, and sits on the granary bench, at the gate and on the market bench as before. He asks the Gate Warden for nothing and does not change the figure he gave for the watch's rations. He does not go into the cellars.

**Month 4.** The star dims on the fourteenth night, the third of the year and the seventeenth since the Counter. A farming head at the sitting at the end of the month says across the table what the market says, and asks the stranger plainly whether he is the one who dims it. He says he does not know. The farming head says that is what he said to the old woman and to the child, and the stranger says it is still so. The Gate Warden says the town now has three nights in the autumn and winter alone and that he counts seven in the year if the year goes as the last, and is told by the Speaker that the table does not sit on the sky. The Crier cries the count of the nights at market in the same breath as the price of salt, and does not like to.

**Month 5.** The winter fever comes through the narrow streets near the river, lighter than last year's among the old and heavier among the small. About a hundred and seventy die in the year in all. The hedge-workers are busy and two go to bed after. The cellars take water again in the wet winter, the lowest worse than last year. The Clerk's boy carries the second-lowest cellars' barrels up for the third time.

At the end of the Clerk's season of quiet, which the stranger counts from the cry of the count in the sixth of month 2, the stranger goes to the Clerk's house in the fifth month, on the fifteenth day, at the end of the afternoon, and sits with him over a cup as idle talk.

**Month 5, the fifteenth day: the Clerk's house.** The stranger says plainly that in the evening of last winter, on the third tally, he paused at a stick longer than the others, and that the Clerk wrote that he paused. He says he read the marks as their makers would have, and that one of the sticks is a count of grain and salt owed by the old factory here to a salt house of the delta in the far west, never struck off. He says that before he says what the stick says he asks whether the Clerk wishes to hear it, and that if the Clerk does not, it is the Clerk's to say or not, and the stranger will not raise it again this year.

The Clerk is a long time quiet. He says the man told him last winter, and told him in the first year, that he does not read. The stranger says he does not read the way a clerk reads, and that this is a thing he can count to the end of and not otherwise, and that he will not say more than that about how. The Clerk says that is not an answer, and the stranger says it is as far as he can go and that he knows it is not enough. The Clerk, who has spent the winter at his own table with the sticks of the Warden's uncle laid out in rows and has found them clean of any draw for the sum in question, says that a kept book shown by its keeper is better than one taken, and that he has heard the man say it too often to pretend he did not mean it. He says yes, he wishes to hear it, once, and not here but in the room with the chest, with the door shut and his boy sent to bed.

The stranger gives him the figures as the stick gives them. He says that he does not know whether the debt is live, or whether the salt house still stands, and that what the town does about it is the council's and the Clerk's and not his. He says he carries no figure to the council himself and will not. The Clerk asks why he is told. The stranger says he would sooner the Clerk hear it from him, privately, than learn he knew and said nothing. The Clerk writes it in his private book that night in his own hand, with the figures, and writes beside it that the man says he cannot read and read it, and that he will not decide what that is. He tells no one. He says to the stranger at the door that he will carry it to the council himself if he carries it at all, and not before the harvest, and not on the strength of a figure he cannot read for himself. The stranger says that is the Clerk's.

**Month 6.** The Clerk sets before the council, at the end of the month, the result of his winter's work: the sticks of the Warden's uncle show no draw from the far run for the sum in question in the uncle's time or his father's, and the roll's entry for the far run had been carried over unchanged, without a walk, for eleven winters. He says it is not a promise, not a finding, and not a charge. The east fields head says it proves the carters robbed the house and left no stick; the cooper head says it proves the roll was wrong. Nobody seconds anything. The Warden says nothing and goes home, and at night puts a second stick behind the lintel beside the one he put there last summer.

In the same month the Warden sets his lads at the damp corner for the fourth winter, and the stick stands at 790 tails for the three months and 41 sacks pulled spoilt since the harvest. He still holds that the rats take more than the tails show.

The reed cutter's daughter does not come to the bench. She is seen once in the market street, and the stranger is seen to nod to her, and she to him. Nothing is said.

The second tally keeper of the cooper family goes to the Warden's door by night one more time, in the month, and stands in the doorway. She says her house has lent against the next season's carriage a third more than the open tally shows, to the boat families, and that the second tally and the open tally still lack a sixth. She says she does not know whose it is. The Warden says he will carry only what he has counted. He puts a third stick behind the lintel. She goes home.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

**Month 7.** The ice goes from the river on the fifth day, at its usual time. The Speaker changes with the opening of the river. The new Speaker is the head of the tanners' house, a man of about fifty-eight who keeps a second tally against the boat families, and who says on taking the chair that the table will hold to the dredging rule it made, because it made it. On the eighth day the Warden walks the whole store bin by bin, aloud, with the Clerk's boy and the two keepers cutting sticks. The far run stands as sealed, four marks whole. The whole store stands at 22,360: near bins 18,900, far run 3,460. It squares with the winter's drawing to a few sacks in a hundred. The Warden has it cried at market.

On the same day the stranger goes first to the Boat Master and asks for the first loaded boat that comes to the gate, and asks the crews to sound the channel a third spring at high water and at low. The Boat Master, who is easy and curious, says the crews will do it and asks whether the man means to be on the boat. He does. A boat of the cousin's family comes down loaded and the crews sound beside it. At the spring high water the crown of the bar is two feet and ten inches, with about two feet and two inches a few paces up and down of it, along a channel of about eighty paces. A loaded boat draws about two feet and crosses with two inches to spare, and the steersman says he felt the bottom once. The sticks are made, and the Clerk takes them.

**Month 7, a first count of the springs.** The Clerk sets this spring's depth beside the two before on his table, in a row: three feet and two inches, three feet, two feet and ten inches, at the crown, at the high water of three springs. He sets the groundings of last year beside the dredging's cost. The stranger says what the sticks say, that they are the Boat Master's crews' and the Clerk's, and that the three springs show a loss every year, which is not enough to say whether it is a trend. He says it is the council's to read.

**Month 8.** In the second week, after a dry spell, the crews sound the channel at low water: one foot and four inches at the crown, with a little over one foot and ten inches a few paces up and down. This is three inches under last year's low-water sounding and under the rule's foot and a half. A loaded boat cannot cross without lightering. Three boats ground in the dry spell: two of the cousin's family and a farming house's hired boat. All are lightered by hand and one sits three days till a rain lifts the river. The cost in the Clerk's sum is eleven boat-days and a third of a load wetted, about a hundred and ten worker-days of hired labor at the stranger's rate, against seventy-five last year and forty-five the year before. The Clerk lays the three springs' lows and the three years' groundings beside the dredging's cost at thirteen hundred worker-days and the hire of two boats.

On the nineteenth night the star dims, the fourth of the year and the eighteenth since the Counter. The Boat Master says at the council that the marsh was loud again, and again no one asks what he means.

**Month 9, the sitting.** The Speaker puts the rule: the sounding at the crown in the dry spell is under a foot and a half, and the council dredges in the dry season of this year, with the levy shared by the farming and boat houses by the weight of grain each sends down the river. The Clerk lays the toll chest: the toll of one copper bit on every loaded boat and cart has been gathered since the first of the year, and by his reckoning it stands at about five hundred and twenty worker-days of hired labor at the day wage. The rest, about eight hundred worker-days and the hire of two boats, is the levy. The farming heads say the shallowing is three inches and the dredging will be dry in four years. The boat heads say the rule is the rule and a count is a count. The Speaker says so too. The vote is six to three. The levy is set by the Clerk's weights: the farming houses pay in grain and carting days, the boat houses in carriage days and what silver they can find. Two of the boat headmen say at the table they cannot find it, and the tanners' house and the cooper house, which sit at the table, say they will lend it against next season's carriage. The table is quiet at that. The stranger, asked by a farming head whether he has anything to add, says that the sticks say what they say and that what the council should do is the council's, and he sits.

The summer markets open. The drovers of the plain come in. The coasting boats come up. The town is quiet. The Gate Warden asks the stranger at the market bench, as he passes, what he means to do this summer. The stranger says he will go up the river again, and on past the head, on a crew, and be back before the harvest. The Warden asks why he goes where there is nothing to count. The stranger says he does not know that there is nothing. The Warden says he has lost count of the sensible things this man has done and does not mean to start again.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

**Month 10, the first week.** The stranger goes to the Clerk of the Tally in his house and tells him he is going up the river again and on from the head, that he means to be back before the harvest, and that he names to him the twenty-second day of month 11 as the day on or before which he will come. He leaves nothing in the Clerk's hands. The Clerk writes the day, the river and the room in the guest book, paid ahead as before. Then he goes to the Boat Master and asks, as a trader asking after his own trade, for a berth as a weigher and counter on a crew going upriver, paid in scrap silver for passage and keep. The Boat Master asks why, and is told he may take it or leave it, and that the man means to see the head of the river again and go on. He takes three weights of scrap silver and gives him a berth with the cousin's family's crew, two planked boats, the first steered by the cousin's eldest son, now about thirty-two, who has made the run up nine times. He tells the Clerk, and only the Clerk, that he means to be back before the harvest.

**Month 10, the twelfth day.** At first light, from the town quay, the upriver crew sets out: two planked boats carrying salt, salted fish, beer and reed work, the stranger aboard the first, four crew to each boat. As the first boat shoves off the steersman says the old words to the water, "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's fortune." It is 39.7 miles up the river to its head: a little over three days going up by pole and tow, less than two coming down. The crew's ordinary run is to make the head in four days with a day lost to the current, sell the cargo at the families' landing and lie by it about three weeks while the upper market runs, take on what the landing offers, and come down in two: about twenty-eight days, home about the ninth of month 11. The stranger has told the steersman that he will go on from the landing on his own after the cargo is sold, as a trader with his scales, and that the crew need not wait for him past their ordinary day. He pays for the whole run. What lies beyond the landing and who is met there is the Keeper's.

Written both ways, briefly:
- If he comes down with the crew, about the ninth day of month 11, he comes up from the quay and goes first to the granary yard and asks the Warden to let him look at the store as it stands. The stool at the foot of the table was empty for two sittings.
- If he comes down later but on or before the twenty-second day, by a boat or on foot by the river path, the stool was empty for three sittings, the Boat Master asked after him twice, and he goes first to the Clerk, gives the day he came and the day he said, and then to the granary yard.
- If he is later than the twenty-second but before the harvest, the Clerk keeps the room and says the day was passed, and the Boat Master asks after him thrice, and the Crier says a man who pays a season ahead means to be back.
- If he does not return before the harvest, the Clerk keeps the room as paid, some say he has gone for good, and the Gate Warden says it was the fourth sensible thing and he will not count it.

**Month 10, the dredging.** With the stranger gone, the council's dredging begins in the dry season on the eighteenth day, at low water, under the Boat Master and the Clerk's boy: sixty hired hands, two hired boats and the toll chest's silver, digging the channel at the bar by baskets and antler picks from the boats. The levy is cried at the market the same week. The farming houses send grain and carting days and mutter. The boat houses send carriage days and little silver, and two of their headmen go in the week to the cooper house and the tanners' house for loans against next season's carriage, as the table was told they would. The Speaker's house lends first. The Clerk writes it in his private book.

**Month 10, the twenty-fourth night: the star.** The fifth of the year and the nineteenth since the Counter. Written both ways, briefly:
- If he is on the river, the town says the star dimmed with him away, and the boat families say they sent a stranger up the water and the sky followed him. Others say it proves he does not do it. The Crier cries the count of the nights, the first with the Counter out of the town on the night, and says so.
- If he is in the town, which the room being kept does not say, the talk is as in other years: seen on the bench at midnight looking up and saying nothing.
The crew's talk of what they saw of him on the night, at the landing or on the water, is the Keeper's; it comes down with them.

**Month 11.** The dredging finishes on the twelfth day. The channel at the bar is cut to about two feet and eight inches at low water at the crown, along the eighty paces, with the spoil laid on the south bank. By the Clerk's sum it took fourteen hundred and twenty worker-days and the hire of two boats, against the thirteen hundred of eleven years back. The Clerk lays it on the table beside the three springs. The council, hearing the figure, says it was dear and that the bar is open. The boat houses are glad and in debt. The farming houses say they paid for the boat houses' river.

On the fourth night of the month the star dims, the sixth of the year and the twentieth since the Counter. The same two ways as above, and the crew's talk of it with the Keeper. On the ninth day the upriver crew is due.

If the stranger is back, on the day he returns he goes first to the Clerk, as he said he would, and gives him the day he came and the day he said, so that the Clerk may see whether he was late; he goes next to the granary yard and asks the Warden to let him look at the store as it stands. The Warden walks it with him, with a lad and the Clerk's boy. The near bins are drawn down from the door end as ordered. The far run stands behind its four marks, whole, and is being drawn the last of the winter's stock behind both keepers' sticks, a bin at a time, and the roll no longer carries a bin the Warden has not walked. The Warden says nothing of what the man thinks, and the man says it is well kept and says no more. The Clerk and the Warden each note afterward who looked up when he came through the gate: the watch at the land gate, the Boat Master on the quay, the farming heads at the market, the fish stall woman, and a child of the boat families who runs to the granary and says the Counter is back.

At the lowest water of the summer, about the twentieth, the crews sound the channel at the gate after the dredging and put the sticks to the Clerk.

**Month 12.** The reaping begins in the last weeks and the town is busy. On the eleventh night the star dims, the seventh of the year and the twenty-first since the Counter, in a town in which, if he is back, he is on the bench. The far run is drawn down in months 11 and 12, the last, by the sack, with both keepers at the doors and the four marks broken before witnesses, and the old stock the Warden carries into the new harvest is 6,860 sacks, all in the near bins. The cooper head's keeper, the widow, is at the bin and does not meet the Warden's eye. The Warden has the carting begun on the same footing as last year and this one: the count aloud at the door, both keepers' sticks, the Clerk's boy marking.

The year closes with the harvest coming in and looking ordinary. The dredging is done and paid for in part by debts. The second tallies stand.

## What did not move

- The second tallies. Two large houses still keep them, and the table has been told by the Speaker's own house that it will lend the boat families their levy shares. One keeper has told the Warden more than before, and no one else.
- The cellars. Took water again, the lowest worse than last year.
- Where the grain went. Nobody knows it as a body. The Clerk's winter found no draw in the uncle's sticks.
- The delta stick. Known to the Clerk and the stranger only.
- The helpers. No office has taken the stranger's word as a god's. Nobody acts in his name. See the report.
