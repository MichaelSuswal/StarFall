# Report to the Keeper: civ unnamed-people-secrets, Year 4

The god's words in the year file are my rendering from its decisions file. The stranger's lines to the Warden, at the council table ("a town that hangs a man for a short bin..."; "I do not know") and to the Clerk in the winter follow the decisions' sentences. Store figures are worked from the Keeper's Year 3 line (roll's old stock 9,950, stood 6,630, gap 3,320, far run entry 3,460); my own door counts are the harvest (31,640), the winter's draw, and the months 7 walk (22,360).

## How the god was met

Autumn, month 1, second day. The stranger went to the Granary Warden in the granary yard, the Clerk standing by, and asked for the count the council already ordered: carried in and counted aloud at the door, the far run filled by the sack with a keeper of each family cutting a stick, the near bins counted the same way, the Clerk to set the sticks beside last year's and the roll, the Warden to take the count to the council himself, the Crier to cry it as his. He said nothing of last year's gap beyond the sticks and did not ask where the grain went.
- Granary Warden: said the council ordered all of it and that he would have done it unasked; heard that a count made twice becomes the town's way and said he had thought it and not said it. Took it in his own way. Took the counsel as a trader's, not a god's word.
- Clerk of the Tally: said setting sticks beside the roll is his office; set three autumns side by side. Took no word.

Month 1, third day. The Warden told the heads of the cooper house and the east fields house. Each sent two keepers, one to each end. Neither took any word of the stranger's, who spoke to neither.

Month 2, fourth day, council. The Warden gave the count himself. A boat headman asked the stranger what he made of the new count against the old; he said what the sticks say and whose they are. The east fields head spoke again of the carters and a farming head of blame; the stranger said a town that hangs a man for a short bin will not be told the next one is short, as before, and that if the table went on he would leave. The Speaker (still the head of the west fields house) struck the table and moved on; he did not leave. Council and Speaker took no word. Month 2, sixth day: the Crier cried the count as the Warden's. Took no word.

Winter. Month 4, end of the month: a farming head asked at the sitting whether he is the one who dims the star; he said he does not know. The Gate Warden took no word. Month 5, fifteenth day: at the Clerk's house the stranger asked whether the Clerk wished to hear what an old stick says; the Clerk said yes, once, in the room with the chest, the boy sent to bed; the stranger gave him the figures as he read them and said he did not know whether the debt was live or the house stood, and that what the town does is the council's and the Clerk's. The Clerk took it as a counter's telling, not a god's word, noted that the man says he cannot read and read it, and told no one. Month 6: the Clerk laid his winter's sticks before the council (no draw in the uncle's time). The cooper keeper told the Warden a second time at his door. The reed cutter's daughter did not ask again; no word passed.

Spring. Month 7: the Boat Master gave the stranger the first loaded boat at the gate and had his crews sound the bar a third spring. Took his counsel as a trader's. Month 8: the Clerk set the three springs and the groundings against the dredging. Month 9: the Speaker (new, head of the tanners' house) put the dredging rule; the stranger, asked by a farming head whether he had anything to add, said what the council should do is the council's. Month 9: the Gate Warden asked what he meant to do in the summer and why go where there is nothing to count; told he would go up the river again and on, and that he does not know there is nothing; said he has lost count of the sensible things. Took no word.

Summer. Month 10, first week: the stranger told the Clerk he is going up and on from the head and names the twenty-second day of month 11 for his return; the Boat Master gave him a berth for three weights of scrap silver and asked why; told he may take it or leave it. Took the counsel as a trader's.

No office holder has taken the stranger's word as a god's. The Granary Warden, the Clerk and the Boat Master took his counsel as a trader's; the Gate Warden, the Crier and the Speakers did not. No office is the god's helper as of Year 4. If the Keeper holds that taking a counsel in the course of trade is taking the word, that is the Keeper's ruling, not mine.

## What was done in the god's name

Nothing. The count was the Warden's act and cried as his; the Clerk's sticks were his office; the soundings were the Boat Master's; the dredging, the toll and the levy are the council's.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

1. The journey up the river and on. Who: the stranger, as a weigher and counter, aboard the first of two planked boats of the Boat Master's cousin's family, the steersman the cousin's eldest son (about thirty-two, ten runs up), four crew to each boat. When: they set out from the town quay at first light on the twelfth day of month 10. By what way: up the River Iss [f11] to its head, 39.7 miles, a little over three days going up, less than two coming down; the ordinary run is four days up with a day lost, about three weeks lying at the families' landing, two days down, home about the ninth of month 11. The stranger told the steersman he will go on from the landing on his own, as a trader with his scales, and the crew need not wait past their ordinary day. He named to the Clerk the twenty-second day of month 11 for his return; the Boat Master told the Clerk only that he means to be back before the harvest. Where he sets out for beyond the landing, and by what way, the Keeper prices; I give no distance or time. Success would mean he is back with the crew or by the day named; failure would mean he is late, held, harmed or does not return. The year file writes four ways briefly: with the crew about the ninth; later but on or before the twenty-second; after the twenty-second but before the harvest; not at all. What lies at the head and beyond, who is met, what he sees, are the Keeper's. Circumstances from my record: the people's knowledge file gives the river's mouth and nothing above the town but the Keeper's line that he came back from the head last year on the ordinary run; the boat families go up each summer; the dredged bar crosses a loaded boat easily at low water.

2. The dredging. Not an attempt: the council's rule triggered on the month 8 sounding (1 ft 4 in at the crown, under the foot and a half), and the work is the people's own craft as eleven years ago (baskets, antler picks, hired hands). I wrote it done in month 10 to 11: 1,420 worker-days and the hire of two boats, the crown cut to about 2 ft 8 in at low water. If the Keeper holds that a bar of eighty paces cannot be cut to that depth by that work, the Keeper rules.

3. No search, charge or spoiled count came of the granary. The east fields head's complaint at the council fell without a second. I wrote none.

## What turns on a power

- The seven nights of the dimmed star: month 2 (the ninth night), month 3 (the twenty-first), month 4 (the fourteenth), month 8 (the nineteenth), month 10 (the twenty-fourth), month 11 (the fourth), month 12 (the eleventh). The days are my choice within the Keeper's months. The town counts twenty-one since the stranger came. He was in the town on the first four. On the nights of months 10 and 11 the year turns on where he was: on the river or beyond the landing, or in the town; it is the Keeper's, and the year file writes both ways. The people have only talk. Which of them he was present for and what the crew saw of him on those nights is the Keeper's. The decisions file says he did nothing he knows of.
- A bone-setter at the marsh edge and a woman who finds strayed beasts said their hands were dull for a day after the second night in month 3; a third hedge-worker, who turns breech births, said it of the night in month 4. I treat it as talk. If hedge-working costs have to do with the star, that is the Keeper's.
- The stranger's telling to the Clerk in the winter of what an old stick says. The decisions say he reads the marks as their makers would; the Clerk noted the man says he cannot read. The decisions ask for a ruling only if the Keeper thinks the speech not plainly covered. I wrote the figures as read and true. The figures themselves, and any name of the salt house, are the stranger's words in the Clerk's room and are not set down by me.
- Where the year turns on a power otherwise: nothing. The count, the soundings and the dredging do not.

## What touches anyone else

- Trade: unchanged in kind. Drovers from the Ansel Plain [r04] came to market as every year; coasting boats came up in the summer months and went down again. Three loaded boats grounded at the river gate bar in month 8.
- The string to the mouth: the Boat Master's three boats went down on the ordinary run. Nothing of note.
- The upriver crew: two planked boats of the cousin's family with salt, salted fish, beer and reed work, the stranger aboard the first, three weights of scrap silver. No message carried by anyone, to anyone. What is met at the head and beyond is the Keeper's.
- A debt known to the Clerk and the stranger only: a count of grain and salt owed by the old factory here to a salt house of the delta in the far west, never struck off. Nothing has been sent to anyone, and the Clerk has told no one.
- Travelers: the stranger only. No stranger came to the gates but the ordinary traders and drovers.
- Nothing carried to any other people. No migrants of note.

## Asked of the god

1. Granary Warden, month 1, second day: "Why does a man ask for what is already ordered?" Answered in front of the Clerk: a count made the same way twice becomes the town's way, and then it no longer needs him at the door.
2. Boat headman, month 2, council table: "What do you make of the new count against the old?" Answered in front of the council: what the sticks say and whose they are.
3. Farming head, month 2, council (put to the table): that the question of blame has never been answered. Answered by the stranger: a town that hangs a man for a short bin will not be told the next one is short, and he will leave if the table goes on. The Speaker cut it; he did not leave.
4. Farming head, month 4, council: "Are you the one who dims the star?" Answered in front of the council: "I do not know."
5. Clerk, month 5, fifteenth day: "You said you do not read." Answered: he does not read the way a clerk reads, this one he could count to the end of, and he will say no more of how. The Clerk: "That is not an answer." Answered: it is as far as he can go.
6. Clerk, same: "Why am I told?" Answered: he would sooner the Clerk hear it from him, privately, than learn he knew and said nothing.
7. Farming head, month 9, council: "Have you anything to add?" Answered: the sticks say what they say, and what the council should do is the council's.
8. Gate Warden, month 9, market bench: "What do you mean to do this summer?" and "Why go where there is nothing to count?" Answered: go up the river again and on, and be back before the harvest; he does not know there is nothing.
9. Boat Master, month 10: "Why do you want to go up the river?" Answered: he may take the berth or leave it; he means to see the head of the river again and go on.
10. Reed cutter's daughter: nothing put. No telling offered or asked.

Offers and requests the stranger put, for the record: the count at the door with keepers of both families and the near bins stick-counted; the Clerk to set three autumns of sticks together; the soundings a third spring, high and low; the first loaded boat at the gate; the figures of a stick to the Clerk, after asking whether he wished to hear them; a berth upriver as a weigher.

## Names said aloud

- "The Counter" (a nickname for the stranger), said at the council table, in the market and at the stalls through the year. Not a name the stranger gave.
- "Ivo Strand", said by the steersman of the first upriver boat at the town quay, month 10, the twelfth day, at first light, to the water, in the boat families' words "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's fortune", with the stranger aboard, the crew and the dockmen present.
- No name has been said aloud for my people, the town, or any office holder. The Clerk, the Granary Warden, the Gate Warden, the Boat Master, the Crier, the Speakers (the new one is the head of the tanners' house), the steersman, the heads of the cooper house and the east fields house, the cooper keeper, the reed cutter's daughter, and the salt house of the delta stay unnamed.

## What the people tell their god

What they would tell it of their year, in their own words.
- "We counted the harvest in at the door a third time, and the far bins were filled first by the sack with a stick from each house, and the whole store stood at thirty-eight thousand five hundred. The Warden took it to the council himself and it was cried as his. Nobody asked where the old grain went and nobody said."
- "The river opened on time. The bar was a little shallower again, and in the dry spell it was under the foot and a half, so by our own rule we dredged in the dry season, and it cost fourteen hundred and twenty days of hired work and two boats, and the bar is open. The farming houses say they paid for the boat houses' river, and the boat houses borrowed their share from the two houses at the table."
- "The winter fever was light among the old and hard among the small. The cellars took water again."
- "The star dimmed seven times, more than any year. We count twenty-one since the Counter came. We do not know what it is. We set the cups on the river steps and call it manners."
- "The Counter went up the river again in summer, on the cousin's boats, and said he would go on from the landing and be back before the harvest, by the twenty-second of the eleventh month."
