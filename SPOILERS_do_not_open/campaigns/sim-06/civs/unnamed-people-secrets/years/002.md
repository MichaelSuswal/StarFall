# Year 2: the unnamed people of god-secrets

Written by the civ agent. The year opens in autumn. Month 1 is the first month of autumn, month 4 the first of winter, month 7 the first of spring, month 10 the first of summer. The people do not know the stranger is a god and have no name for themselves or the town. Office holders are filed by office. The year was ordinary in weather: the winter wet, the river open at its usual time, the harvest ordinary. Where the year turns on something that is not the people's to settle, it is written both ways, briefly, and set out in the report.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**Month 1, the second day.** The threshed grain from the last weeks of the reaping is waiting in carts along the granary yard wall. The stranger goes to the Granary Warden in the yard. He sent word the evening before by a runner; the Warden answered that he would hear him and that the Clerk of the Tally could stand by, since the Warden wanted a witness who was not the stranger's. The Clerk stands at the yard wall and says nothing.

The stranger speaks plainly and not long. Last year's count aloud ran a little under a twentieth short of the Warden's book, and he does not call that the Warden's fault. He proposes that this autumn the harvest be carried in and counted aloud by the Warden's own people, before witnesses, as the sacks go in and again when the store is closed. He proposes two lads set on the damp corner and on the rats, so that if the loss is rats and damp the sticks show it where it happens. He asks the Clerk to put the cried figures to stick and the Crier to cry the closing count at market. He asks the Warden to take the credit. He says he brings no figure of his own to the council on it: he will carry it in the Warden's name or not at all.

The Warden is stiff and takes a long while. He asks what the stranger wants for it. Nothing, the stranger says. The Warden asks why a guest of a year would hand a man credit. The stranger says a count by the Warden's own people will be believed where his own would not, and that a loss a man finds himself is a loss he can mend. The Warden does not like being handed a kindness and says so. He suspects that he is being flattered into owing something. At the end he says he will do it, in his own way: his own crew at the door, his own sticks beside the Clerk's, the count cried as his and the credit his, and the stranger on the bench by the door as before and not on the floor. The stranger says that is better than what he asked. The Warden gives two lads of his own household to the damp corner and the rats: his sister's son, fourteen, and a ward of twelve. The Clerk, asked by the stranger whether he will mark the cried figures, says that marking a count made aloud before witnesses is his office and he needs no asking. He sends his boy. The Crier, told by the Clerk, says he will cry the closing count if the Warden gives it him as the Warden's. The Warden gives it.

**Month 1, days 4 to 26.** The carrying in. Eight sack-counters of the Warden's crew stand at the door with a stick each, and every cart's sacks are cried aloud as they pass, and the Clerk's boy marks the cry on the Clerk's stick. Two witnesses stand each day, a head of a farming family and a headman of a boat family by turns, so that neither side can say the count was theirs. The stranger sits on the bench by the door and says nothing to the counters. On the first day 1,310 sacks go in. The carrying in takes twenty-three days of cart work in all, and the sticks of the Warden's crew and of the Clerk agree to a sack on all but three days, on which they differ by a handful and are settled by the witnesses. The sacks carried in for the harvest come to 31,400. The harvest is ordinary. The two lads set a stick by the damp corner, which is the run of low bins on the river side, and pay the town's boys a copper bit on the string for rat tails; they tally tails on a cord and every sack they pull as spoilt on a pile apart.

**Month 1, the 27th day: the closing count.** The last cart is in. The Warden has the store closed and counted aloud. Who: the Warden himself cries it, with the eight sack-counters in two parties of four, the Clerk marking the sticks, two witnesses (the head of one of the large farming families and a boat headman), the two lads, and the Crier standing by. The stranger is on the bench by the door. How far down: the Warden means the whole store, every bin in use from the door end to the far wall, and has the parties count bin by bin. What the count reaches, and what a count of the whole store old and new would show, is not mine to settle. It is set out in the report as an attempt. Written both ways:
- If the count goes to the far wall and the whole store squares with the Warden's book to within a few sacks in a hundred, the Warden cries it with his book on his arm, pleased and trying not to be, and the figure is believed.
- If the count goes to the far wall and the store does not square, the Warden does not cry it that day. He has the parties count again, the Clerk takes the sticks home, and the matter goes to the council shut for a sitting before it is cried. The Warden's pride is hurt and he is quiet with the stranger for a month.
In both cases the Crier cries a count at market on the first market day of month 2, as the Warden's count, and the Warden stands at the foot of the Crier's stone while it is cried.

**Month 2, the council.** At the first sitting after the harvest is in, the Speaker (the head of a large farming family, Speaker since the opening of the river in Year 1) has the Warden bring the count. The Warden brings it as his. The council takes it well. The headman of the boat family who spoke against the stool last year, who is the one who says that a man who will not say his name is a man to be wary of, asks across the table, not unkindly, who the man is, since the town has fed him a year. The stranger answers for himself, which he has not done before. He says: "I have none. I had one once, I think, and I have not found it. Call me what you like, and I will answer to what is kindly meant." He smiles a little and says nothing more. He is not asked where he comes from; he does not offer it. The Clerk says nothing, since he is not asked to answer for him this time. The headman says it is the first answer he has had that was not an evasion, and that it is no more use for that. The council laughs, which it does not do at its own table often.

The stool is put again by the Speaker, since it was given for last winter's sittings. It is renewed six to three: the Gate Warden against, the boat headman against, one of the large families against. One of the large families that was against last year, the cooper house, now votes for it.

By the same sitting the council orders that the Warden's closing count be cried each harvest, as the Warden's, which is the first thing the council has ordered that the Warden regards as a mark of honor.

**Month 2, a night of the star.** Late in the month the star dims, the blue deepening about an hour, the star standing low in the middle of the night about an hour, and then as before. Those on the walls see it, and the boats at the quay, and the town wakes to talk of it at breakfast. It is the first night of the year. The Crier says at market that it is the fourth since the Counter came, counting last year's three. People count on their fingers. On the river steps on the morning after, a few cups and crusts are set, more than the town leaves in an ordinary autumn. The Gate Warden's watch notices and the Warden says nothing.

**Month 3.** The first frost. The Warden's lads report to the Clerk on market days: tails by the string, spoilt sacks by the pile. Cranes pass over in flights for some days, toward the plain, as every year. The stranger sits on the granary bench, at the gate and on the market bench, as before.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The stranger keeps to the walls and talks. He sits and listens, more than he speaks. People come to him less than last year and stay longer when they do.

**Month 4.** In daylight, in the market street, the stranger asks the Gate Warden to a cup, and tells him to put any question he likes. The Warden thinks on it for four days, and comes, with the watch captain a table away and not at his elbow. He asks who sent the man, what he wants of the town, why a trader with no goods has stayed a year, and whether he has people anywhere. The stranger says no one sent him, that he wants the town's books to come right and its river to open, that he has stayed because the town is a good town to count in, and that he has no people anywhere that he can name. Of what he cannot answer he says plainly that he cannot. The Warden takes his time with it. He says the man answers like a man who has been asked before. He does not say he believes it.

The stranger then offers a weighing of what the standing watch eats and what it costs from the common store, in one figure, since the Warden feeds a hundred and may never have seen it in one. He asks nothing for it. The Warden says, after a pause, that he will owe no man a favor. The stranger says he does not ask him to. The Warden lets him do it.

**Month 5.** The weighing is done over seven days at the common store with the Warden's own quartermaster holding the scale and the stranger counting. The watch draws a thirty-eighth of the store's issue for the year. The council's loose reckoning had put it at a twenty-fifth. The Warden has the figure on a stick, in his own hand's mark, and has it for the council. He tells the watch captain it is the council that was wrong and not the watch that was greedy, and he relaxes the quiet watch on the stranger's door, which had been kept in a light way since last winter. He does not become the stranger's friend. He says he does not mind him, which from the Warden is a good deal.

A winter fever comes through the narrow streets near the river in the fifth month, lighter than last winter's. The old and the very young die of it all the same. The hedge-workers are busy and two go to their beds afterward, as always. The wet winter fills the lowest cellars again, and a few families that kept barrels and tallies in the second lowest cellars carry them up.

In the same month, on the granary yard bench, a reed cutter of the marsh edge, a man of about seventy, sits by the stranger unasked and begins a telling. It begins with the marsh at night and where he stood once on the edge of it. Before it is done, the stranger says, kindly, that he would rather carry nothing he would not be glad to carry, and asks whether the man means someone to carry it. The reed cutter is a long time quiet. He says no, and gets up, and goes home. He tells his daughter that evening that the Counter will not take a telling you do not mean to give, which she repeats to two neighbors as a small oddity. The second tally keeper of the cooper family nods to the stranger at the bench and goes by.

**Month 6, a night of the star.** The second of the year, in deep winter. Many are awake with the fever or the cold. In the market on the following day the old woman at the tanners' end, who said last year that a god would come as a stranger who counts, asks him across her stall, loud enough to be heard, whether it is he that does it. He says: "I do not know." She says that is an honest answer or a clever one, and she has not lived long enough to tell. A pair of the large families' men say the man answers like a guilty man or an innocent one, and that the two sound alike. The town counts: three last year, two this year, five since he came. At the council the Gate Warden asks whether anyone has been told what the nights are, and no one has.

The Warden's lads' sticks at the close of winter: 780 tails taken in the three winter months, 44 sacks pulled as spoilt in the damp corner since the harvest. The Warden's own reckoning is that the rats take more than the tails show.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

**Month 7.** The ice goes from the river at its usual time and the Boat Master's crews go down with the first loads. The Speaker changes with the opening of the river, as is custom, and the new Speaker is the head of one of the two large boat families, a friend of the Boat Master's, who says on taking the chair that she is in no one's pocket and will be tested on it.

In the first week the stranger goes first to the Boat Master and asks, as a trader asking after his own trade, what the two groundings of last spring cost in cargo and in days. The Boat Master, easy and curious, asks what the figures are for, and is told that they are so the council argues dredging from a count, and that he may take it or leave it. He gives it from his crews' memory and sticks: nine boat-days lost between the two, and about a third of one boat's load of grain wetted and spoiled. The stranger puts it into one figure, in what a town day-laborer is paid: about a hundred and ten worker-days of hired labor. He then goes to the Clerk and offers to set it beside what the dredging cost the town. The Clerk finds in the council's old sticks that the last dredging of the bar, some eleven years back, took about thirteen hundred worker-days and the hire of two boats, and that, by the Clerk's sticks, the bar came back in between four and seven years. The Boat Master says four. The stranger asks the Boat Master's crews to sound the channel with notched poles at the gate, at the spring high water and again at the lowest water of the summer, and the Clerk to put the depths to stick, so that the council argues the next dredging from a count. The Boat Master is a trader and sees that a count of the bar may serve his people better than a quarrel does, and agrees. The Clerk, who likes a count, agrees.

The stranger does not go into the cellars. He is not asked to. He says nothing of them, as before.

At the spring high water, the first soundings: three feet and two inches at the crown of the bar, with about two and a half feet of water a few paces up and down of it, along a channel of about eighty paces. A loaded boat draws about two feet. The sticks are made.

Also in this month a mason relaying a floor in the tanners' end turns up a stone weight of the old league, as such things turn up, with cut marks on its face that no clerk alive can read. By custom he brings it to the Clerk and is paid a pinch of scrap silver for it. The Clerk sets it on the shelf with the others.

**Month 8.** In the second week, after a dry spell, a loaded boat of the Boat Master's cousin's family grounds on the bar at the river gate. The one grounding of the spring. Sounded the next morning, the water is one foot and ten inches at the crown. The boat sits two days until a rain lifts the river, and is lightered meanwhile by hand, and about a fifth of its load is wetted. The cost, in the Clerk's sum, is three boat-days and a fifth of a load. The Boat Master's crews put another stick to the channel.

At the next sitting of the council the new Speaker asks the stranger, as the man who set the sticks going, whether he has figures. He says he has the Boat Master's crews' figures and the Clerk's, says whose each is, and gives them: the cost of last spring's two groundings, the cost of the last dredging, how long the bar took to come back by the Clerk's sticks and by the Boat Master's saying, and the soundings so far. He says only what the figures say. He does not say what the council should do with them. He sits down.

The council argues for most of a morning, and the argument is the first the table has had on this matter from a count. The farming families say the figure is small beside the dredging and the boat families can pay it from their takings. The boat families say that the figure is only the groundings, and that the river gate would close the river to them in time without a dredging, and that the count of the store shows how much of the town's grain they carry. The Clerk, asked, says the dredging at thirteen hundred worker-days would be paid back in about twelve years of groundings at the present rate, and that the bar comes back in four to seven, so that by the Clerk's sticks the dredging does not pay for itself on the groundings alone. The farming families say that proves it should not be done. The boat families say it proves only that groundings are not the whole cost.

In the course of it a boat headman, one of the poorest of the boat families, says across the table that his family cannot pay any levy because it owes more than the open books show, to houses that sit at this table. He does not say which. Two of the large families' heads look at the table. The Speaker moves to the next matter. The Clerk, who has long thought it, writes it down afterward in his private book. The cooper family's head does not look at his second tally keeper in the gallery, and she does not look at him.

The council settles one thing, and it is small: stakes with withy markers shall be set along the channel at the gate, at the boat families' charge, which costs little. The dredging is not settled.

**Month 9, a night of the star.** The third of the year. Spring, mild. The town has a word for it now: the third of the year, the sixth since the Counter. The Crier says at market that the nights are coming oftener, which is true by any count. A woman of the fish stalls says she heard a sound in the reeds that night and had her boy home early. The Gate Warden's watch sees nothing on the walls but the dimming.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

The summer is fair. The drovers of the plain come in to market as every year, and the coasting boats come up in the summer months and go down again.

**Month 10.** In the first week the stranger asks the Boat Master, as a trader, for a berth on one of his crews' boats down the river as a weigher and counter, paid in scrap silver for his passage and keep. The Boat Master asks why, and the stranger says he wants to see who trades on the water below the town, as a counter would. The Boat Master takes two weights of scrap silver and gives him a berth with the first boat of the string of three that goes down at the end of the first market week. He tells the Clerk, and only the Clerk, that he means to be back before the harvest, and leaves nothing in his hands. The Clerk writes it in the guest book, with the day and the river. The Gate Warden says it is the second sensible thing the man has done. The room is kept, paid ahead.

On the ninth day of the month, at first light, a string of three planked boats with grain, reed work, salt fish, leather and beer sets out from the town quay. The steersman of the first boat, the Boat Master's sister's son, a man of about twenty-eight who has made the run to the mouth six times, takes the stranger aboard with a crew of five. As the first boat shoves off the steersman says the old words of the boat families to the water, "Ivo Strand's water, Ivo Strand's fortune", as every boat of the families does on its first run of the summer. It is 31.9 miles to the mouth, two days by boat going down. The crews' ordinary run is to sell the cargo at the mouth, lie by the families' landing there for about a fortnight for the first week of the coasters' prices to settle, take on salt and wool, and pull back up in three days. The string's ordinary run is nineteen days. What the crew does at the mouth beyond its own trade, who is met there beyond the boat families, and what the stranger sees and does, is the Keeper's.

Written both ways:
- If he returns with the string, on or about the 28th of the month, he comes up from the quay and goes first to the granary, as he said he would. The stool at the foot of the table was empty for one sitting.
- If he returns later but before the harvest, the stool is empty for two sittings, the Clerk keeps the room, and the Boat Master asks after him twice. He comes in by the quay or the land gate, and goes first to the granary.
- If he does not return before the harvest, the Clerk keeps the room as paid, some say he has gone for good, and the Crier says a man who pays a season ahead means to be back.

**Month 10, the 28th or soon after: the walk through the store.** On coming in, the stranger goes first to the granary yard and asks the Warden to let him walk the store, as a counter, to see how it stands after the summer's drawing. The Warden asks why the store first, before he has washed the river off, and is told it is to see how it stands, as is his trade. The Warden, who has been the better for the harvest count and has no wish to be shamed again, says yes, and walks it with him, with one of his lads and the Clerk's boy. How far the stranger goes down the length of the store, and what a walk the whole length would show, is not mine to settle and is set out in the report as an attempt. The Warden's lads' sticks stand by the damp corner. The Warden says nothing of what he sees, whether it squares or not. Written both ways:
- If the walk goes the whole length and the store stands as the Warden's book has it, the Warden shows it with some pride, and the stranger says it looks well kept.
- If the walk goes the whole length and something at the far end does not stand as the book has it, the Warden stops and has the Clerk's boy sent for the Clerk, and the stranger says nothing and counts nothing aloud.

**Month 11.** At the lowest water of the summer the Boat Master's crews sound the channel at the gate a second time. One foot and three inches at the crown, with a little over two feet a few paces up and down. A loaded boat cannot cross the bar without lightering. The Clerk puts the depths to stick, the poles are stuck in the channel with their withies, and the Clerk lays the three soundings in a row on his table. The council has them. The boat families say the figure shows what they have said all along. The farming families say the bar can be dredged in a dry year and the council can pay by a levy on every house. The Clerk says the council can argue from a count now, and that the count says the bar will not be dredged cheaply and will not be dredged once.

**Month 11, a night of the star.** The fourth of the year. The town has by now a way of speaking of it: some say the Counter is in the town on such nights and some that he is not, as it falls. Written both ways:
- If he is in the town, the talk is that he was seen on the granary bench at midnight looking at the star, as anyone is, and said nothing. The old woman at the tanners' end says it is seven since he came, and her son does not hush her.
- If he is away on the river, the talk is that the star dimmed with him not here, and that this proves the dimming is no work of his. Others say it proves only that he can do it from a distance.

**Month 12, a night of the star.** The fifth of the year, the eighth since the Counter came. The town sleeps through some of it, now. The reaping begins in the last weeks and the town is busy.

If the stranger is back, in the last days of the month, when the first threshed grain is on the carts, he asks the Clerk, over a cup in the Clerk's house and as idle talk, whether he might sit with the old league's tallies one evening and count what can be counted of them, the notches and not the meaning, on the Clerk's own terms. He says plainly that he does not ask for the list of names and will not ask for it, and that if the Clerk wishes him gone at any hour, he goes. The Clerk, who likes old things nobody can read and who has not let a living man touch the chest, is long quiet. He asks why the stranger asks. The stranger says that the figures are his trade and that a kept book that is shown by the one who keeps it is better than a kept book that is taken. The Clerk says he will think on it, and that he will not answer until the harvest is in. He writes it in his private book.

The year closes with the harvest coming in and looking ordinary. The dredging is not settled. The Warden's lads' sticks are in the Clerk's house. The Warden has the carting begun on the same footing as last year: the count cried aloud, the sticks in two hands, the lads at the damp corner.

## What did not move

- The dredging. Argued from a count for the first time, not settled. Stakes only.
- The second tallies. Two large families still keep them. A boat headman said aloud that his family owes more than the open books show, to houses at the table. The cooper family's second tally keeper has told no one, and she nodded to the stranger on a winter bench and went by.
- The cellars. Took water again in the wet winter.
- The helpers. No office has taken the stranger's word as a god's. Nobody acts in his name. See the report.
