# Report to the Keeper: civ unnamed-people-secrets, Year 1

## How the god was met

Autumn, month 1, day 1. Sunrise: a sentry on the east wall saw a figure in a brown coat standing on the rise by the Iss Track [f37] for the early morning, motionless, turning its head to the smoke, the track and the river. Mid morning the man came to the land gate and asked the guest's three days. The watchman on the gate was told he came by the track from the south-west, was a trader with scrap silver and brass weights, and had no name for the book. The watchman called him the Counter to the next man. The Clerk of the Tally entered him as a guest with a mark of his own drawing, scales over a short stroke, and the day. The man paid for food and a bed in scrap silver at a house near the market street that lets rooms to traders, and was given a place on the granary yard bench.

Days 2 and 3. The Granary Warden, with the Clerk standing by, showed him the harvest and store books, not the council's own books. He told them plainly of a difference of a few measures in a hundred between the Warden's book and the sticks, and urged a count aloud on the floor and a count cried at market. He asked it as a favor.
- Clerk of the Tally: heard him, said he would think on it, and later sent a boy asking whether he reads. He does not. The Clerk did the count and gave it to the Crier. The Clerk did not take the stranger's word as a god's and does not know there is a god. He took his counsel as a trader's.
- Granary Warden: stiff, took a second pair of eyes for the bins and no more. Ran the count in month 2 with witnesses and found a little under a twentieth short. Took the counsel. Did not take the word of anything beyond it.
- Gate Warden: cool, said a guest after three days is a council matter, put a quiet watch on the room. Did not take his word.
- Boat Master: easy and curious, took up a weighing of a cargo of grain for the south. Took his counsel on that, as a trader's. Did not take his word.
- Crier: amused, will cry what the Clerk gives him. Cried the count at market in month 2.

Late month 3. The Clerk brought him before the council as a guest who had done the town a service. The council gave him a stool at the foot of the long table, on the stores only, five to four. He is not on the rolls.

Winter. He sat at the granary yard, the gate and the market bench, was asked three times at the table for a figure, and gave it. In the deep of the winter a woman who keeps the second tally for the cooper family (see `people/second-tally-keeper-of-the-cooper-family.md`) sat by him on the granary yard bench unasked and told him the matter of the second tallies and that her sum does not close. He did not stop her. She asked whether the sum closed. He said it did not. How many such tellings the god holds is the god's to say. By my count it was one, and the only one.

Spring. In month 7 the Clerk, asked over a cup about old unreadable things and whether the rolls hold any name, told him: the league tallies in a chest in the Clerk's house, the walkers' sung list of names in a private book he will not show, twenty and some names unlike any on the rolls. In month 8 the stranger walked open ground with a small bronze thing in his hand, watched at a distance by a man the Gate Warden sent.

Summer, month 10. He told the Clerk only that he would be gone about a fortnight down the Iss Track to the south-west and back by the harvest, and left nothing in his hands.

No office holder has taken the stranger's word as a god's. All five met him; the Clerk and the Granary Warden took his counsel as a trader's, the Boat Master took one weighing, the Gate Warden did not, the Crier cried what the Clerk gave him. No office is the god's helper as of Year 1. If the Keeper holds that taking a counsel in the course of trade is taking the word, that is the Keeper's ruling, not mine.

## What was done in the god's name

Nothing. No office acted in his name, and none was asked to. The Clerk gave the count to the Crier as the Clerk's own count and the Crier cried it as such. The council's stool was given by the council in its own right.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

1. The search of open ground in month 8. The stranger walked the rise, the verge of the Iss Track for a mile each way, the public ground by the river gate and the strip of common between the east wall and the marsh, with a small bronze object in his hand. A watcher sent by the Gate Warden saw him stop at four places. Success would mean something was found on open ground that was put there on purpose (a cache, a sealed cellar, a closed road); failure, nothing. Circumstances from the record that bear on it: the town is old and says it has always been there; the lowest cellars were dug before the first boat family came and belonged to merchants of an old league whose weights turn up under floors; the walkers of the old people's days came in by the land gate; the lowest cellars take water. The year file writes it both ways.

2. The journey. He set out in month 10 at first light on a market day, on foot, by the Iss Track to the south-west as a trader with his scales, for about a fortnight, meaning to be back by the harvest. I give no distance or time. The Keeper prices the trip and whether he is back before the year's end. The year file writes it both ways.

3. Whether anyone could have taken the key from his room in the winter, after the dog's night. Nobody entered the room as far as my people's account goes: the woman who lets it was in her own rooms and heard him cough at midnight, the door was shut as he left it, and the Gate Warden's quiet watch had been relaxed. This turns on a power (the dog form and the key) and I leave it to the Keeper.

## What turns on a power

- The dog. A brown dog the town does not know was seen in the alleys behind the market street and along the marsh edge, in the dead of the winter, by the river quay watchman and by a bone-setter at the marsh edge, and by a boy by the tanners' yard. The town's dogs did not bark at it. It stopped at the doors of two hedge-workers' houses and looked at them. The bone-setter woke to it lying across her threshold and gave it water. The stranger came down late the next day, slow and short of words, which the woman who lets his room took for a hard night's drinking. What the dog form did to the hedge-workers or to the town's dogs, if anything, and whether any hedge-worker felt anything, is the Keeper's.
- The three dimmed nights of the star. My people take them as a thing that happens. Two fell in month 1 near the stranger's coming and one in month 5. Whether the god's coming caused any of them is the Keeper's. The people think the stranger and the dimming are connected only by gossip.
- The bronze object in his hand in month 8. What it did or showed is the Keeper's. My people saw only a man holding something small that caught the light.

## What touches anyone else

- Trade: unchanged in kind. Drovers from the Ansel Plain [r04] brought stock as always; coasting boats came in summer. The river gate bar grounded loaded boats twice in the spring.
- Travelers: only the stranger, who set out for the south-west by the Iss Track in month 10.
- Parties sent out: none. No messages carried. Word has gone down the river on the boats as a curiosity: a counter sits at the foot of the council's table in the town on the bend. This is talk, not a message.
- Nothing carried to any other people. No migrants of note.

## Asked of the god

The questions and requests mortals put to him, in their words as near as I can set them, and whether he answered in front of them.
1. Land gate watchman, first day: "What name for the book?" Answered: none, plainly, with a small smile.
2. Land gate watchman: "Where have you come from?" Answered: by the track from the south-west.
3. Watch captain or Clerk, first day: "What are you carrying that brings you to this gate?" Answered: scrap silver, brass weights, scales.
4. Granary Warden, day 2: "Why would you see our bins?" Answered: that he is a trader and a counter and would like to see how a town this size keeps its count.
5. Gate Warden, day 3: "Who are your people, and why does a trader come with no goods?" Answered: that he trades in counting. The Warden did not like the answer.
6. Boat Master, day 3: "What did you see on the river?" Answered: which boats were out and which way they carried what.
7. Clerk, by a boy with a message: "Do you read?" Answered by the god: no, only counts.
8. Council, month 3 (put by those against): "Who is he that will not give a name?" Not answered by the god in front of them. The Clerk answered that he is a guest who has done the town a service.
9. Woman who keeps the second tally, winter, the bench: "Does the sum close?" Answered: it does not.
10. Clerk, month 7: "Why do you ask?" (of the old things). Answered: that he likes old things that cannot be read.
11. The Gate Warden's man behind him in month 8: no word passed.

Offers the god put, for the record: a counting, a weighing, a second pair of eyes. Requests to the town: a bench in the market street or granary yard; a hearing; a count cried; a place on a bench at the long table; hired lodging past guest right.

## Names said aloud

- "The Counter" (a nickname for the stranger), said by the land gate watchman to the next man on the gate, day 1, month 1. Not a name the stranger gave.
- No name has been said aloud for my people, the town, or any office holder. The Clerk of the Tally, the Granary Warden, the Gate Warden, the Boat Master, the Crier and the Speaker stay unnamed. The woman who keeps the cooper family's second tally is unnamed. No one has said the name Ivo Strand aloud this year, as far as I have set down.
