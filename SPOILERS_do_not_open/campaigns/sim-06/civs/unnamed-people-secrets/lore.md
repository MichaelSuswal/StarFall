# What the unnamed people now say: Year 1

Written by the civ agent. This is the people's own telling, and it is wrong about things that matter. No god is named by the people, because none is known to them.

## The stranger
- They call him the Counter. A watchman on the land gate said it on his first day and the market took it up. Many do not know he has no name in the book, only a mark.
- They say he came down the track from the south-west with scales on his belt and no goods, paid his way, kept to himself, and never asked for anything the town would not give a guest. That is true, and they think it dull, which is why they find it odd.
- They say the books come right behind him. This began as a market joke after the count was cried. An old woman at the tanners' end said that the old people said a god would come as a stranger who counts, and that you would know him by the way the books come right. Most laugh. Some do not. Her son hushes her. What the people do not see is that the count was cried and made right because the Clerk and the Warden did it aloud before witnesses, and that the stranger was on a bench at the door saying nothing. They credit his arithmetic, not their own count.
- The Clerk says a book comes right when a man counts it. The Gate Warden says a man with no name is a man to be watched, and that the town does not need a god to count its own bins.
- The stranger gave no name and was a quiet man. They say a man who will not give his name has something to be quiet about, a debt, a crime or a family, and they argue which. This is wrong about what matters.

## The dim star
- The star dimmed on three nights, two close together in the first month and one in the fifth. The town has seen such nights in other years and does not trouble over them.
- This year the market talked about it more because the stranger came the same month. Some say the stranger brings the dimming. Some say the dimming brought the stranger. Some say the marsh folk walk across the face of the star on their night errands and block it a while. The old say the star dims when the river is low. None of these is right, and none has anything to do with the river.

## The dog
- The river quay watchman and a bone-setter at the marsh edge both tell of a brown dog the town's dogs did not bark at, in the dead of the winter. A boy tells of it a month late. They tell it as a dog story and not in the same place. The talk is that it was a marsh dog, or a ghost of one, or a messenger of the odd folk of the Iss Marsh [f36]. Nobody connects it to the stranger.

## Their own rulers
- The council: the stool at the foot of the table, given by five to four, is the council's own business and a mark of the Clerk's judgment, they say. The four who were against say it will end badly. The five say it ended the argument over the store, which is true and not the cause they think.
- The count cried at market is now talked of as a custom, as if it had always been done. It was done for the first time in living memory. People say the old Clerks did it, and are wrong about that.
- The dredging: the farming families say the boat families should pay, because the count shows the boat families send the most grain down the river. The boat families say the count is a stranger's count and no friend of theirs. Both are partly right and both blame the Counter for it.

## Their neighbors
- Drovers from the plain bring stock as always and say nothing new. Coasting boats came in summer and went down again, and spoke of the harbor town on the Garrow Shore [r01] as they do.
- Word has gone down the river on the boats that a counter sits at the foot of the council's table in the town on the bend. It is told as a curiosity.

## The old things
- The Clerk still keeps the league tallies in a chest and the walkers' names in a private book. The clerks argue over both this winter as they do every winter. The stranger asked about them, and word of that went round the clerks and nowhere else. Some clerks say he asked because he can read them. He said he cannot read at all.

## The odd folk of the marsh
- They say the odd folk deal by night, and that they were here before any of the town's people. Nothing new this year but the dog.

# What the unnamed people now say: Year 2

Written by the civ agent. This is the people's own telling, and it is wrong about things that matter. No god is named by the people, because none is known to them.

## The Counter
- They say he answered for himself at the council table, at last, in a sentence: that he has no name and had one once, and will answer to what is kindly meant. The market makes this into two stories. The kind one is that he is a man who lost a name in a war or a flood and is ashamed. The unkind one is that it is the sentence of a man who has practised it. Neither story is true, and each leaves out that he did not ask anyone to believe either.
- They say he made the Granary Warden's count, and the Warden's pride was never his. The town, in fact, gives the Warden the count: the Warden's count, cried at the Warden's own stone, is spoken of as if the Warden always did it, and as if the Warden thought of it. The old woman at the tanners' end says the Counter does not take what is owed, which is a thing the town likes. This is wrong about what matters, which is that the count was made in the Warden's name on the stranger's urging and the town's belief in it rests on a thing it does not see.
- They say he will not take a telling you do not mean to give. A reed cutter's daughter began it, and it has gone round the benches. It is taken for a rule of courtesy. They are wrong about what matters: he does not refuse tellings out of courtesy to the teller.
- They say the Gate Warden has taken him to a cup, which is thought a wonder. The Warden says he did not take the stranger to a cup, the stranger took him. The watch say the Warden has gone soft. He has not.
- They say he went down the river in the summer on the Boat Master's string, as a weigher. How the town tells his coming back depends on when he came, which the Keeper prices; if he is back, the boat families say the Counter has seen the mouth now and will want to count it. The farming families say a man who sails away at the start of summer is a man who is not tied to the town. Both say this with a smile and a shrug.

## The dim star
- It dimmed five times this year and the town has stopped saying it does not trouble. People count: three last year, five this, eight since the Counter came. The Crier says it is a count he does not like to cry.
- They say the stranger brings it. Some say it dims only when he is in the town. If the night of the eleventh month fell while he was away on the river, those some say he must do it from a distance. The old woman at the tanners' end asked him to his face and he said he does not know. She says that is an honest answer or a clever one, and that either would be a thing a god might say.
- Some say the marsh folk walk across the star's face on their night errands and block it a while. Some say the star is old and is going out and the stranger came to see it out. A fish stall woman says she heard a sound in the reeds on the night in the ninth month. A bone-setter at the marsh edge said her hands were cold after the night of the sixth month, and no one else said it of theirs. The old say the star dims when the river is low, and now say it dims when the river is high, and have not noticed that they have changed.
- More cups and crusts are set on the river steps after such nights than the town has ever done in an ordinary autumn. They call it manners.

## Their own rulers
- The Granary Warden: the town says the Warden has found his feet. They say he has counted the store himself and stood at the stone for it, which is true, and that the rats and the damp took the old twentieth and that is the end of it, which is not known. The Warden says the rats take more than the tails show.
- The Gate Warden: they say he is a hard man and has been proved fair. He has not changed his mind about the stranger.
- The council: they say the stool was kept six to three because the Counter's count is a good count. The six say the stool is for the stores, and has done good. The three say it is the thin end of a thing. The boat headman who asked who the man is says he got an honest answer, and it was no more use.
- The Boat Master: they say he has been the stranger's friend since the weighing, and that he took him on a crew's boat and a passage's silver for it. The boat families say he is too easy with strangers.
- The Speaker (from the opening of the river, the head of a large boat family): they say she is her own woman. She proved it by asking the stranger for figures and not letting either side shout.

## The dredging and the river
- The figures are now spoken of in the market as if they had always been counted. People say the bar is a hand deeper in spring and a hand shallower in summer, which is near the truth. They say the council has agreed to put stakes in the channel and so has done something, which is true.
- The boat families say the bar closes in four years and the Clerk's sticks say between four and seven. The farming families say the boat families made the bar. They say the boat families' boats scour it as they cross, which is not so.
- The poorest boat family's headman said at the table that his house owes more than the open books show to houses at the table. The market has not stopped talking of it. The market's guess is the coopers and the tanners, and the market is right, though it does not know why.

## The river below the town
- They say a counter has gone down the river with the boat families' string and come back. The boat families tell it as a curiosity: the Counter weighed the mouth. They say he is a trader after all.
- The farming families say the river below the town is the boat families' business, and they have never asked what is there.

## The old things
- A mason turned up a league weight in the tanners' end. The Clerk paid for it as is custom and put it on the shelf. The clerks say the marks on its face are the same as the marks on the tallies, and they cannot read either. The old say a weight turns up when the river is low. This is not so.
- The Clerk was asked at the end of the summer by the stranger, over a cup, whether he might sit with the tallies. The Clerk has said he will think on it until the harvest is in. Only the Clerk and the stranger know of it, so it is not yet a thing the town says.

## The odd folk of the marsh
- They say they were here before any of the town's people, and that they deal by night. A reed cutter began to tell a thing to the stranger on a bench in the winter, and did not finish it. His daughter says it was about the marsh at night. Nobody else knows more.

# What the unnamed people now say: Year 3

Written by the civ agent. This is the people's own telling, and it is wrong about things that matter. No god is named by the people, because none is known to them. Where the Year 2 telling above says the Warden's count was cried at the stone, the Keeper's line is that the cry was held for the council; the town knew only that it was held, and in the winter of Year 2 the market said the Counter's count had not come right. That is the town's own mistake about whose count it was.

## The hollow bins
- They say the far bins were hollow behind a wall of sacks, and that the Warden said so himself at the council table and the Crier cried it. This is true, and they are proud of it as of a thing the town did. They say it has never happened before, which is true of the saying and not known of the thing.
- They say the Warden is an honest man who walked nothing for nineteen years. The cooper stall says the Warden lost a fifth of a harvest. The boat families say he found it. Both say it of one fact.
- Where the grain went, the town has every answer. The east fields house says it was robbed or drawn and not struck, in the Warden's uncle's day. The old say the rats took it, which cannot be, since rats do not face a wall in sacks, and they say it anyway. The farming families say the carters. The boat families say the farming houses carted it at night and sold it. A fish stall woman says the marsh folk carry grain off by night. A number say the star dims for a short bin: that the two things come from one cause, and the cause is hunger coming. None of these is known to be right. Some of them are wrong about the thing that matters, which is that the roll was never walked and anyone's story could stand on it.
- The boys had a rhyme about the hollow bins. It lasted three weeks.
- The count is now spoken of as the town's own custom, cried at the Warden's stone. They say the Warden stood at the foot of it with his hands folded and did not look at anyone. This is true.

## The Counter
- They say the Counter found the hollow bins. This is half true and the town draws the wrong half: they say he found it and made the Warden say it. The Warden says he said it himself, and the market does not hear him.
- They say the Clerk let him into the chest. The boy told the boy, who told his mother. They say the Clerk gave him the league tallies, which he did not; they say the Clerk has given the Counter the walkers' names, which he did not. Some clerks say the Counter can read the old marks after all. The Clerk says he counted, and that the man counted better than the Clerk. The market takes that for a sign.
- They say he does not take a telling you do not mean to give. They now say it is because the Counter is afraid to be told things that could not be unheard. This is wrong about why.
- They say a child asked him if he makes the star dim and he said he did not know. A fish stall woman says a child may ask what a council may not. The boat families say he is a good-natured man and that is all there is to it.
- They say he went up the river in the summer on the cousin's family's boats, as a weigher, for a month. The boat families tell it as a curiosity, as they told the mouth. The farming families say a man who goes away twice in two summers and comes back twice is a man with somewhere to be. The Gate Warden says nothing.
- They say the books come right behind him. The old woman at the tanners' end says it again at her stall, and her son does not hush her.

## The dim star
- It dimmed six times this year, once in the first month, a pair in the fifth, a pair in the seventh and one in the eighth. The town counts fourteen since the Counter came, and speaks of it more as one speaks of a thing in the house than of the sky. The Crier says it is a count he does not like to cry and cries it.
- They say the Counter brings it. They say it comes oftener since he had the far bins opened. They say it dims for a short bin. They say the marsh was loud on the second night of the pair in the seventh month. The old say the star dims when the river is low, and also when it is high, and when the river is middling, and have not noticed.
- A bone-setter at the marsh edge says her hands were dull for a day after the second night in the fifth month. A woman who finds strayed beasts says the same. Nobody else says it. The two do not say it together.
- More cups and crusts are set on the river steps after such nights than the town has ever set in an ordinary year. They call it manners.

## Their own rulers
- The Granary Warden: they say he has been proved honest by being shamed in public. He says the shame was a thing he carried forward from his uncle and that he walks the store now. He is spoken of as a man who stood at his own stone and said it. He does not like to be spoken of.
- The two families of the far end: the cooper house is thought to be smooth and the east fields house to be loud, and the town thinks neither is the whole of it. The east fields head says he was robbed. He is believed more than he is liked.
- The Clerk: they say he is the one man in the town who has not been wrong in public. He has been, in the dark, about a notch.
- The council: they say the motion to search the Warden's house had no second and that this shows the council has sense. It shows the cooper head looked at his hands.
- The new Speaker, the head of the west fields family: they say he holds the table to counts. He says he does.
- The Gate Warden: they say he asked the Counter a plain question at the watch's fire and got a plain answer and did not like it. This is true.

## The dredging and the river
- The soundings are spoken of as if they had always been counted. They say the bar is three inches shallower than last year, which is so, and that the river is closing, which is likely, and that the council has made a rule, which is true. Some say the rule means the dredging will be done in the spring. It will be done only if the count says so.
- The boat families say the bar closes in four years. The farming families say the boat families made the bar. The latter is not so.
- The toll of one copper bit on every loaded boat and cart is spoken of as the Counter's toll, which it is not. It was the council's, put by the Speaker.

## The river above the town
- They say little of it. The boat families go up each summer with salt and beer and come down with what they took on at the landing at the head. They say the Counter has now seen both ends of the water. The farming families say they have never asked what is up the river and do not mean to.

## The old things
- The market says the Counter counted the league tallies in the Clerk's house on a night of frost. They say the tallies are a count of grain, of years, of dead men or of debts, and argue which, as they have argued every winter.
- The clerks say the old walkers sang their lists of names for three nights, and that the Counter has been in the town for three years. They say it to one another and not to the market.

## The odd folk of the marsh
- They say they were here before any of the town's people, and that they deal by night. The reed cutter who began a telling on the bench two winters ago died this winter. His daughter is said to have kept what he began. Nobody knows what it was.
