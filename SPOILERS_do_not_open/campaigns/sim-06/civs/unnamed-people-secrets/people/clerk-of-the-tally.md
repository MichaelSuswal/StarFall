# The Clerk of the Tally (unnamed)

Filed by office; no name has been said aloud. Fixed in Year 1.

## Nature
About fifty-eight years old in Year 1. Dry, exact and proud, a man who has kept the council's books for twenty-six years and the rolls of who is entered for as long. Courteous to the point of coldness. He distrusts anyone who counts better than he does and is drawn to them for the same reason. He likes old things that cannot be read, and holds that the town's pride and shame are the same chest of league tallies in his own house.

## History
Born in the town to a clerk's family; his father and his father's father held the office before him. Learned the tally sticks and the clay and wax marks as a boy. Never learned to read the league marks, which his grandfather said could once be read by any clerk, and which he has argued over every winter of his life. Keeps, in a book that is not the council's, the list of names the walkers sang in the market street in the old people's days, some twenty and more. Keeps also a private book of what he is told and by whom.

## Year 1
- Entered the stranger in the guest book on the first day with a mark of his own drawing, a pair of scales over a short stroke.
- Was the first office holder the stranger met in private. Heard him out and said he would think on it. Took the cried count to the Crier and to the council.
- Brought the stranger before the council in the third month as a guest who had done the town a service.
- In the seventh month, asked over a cup about old things nobody can read and whether the rolls hold a name, told the stranger more than he meant: where the league tallies are kept, the walkers' story, that the names are in a private book he will not show, that there are twenty and some and that they are unlike any name on the rolls. Wrote down afterward what he had said.
- Does not take the stranger's word as a god's. Regards him as a counter of unusual skill who tells no lies he can find and gives no name. Watches him the more for it.
- The Clerk is the office through which any helper arrangement would first run, since he keeps the council's books and the guest book and knows the stranger best of any office holder.

## Standing
Respected, not loved. Sits at the council's long table by custom without a vote. Will be asked for in any matter of the rolls.
