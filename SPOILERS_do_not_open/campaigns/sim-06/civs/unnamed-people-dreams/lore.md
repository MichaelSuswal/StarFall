# What the people now say, end of Year 4

These are the people's own tellings. They explain causes, and are wrong about something that matters in at least one of them; I do not read the Keeper's truth, so where the error lies is for the Keeper to mark. Items kept from earlier years are marked as such.

## About the guest
- That the guest with the lamp has come, as the first of the seated line promised in a dream, and that she came on the first morning because the dark had a keeper who had waited long enough. The old tale never named a hind, and some on the Gate Bench say so. (Year 1, still told.)
- That her lamp holds off fever and the night, and a house with the flame is a house no sick child dies in the dark of. About eighteen died in kept houses this winter; the households say "kept and not lost". (Year 1, still told.)
- That she walks in the sleep of the dying and leaves her body standing pale in the yard, and that it can be moved by the cord at her waist. The wall's men now say they know the standing body from the lying one, by its murmur. The households who say she is a traveller's ghost say a ghost's body is where it fell. (Year 3, told with more certainty.)
- That she cannot be killed, and that if her body is broken she comes back at the nearest house with a lamp, and that in this town every house has a lamp burning through the night so the nearest house is any house. The lowest streets near the guest house yard say she will come back to their street, and some say as a child of the house; they keep their lamps bright. The wall's men say the break does not matter. The Bench elder against her says a god that can be broken and mended is a guest that needs the town. (Year 4.)
- That she is a giant's kin. The cutters say she looked at the lowest cuts as one reads a name, or as one looks at a stranger's hand, and the ring of stones round the three cuts is said to be for her. (Year 2, still told.)
- That she is afraid, and her fear makes the flames dip. The wall watch say it is at the same hour each dawn and they have stopped being surprised. (Year 1, still told.)
- That she asks and does not order, and that this makes her a true guest. The Warden of the Wall says a thing done without an order is a thing no one can be held to, and the watch repeat it. (Year 2, still told.)
- That she does not see what happens in the town unless she is told, and that she carried a wrong word to a door because no one told her it was wrong. Others say that she stood at the second house's door and said she was sorry and asked who gave her the word, and that a keeper who does that is a keeper who can be trusted with a word. The second house says a plain thing was plainly said and that the quay roll was wrong, and is not sure which of them was right. (Year 3 and 4.)
- That the flame may be passed from niche to niche by anyone, and that she bade it so. The Bench's ruling that a flame is lit only at a household's own asking is told as the Bench's. (Year 3, still told.)
- That when the town asked what it would do if she were away she was going west to the god in the delta, and that a keeper who asks what the court would do without her is a keeper who has made up her mind. The seat says she asked so she might be back. The old woman of the lower town who keeps the old tale says the guest will walk out one night in the dark as she came. (Year 4.)
- That the warm wind after the hard frost in month 6 was hers, a breath she let go for the thaw, because the guest had kept three households that night. The elder against her says wind follows frost in any country. (Year 4.)

## About the strange things of the year
- The star dimmed on seven nights, twenty-one in all since she came. The cord says seven of the twenty-one had a dying in the town. The town says: it dims for the dying of any house; it dims when she is afraid or lying down; it dims when she is awake in the yard; it dims when she is in a hamlet and the hamlet is under it too; it dimmed for the barrow singer's song. The young say the star pays for the flames, and the roll of kept houses has gone from three hundred and thirty to four hundred and fifteen to five hundred and twenty to about five hundred and ninety as the nights went three, five, six, seven. The old say it dimmed in their grandfathers' time on nights when nothing happened, and that more nights are not different nights. The Keeper of the Count says two things that rise together are two things that rise together and the cord does not say why. (Year 1 to 4.)
- That the iron went soft because the star dims and the blue gets into it. The smiths say the mining town is sending its iron down before it is worked enough. The lowest streets say a god's lamp is near iron and iron goes soft near a god. The Warden of the Wall says it is the iron and not the guest. The carriers say they carry what they are given. The cutters say the harbor's stone will use up the picks. (Year 3 and 4.)
- That the west is empty: the party that went to the hills saw no one, and the town says no one lives there. This is told as certain. Four people walked and did not see. (Year 2, still told.)
- That the harbor wants the scar's stone because it is the giants' stone and holds against the sea. The cutters are proud of it and the old cutter says the harbor wants stone and has not said what for. (Year 3, still told.)
- That the last third of the third barrow song went into the barrows with the oldest of the barrow singers, and that the song is now two parts and a gap. The storytelling households say that a keeper who walks a dying sleep might have taken the song out of her sleep as a cord is lifted from a hook, and that no one asked, and that it is a shame on the household that did not. The singer's own house says she would not be walked in. The young households sing what they have, and stop at the gap. (Year 4.)
- That the one in the delta who reads scales is the second guest, and that the old tale never said one. The storytelling households say there were two promised and the first came by the river and the second by the road. The lowest streets say a god who sits at the scales weighs us. The Bench elder against the guest says two gods in a river country are one more than it has bread for. The Warden of the Wall says nothing and keeps the wall. (Year 3 and 4.)

## About their rulers
- That the seat speaks only what she has weighed, and that she holds the bargain with the harbor as the town's and not the guest's, and that a ruler who says so is a ruler. (Year 3, still told.)
- That the new Warden of the Door has said she takes the guest for the awaited, and that a woman who keeps two lamps keeps one mind. The Bench elder against the guest says she may keep two minds yet. (Year 4.)
- That the old Warden of the Door told the guest her son was home and she carried a wrong word anyway, and that it is the court's roll that was wrong and not the old man. (Year 4.)
- That the Warden of the Wall asked the guest the same thing two years ago and has his answer, and that he is not easy with it. He says the wall holds what sleeps within it, and that a ruler who asks what is to be done when she is gone is a ruler, though he has not said so to her. (Year 3 and 4.)
- That the Steward of the Store sent nine this time and a third boat because the stone is heavy and he did not trust the bars, and sent no spears because the seat said so. He counts the winter aloud now without being asked. (Year 4.)
- That the Gate Bench fined two fathers a day's labor for their lads over the yard wall again and was right to, and that it holds a flame is lit at a household's asking and in no other way. (Year 3 and 4.)
- That the Keeper of the Count waited a year for one line and laid three cords on the table in month 2 and would not say what they meant, and that this was wise. He now writes lying beside the cord's old word asleep. (Year 4.)
- That the old cutter said no more than a fifth of a year's stone should go to the harbor because the wall is the town's and the sea is not. (Year 3, still told.)

## About neighbors
- That the court has sent a third party to the harbor, with sixty blocks and the guest's word to the one in the delta, and that it is the guest's doing and the court's own. The town says the party will bring salt or the terms for it. Nothing is known in the town beyond that at the year's end except as the Keeper's pass two says.
- That the carriers know more than they say. This is said of carriers in any year. This year it is said they are more careful about the iron than the salt.
- That the harbor sells salt cheap because the sea gives it free, and that the carriers keep the price up. (Year 2, still told.)

## About themselves
- That the Night Sitter and the guest sit well together, and that the guest is good with a kettle. The Night Sitter says she sleeps more, and says the hamlet girl found she could by the thaw and that a sitter is not taught. The hamlet girl, when asked, said she would sit no more than two nights in ten. The lowest streets say the court is feeding the guest's pupils. (Year 3 and 4.)
- That the Dusk Table sets the guest's place now, and that a town that counts its own dim nights is a town that has begun to count its gods. (Year 2, still told.)
- That a roll nobody struck led a god to a father's door, and the Steward has had the roll read over by two. The town says this is how small a thing it takes. (Year 4.)
