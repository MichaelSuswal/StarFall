# What the people now say, end of Year 5

These are the people's own tellings. They explain causes, and are wrong about something that matters in at least one of them; I do not read the Keeper's truth, so where the error lies is for the Keeper to mark. Items kept from earlier years are marked as such.

## About the guest
- That the guest with the lamp has come, as the first of the seated line promised in a dream, and that she came on the first morning because the dark had a keeper who had waited long enough. The old tale never named a hind, and some on the Gate Bench say so. (Year 1, still told.)
- That her lamp holds off fever and the night, and a house with the flame is a house no sick child dies in the dark of. About nineteen died in kept houses this winter; the households say "kept and not lost". (Year 1, still told.)
- That she walks in the sleep of the dying and leaves her body standing pale in the yard, and that it can be moved by the cord at her waist. The wall's men say they know the standing body from the lying one by its murmur, and now write what the lying one does: that it breathes slow and even, that the ears do not turn, that it does not stir to a hand. The households who say she is a traveller's ghost say a ghost's body is where it fell. (Year 3, told with more certainty.)
- That she does not know what her own body is when it lies still, and has said so to the wall's face. The wall's men say it is the plainest thing she has said. The storytelling households say a keeper who does not know what lies in her own yard is a keeper who is still arriving. The elder against her says a god that does not know her own body is a guest that needs the town. The lowest streets say it means she may come back lacking. (Year 5.)
- That she cannot be killed, and that if her body is broken she comes back at the nearest house with a lamp, and that the nearest house is any house, since every house keeps a lamp through the night. This year she told the Warden of the Wall the house nearest is the one that keeps a lamp through the night, and need not be kept for her; the wall's men say that is what they said. The lowest streets near the yard say she will come back to their street, and some say as a child of the house, and that she may come back lacking a name or a song and not know which. (Year 4 and 5.)
- That she is a giant's kin. The cutters say she looked at the lowest cuts as one reads a name, or as one looks at a stranger's hand, and the ring of stones round the three cuts is said to be for her. (Year 2, still told.)
- That she is afraid, and her fear makes the flames dip. The wall watch say it is at the same hour each dawn and they have stopped being surprised. (Year 1, still told.)
- That she asks and does not order, and that this makes her a true guest. The Warden of the Wall says a thing done without an order is a thing no one can be held to, and the watch repeat it. (Year 2, still told.)
- That she will not take a song out of a sleeper's sleep, and that the old singer's house was right not to ask. The storytelling households who said last year it was a shame now say it was a mercy; the singers' households say she listened to the end. The old woman of the old singer's house says the guest could have asked and did not, and that it is the same thing. (Year 5.)
- That she asked leave to walk to the harbor and be back, and named the day, and that a keeper who names the day is a keeper who means to be back. The lowest streets say she went to the sea to look for the second guest. The old woman of the lower town who keeps the old tale says a guest leaves as she came, in the dark, and that the name of the day is the first night of the leaving. (Year 5.)
- That the warm wind after the hard frost in Year 4 was hers, and that none came this year because she had not been asked for one. The elder against her says wind follows frost in any country and none followed this year. (Year 4 and 5.)

## About the strange things of the year
- The star dimmed on three nights this year, twenty-four in all since she came, and a dying in the town on eight of the twenty-four. The town says: the star was resting; it was afraid less because she was written down; it dims when she is lying and not when she is walking, which is not so, since it dimmed in the yard when she was awake; it dimmed for the old woman who died in a kept house. The young say the star pays for the flames and the roll has gone to about six hundred and five while the nights fell, which they say proves the star was paid. The old say more nights are not different nights, and fewer are not either. The Keeper of the Count says the cord does not say why. (Year 1 to 5.)
- That the iron went soft because the star dims and the blue gets into it. The smiths say the mining town is sending its iron down before it is worked enough. The lowest streets say a god's lamp is near iron and iron goes soft near a god. The Warden of the Wall says it is the iron and not the guest. The carriers say they carry what they are given. The cutters say the harbor's stone will use up the picks, and the old cutter says he has one pick in three of the old iron. (Year 3 to 5.)
- That the west is empty: the party that went to the hills saw no one, and the town says no one lives there. This is told as certain. Four people walked and did not see. (Year 2, still told.)
- That the harbor wants the scar's stone because it is the giants' stone and holds against the sea. The cutters are proud of it and the old cutter says the harbor wants stone and has not said what for. (Year 3, still told.)
- That the third barrow song is lost and the old people of the hamlets remember it only by its first words, and that the song went into the barrows with the oldest singer. The old woman at the farthest hamlet who wept said her mother sang it at the harvest. The storytelling households say it will come back if it is sung awake, since a sung thing is not lost. The barrow households say what they have, and stop at the gap. (Year 4 and 5.)
- That the one in the delta who reads scales is the second guest, and that the old tale never said one. The storytelling households say there were two promised and the first came by the river and the second by the road. The lowest streets say a god who sits at the scales weighs us. The Bench elder against the guest says two gods in a river country are one more than it has bread for. The Warden of the Wall says nothing and keeps the wall. (Year 3 to 5.)
- That the stack of stone that lay at the river's mouth was lifted by the harbor's ship, as the factor said it would be, and the harbor has the town's stone and has not said what for. No one in the town has seen the mouth since the party came home; it is said with certainty on the quay all the same. (Year 5.)

## About their rulers
- That the seat speaks only what she has weighed, and holds the bargain with the harbor as the town's and not the guest's, and that a ruler who says so is a ruler. She said this year that the town keeps its own nights and that anyone who says "the guest says" of what she has not said answers to the Bench. (Year 3 to 5.)
- That the new Warden of the Door takes the guest for the awaited, and held the guest's place and the cord of questions all summer, and said she was afraid for her, and that a woman who keeps two lamps keeps one mind. The Bench elder against the guest says she may keep two minds yet. (Year 4 and 5.)
- That the old Warden of the Door told the guest her son was home and she carried a wrong word anyway, and that it is the court's roll that was wrong and not the old man. The Steward has had the roll struck and the west road gate keeper told to strike a party home by that road. (Year 4 and 5.)
- That the Warden of the Wall has his answer and is not easy with it, and that he did not choose the four of his watch who went with the guest and did not stop them. He said the wall has begun to know what it writes and does not know what it has written. (Year 3 to 5.)
- That the Steward of the Store sent forty and held twenty back because the picks are worn, and that he counts the winter aloud now without being asked. (Year 4 and 5.)
- That the Gate Bench fined a man a day's labor for saying "the guest says" of the weir, and was right to, and that it holds a flame is lit at a household's asking and in no other way. (Year 3 to 5.)
- That the Keeper of the Count laid the wall's tablets beside his cord and would not say what they meant, and that this was wise. He writes lying beside the lot nights now and has a deputy who drew the lots in the summer. (Year 4 and 5.)
- That the old cutter said no more than a fifth of a year's stone should go to the harbor because the wall is the town's and the sea is not, and this year held twenty back. (Year 3 to 5.)

## About neighbors
- That the court has sent another party to the harbor, with forty blocks and the guest's word, and a gift for any carrier who will take it to the delta, and that it is the guest's doing and the court's own. The town says the party will bring salt and iron, or the terms for them. Nothing is known in the town beyond that at the year's end except as the Keeper's pass two says. (Year 5.)
- That the carriers know more than they say. This is said of carriers in any year. This year it is said they are more careful about the iron than the salt. (Year 3 to 5.)
- That the harbor sells salt cheap because the sea gives it free, and that the carriers keep the price up. (Year 2, still told.)

## About themselves
- That the Night Sitter and the guest sit well together, and that the guest is good with a kettle. The Night Sitter says she sleeps more, and says the hamlet girl sits two nights in ten and her hands shake. The lowest streets say the court is feeding the guest's pupils; the Night Sitter says she has no pupil of the guest's and the Steward can count. (Year 3 to 5.)
- That the Dusk Table sets the guest's place now, and that a town that counts its own dim nights is a town that has begun to count its gods. (Year 2, still told.)
- That a roll nobody struck led a god to a father's door, and the Steward has had the roll read over by two, and now by a third at the west gate. The town says this is how small a thing it takes. (Year 4 and 5.)
- That a town that kept its own nights while the guest was away is a town that can keep them. The seat said it from the wall. The lowest streets say it kept them bright. (Year 5.)
