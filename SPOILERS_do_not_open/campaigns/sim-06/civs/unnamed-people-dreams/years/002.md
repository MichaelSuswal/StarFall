# Year 2: the unnamed people of god-dreams

The year opens in autumn. Months are counted from the opening: month 1 is the first month of autumn, months 4 to 6 are winter, 7 to 9 spring, 10 to 12 summer. Offices are named by office; no mortal has said an office holder's name aloud this year except as the report says. Where a thing turns on what the god's power did, I write only what was seen and said, and the report sends it to the Keeper. The salt party is an attempt the Keeper settles; the parts of the year that turn on it are written both ways, marked A (the party home before the hard cold, with salt) and B (not home until spring).

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**The grain and the western party.** The grain was in and the threshing under way in the barns, an ordinary harvest. In the first week of month 1 the western party came home along the road, all four alive and thin. They had found no one in the hills and no smoke. Their meal ran short, they ate what they snared, and they turned for home when half of it was gone. They brought back the linen and pots they had carried as gifts. The seat had them fed at the court's table and heard them one at a time. The town took the word as plain: the west is empty. It is a conclusion from four people's walk and the party did not claim it. The Warden of the Door told the guest the same evening. Because the party was home, the small party of runners she had meant to ask for in spring, if none came back, was never wanted.

**The answer from the wall.** On the sixth day of month 1, at dusk, the seat spoke from the wall, with the Warden of the Door standing at her side to give the guest's words in her own. She said the guest had been asked a thing last year and left it, and now answered. The guest does not know why the star dims. She does not know that she makes it dim. She will not take the credit or the blame. She asks the Keeper of the Count to keep a tally of the dim nights, with the dates and whether any dying fell on them, so that the town may judge by its cords and not by her fear or theirs. The seat added that the Keeper of the Count would keep it and that the Count's door would be open on the first day of each month to any who wished to read the cord. The town was quiet. Many had come to the wall expecting either a claim or a denial and got neither.

What the town made of it, the same dusk and the days after: the Night Sitter and most of the headmen said it was an honest thing and that a guest who says "I do not know" is a guest who will not cheat the table. Some of the kept households were let down and said so low: a keeper of the dark should know her own dark. In the lowest streets it was said that she did not say no. Hearing "I do not know that I make it dim", some heard a confession and some a refusal to confess. The old who remember such nights said again that the star dimmed on nights when nothing happened, and were heard politely.

The Keeper of the Count took the task that night. He began the cord at once: one cord for the dim nights, a knot for each, and beside it a second thread for each night that a dying fell, with a tag for whether the house kept the guest's flame. For the three nights of Year 1 he went to the wall watch and the court's runners and had the dates from memory: two in month 1, about the eighth night and the twentieth, and one in month 5. He marked those as remembered and not counted.

**The scar.** On the seventh day of month 1 the guest walked up to the scar, about a mile north-west, with the Warden of the Door and two runners. The cutters were at the footings for the wall's winter work, about thirty at the face. She told them to their faces, as she had said she would, that she did not know who made the lowest cuts and did not know that she was a giant's kin. The old cutter who had asked the year before, a man of about sixty, said: "Then we are no wiser than we were." She asked him to show her the lowest cuts where she could reach them on four legs. The cutters cleared the loose spoil from the foot of the face and showed her three. She went down to them as far as she could and looked a long time. She did not put a hand to any cut and did not say that any was hers. She said nothing more of them and was not asked. The cutters differ on what they saw. Some say she looked like someone reading a name. The old cutter says she looked like a woman looking at a stranger's hand. He is not sure of that and says he is not.

**The yard of the watch.** On the tenth day of month 1 she went to the yard of the watch behind the south-east gate, with the Warden of the Door and two runners. The Warden of the Wall received her standing and sent his two captains out of the yard. She asked what he distrusted in her and what he would have her do or not do, and listened to the end. She did not argue and showed him nothing.

He told her plainly. He distrusts three things. First, that the flames near her dip when she is afraid, and she is afraid on the mornings after Keeping, and the wall does not know what it guards against. Second, that the town now has two lamps, the old flame and hers, and a watch of two hundred is split between them and a split watch is not a watch. Third, that she asks and does not order, and he has watched a whole town do her asking with no order given, and a thing done without an order is a thing no one can be held to. He would have her do this: bring no flame within the watch's yards, light none for any man on the wall while he is on duty, ask the wall nothing but through him, and tell him the days she means to walk beyond the gate. He said he keeps the beacon watch as he keeps it and would if she had never come, and that he will tell her of strangers on the roads, after the seat. Asked to name one thing she could do for the wall that he would count as honest, he said he would answer after a night.

The next morning his word came by his captain to the Warden of the Door: let the wall hold her door. He asked that the wall watch, not the court's runners, sit the door of her lean-to on the nights of Keeping, and that the men who sit it be told by her what they sit against. He asked leave of the seat for the first and the seat gave it. He did not wait on the second. What she answered is hers. The wall's men took her door from the first night of winter, two men a night from the watch, by turns, and the court's runners went back to the lanes. He did this as the wall's own work. He has not taken her for the awaited one.

**The salt.** In the second month, when the grain was safe in the barns, the guest asked through the Warden of the Door that the court send a party of its own to the harbor town west along the coast where the salt is boiled, to buy at the harbor's price and not at the carriers'. The seat heard the Steward of the Store first, then the others one at a time. The Steward said what he had said before the council last winter: that if salt did not come the meat would go. The Warden of the Wall asked that two of his men go as rowers, belt knives only. The seat refused it, on the Steward's word that a party of the wall is another sort of party in a stranger's eyes, and the guest had asked that no spear go. The Warden of the Wall said then six and not four: the coast is not the river. The seat sent six.

The party: the leader, chosen by the Steward, a man of about forty winters, the Steward's chief cord-keeper of the trade goods, who counts the carriers' loads at the gate each season and knows to the pot what they ask. Four runners named by the Warden of the Door, three of them of last year's river party, who have met the crew of the coasting ship, and one new, a young woman. One boatman of the fishing households who knows the low-water bars, chosen by the Steward. They go in one of the court's planked river boats. Goods: twenty bolts of linen, a hundred pots packed in straw and reed baskets, fifteen jars of flax oil. Those are the gifts and the trade, as the Steward judged what the town can spare. No bronze. No lamp, no wick and no ember from any niche in the town goes with them. The Warden of the Door had the baggage looked through at the quay, and the party's first fire was struck from flint on the river bank. One runner of a kept household asked to carry a lit wick for his house's sake and was refused, and took it well.

The words they carry are the guest's, exactly as given, which the leader had by heart after three evenings with the Warden of the Door, who added nothing. The orders: ask at the harbor for bread and three nights; ask no business until the third; on the third night give the words to whoever sets the price of salt; if no one will hear them, buy salt as any trader would and come home; if the harbor is hostile, do not fight, do not argue, do not give up the goods already promised, and come home with what can be had or winter where it is safe; if asked questions, answer only what was seen and told: the guest woke on the stony ground above the town in the dark before an autumn morning, she does not know why, the seat takes her for the one awaited, and she keeps a lamp that she lights in a house that asks. The leader is not to say what the lamp does or that it can be taken, and if pressed on any of it he says he does not know.

The way. The leader means to go by the court's boat down the river to the mouth. At the mouth he means to ask the crew of the coasting ship that fed the river party last year, if a ship is there, for passage to the harbor for linen, and to go on by the ship. If no ship is there he means to leave the boat, the pots and two hands at the mouth, and go round the shore on foot with the linen, the oil and four carrying. Coming home he means the same in reverse, and the boat up the river with the salt. He has said he will not put the river boat to sea. They set out in the third week of month 2.

**The star, the first night of the year.** In month 2, on the ninth night, eleven nights before the party went, the star dimmed. She was awake in the yard under it with her lamp at her feet and said nothing, by the runners at her door. No death fell in the town that night. The Keeper of the Count stood on the wall with two of his cord-keepers and had the hour from the watch's turn. He knotted the cord at dawn. The town said it was a sign for the party; the other half said it was no such thing and the old said what the old say.

**The Warden of the Door names his successor.** In month 3, a few days before the first frost, the Warden of the Door told the seat and the Keeper of the Count, as he said he would, whom he had chosen: a woman of about forty winters of a storytelling household of the lower town, who has kept the empty place's lamp beside him for four years and was at the table he carried out on the first morning. She keeps her ancestors' niche from the old flame and, since the winter, the guest's flame in her house also, and says she will keep both. The Gate Bench had no quarrel but one: the elder who is against the guest said a woman who keeps two lamps may keep two minds. The seat said the old man had kept the lamp for the awaited through all his years and had earned his choice. From the first frost the new Warden of the Door carries the guest's asks and walks beside her. The old man stays in his house, at his lamp, and counsels. The seat named the office hers at the winter's first dusk. She has said to the seat that she carries what she is given faithfully and will hold her own counsel on what the guest is until she has heard her herself.

**Late autumn.** The salt carriers came in the last weeks, with less than was wanted, at last year's price. They ate the three nights and watched. They had no word of the party. Iron came down from the mining town as in other years. The late pigs were killed after the first frost. Keeping Sleep ran by the lot, two households a night, the deathbeds, newborns, fevers and fresh griefs first. The seat and the Keeper of the Count held the cap at two: the court's runners carried word to the hamlets that no household was to set a night's lamp in the guest's name beyond the lot.

## The salt party, both ways

**A. Home before the hard cold.** The party came up the river in the last days of month 3, ahead of the cold, with salt in the court's boat. The Keeper prices the load and the harbor's answer. The late pigs, just killed, are cured as they ought to be. The town has the salt as its own road for the first time in anyone's telling. The carriers, hearing it in the spring, know the town has seen the harbor's price.

**B. Not back until spring.** No word came in autumn or winter. The late pigs were cured lightly again. Salt ran out in the second month of winter and the meat went early, as last year or a little worse. The Steward counted it aloud before the council and said a party may be wintering at the harbor, or may not. The party came up in the spring, in the high water, in month 7 or 8 as the Keeper prices the ice and the flood, with the harbor's answer and what salt it could bring.

The rest of the year is written for either, with the difference marked.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The winter was cold in the ordinary way. The first night of winter the wall's two men sat her door for the first time. They are chosen by lot among the watch, and not from households that have asked for the flame. At dawn they saw what the wall watch has seen before: she was shaken, the flames dipped. They said so to the Warden of the Wall and to no one else.

**The Steward counts aloud.** In month 4, at the guest's asking through the Warden of the Door, the Steward counted before the council again, grain, fuel, salt. Grain would reach the new harvest with a thin margin, as the year before. Fuel was enough. Salt: A, in hand, and the meat safe; B, short, and the Steward said so as he had before.

**Keeping Sleep, the cap and the three nights.** Through the winter two households a night were kept by the Keeper of the Count's lot. On the hardest nights of deep winter the Warden of the Door named the night to the Keeper of the Count the day before, and the seat's crier told the town, so that the court asked no plan of her the next day and no one was surprised: month 5, night 14, a hard frost with fevers; month 6, night 3; month 6, night 25. On those nights the Keeper drew three households. The wall set four men at her door and not two. What the wall's men saw at those dawns is a thing of her power and the report sends it to the Keeper. The court asked nothing of her the next day and did not send for her; the mornings after were quiet on the streets near the yard.

**A flame at night.** In month 4 a young man of a kept house of the lower town, with his father dying, lit a neighbor's niche from his own kept flame in the night, at the neighbor's asking, on a night when two households were already drawn. The Keeper of the Count counted it and wrote it down: not on the lot, and so not within the cap he had been asked to hold. He took it to the new Warden of the Door the next day and asked whether it was a third household kept that night. No answer I can write. The Keeper entered the neighbor's house on the roll the next day. Nothing was seen that night that was not seen on other nights.

**The old man's cough.** In month 5 the old Warden of the Door kept his bed three weeks with a chest fever, in a kept house. He lived. His successor carried the asks that month, and the old man told her from his bed what he would have said.

**The Night Sitter.** In month 5 the new Warden of the Door took the guest to the Night Sitter and her pupil, at the guest's asking, and she sat through a night with them in the Night Sitter's own work yard with an old woman, very sick, and a boy in fever. She did not work. The Night Sitter sang low, as she does, and the old woman died near dawn, as they do, with the household around her; the boy lived. The guest asked the Night Sitter what her work takes from her and what she would have of the guest. The Night Sitter said it takes three nights' sleep and her hands, and nothing the guest could give back. The guest said she would not take her work from her. The Night Sitter said she could not. The guest said that if the Night Sitter asked it, she could keep the Night Sitter's own household's sleep on the nights after she has sat. The Night Sitter said she would not ask for what she could not see the bottom of.

In month 6 a fever went through the lower town and the Night Sitter sat on the ninth, the tenth and the twelfth nights. On the thirteenth her hands shook so that she spilled the broth, and she had not slept since the ninth. Her pupil, who is afraid of the work, went to the new Warden of the Door that morning and asked: "Will she keep our mistress's house?" The Night Sitter, told, said the girl was a fool and she would not say no. The household was kept on the thirteenth night, one of the night's two. At dawn the guest was shaken, the flames near her dipped, as on other mornings.

Whether the Night Sitter slept is a thing of the power. A: she slept, the night through and the next, and her hands were still by the third night; she said "I slept" and no more, and the pupil wept in the yard. B: she did not sleep, as ever; she said so, flatly, and thanked the household for the trouble. Either way, the Night Sitter keeps her work and the pupil keeps hers.

**The star in deep winter.** In month 6, on the twentieth night, the star dimmed. She was asleep in the yard, a night of the lot, with the wall's two men at her door. That night an old man of the lower town died in a kept house, quietly. The tag went on the cord. The next morning two men of the lower street, one of a kept house and one not, came to blows in the lane over whether the star dims for the kept. The Gate Bench heard them and fined each a day's labor on the wall, and said again that no house is shamed for not asking and no house is to be shamed for asking.

**The barrow songs.** At the long winter tables of the lower town the oldest of the barrow singers said again that the young cannot keep the third song. This time two young households, one of a kept house and one not, sat with him through the winter, and by the thaw they had half of it. The songs stand at three of five. No one has asked the guest about them.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

The river was high in spring, as in most years, and took no seed this year. The flood watch was kept by the headmen and the wall. The Steward lent seed to the lowest hamlet as before and the old debt stands, not pressed.

**The hamlets.** In the second month of spring and into the third the guest went out with a half score of the wall watch, whom the Warden of the Wall gave when asked and chose himself, to the hamlets she had not reached the year before. There are about fifteen hamlets. She reached six last year and six this spring, within a morning's walk of the wall. Three lie farther and she did not go. She walked the bottom land and the flood line with the headmen, lit a flame only where it was asked and in no hamlet pressed it on a house that did not ask. She did not go into any pen, net, narrow lane or barred door. At one hamlet the bridge was too narrow and the headman came out to her. The headmen told her what they feared: the river, the seed, salt, the cold, and now the dim nights. Of the six hamlets, one in three or four has a Night Sitter, as before.

In month 9, on the third night, the star dimmed while she was in one of the western hamlets, a morning's walk from the wall. She was awake, with the headman at the edge of the field, and the whole hamlet saw it. The town saw it too, from the wall. At dawn the headman asked her whether it was she. She answered what she had said from the wall: that she did not know, did not know that she made it dim, and would take neither credit nor blame. No death fell in the town that night. The hamlet's own dying that night are not known: the headman said none.

**The carriers.** The salt carriers came in month 8 with iron, as in other years, at last year's price. In A they found the town less in need and did not say so. In B the Steward asked them hard for any word of the party and they gave none.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

The river was low and the dry months dry enough for the stone cutting. The river-side footing of the wall slumped after the spring water. The cutters relaid it, with the Steward's stone, and finished before the first rain. The cutters left the lowest cuts at the scar as they were, with a ring of loose stones set round the three she was shown. No one told them to.

**The star, twice in the last two months.** In month 11, on the fifteenth night, it dimmed, a night of the lot. A young woman in childbed in a house not kept died that night, and the child lived. The tag went on the cord. In month 12, on the twenty-seventh night, it dimmed again. She was awake in the yard under it, the wall's two men at her door said, and looked up and said nothing. No death fell in the town that night.

**The Keeper's reading.** At the turn of the year the Keeper of the Count laid the cord on the Count's table and said what it shows and no more. This year five nights, last year three, eight in all. A dying fell in the town on three of the eight: two in kept houses and one in a house not kept. By the roll of the town's dead, he said, a dying falls in the town on about one night in four, so three of eight is what the roll alone would give. She was awake on three of the five this year, asleep on two, and in a hamlet on one. He said the cord does not say why. The town heard him out, and kept its explanations.

**The wall at year's end.** The Warden of the Wall has not taken the guest for the awaited and has not refused her. His men have sat her door for a year's winter and a season; he says they are the wall's own work. One of them asked off in the spring, after a dawn, and was let off without a word. He told the seat in the last month that the flames near her dip at the same hour every dawn and that the wall has stopped being surprised, which is not the same as no longer minding.

**The end of the year.** Grain was cut and brought in, an ordinary harvest again. The roll of households keeping the flame stands at about 415. The old Warden of the Door is alive and at his lamp. The seat is well. The seventh place of the guest's keeping is still empty.
