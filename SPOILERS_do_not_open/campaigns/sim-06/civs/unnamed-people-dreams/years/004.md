# Year 4: the unnamed people of god-dreams

The year opens in autumn. Months are counted from the opening: month 1 is the first month of autumn, months 4 to 6 are winter, 7 to 9 spring, 10 to 12 summer. Offices are named by office; no mortal has said an office holder's name aloud this year. Where a thing turns on what the god's power did, I write only what was seen and said, and the report sends it to the Keeper. One thing this year is an attempt the Keeper settles: the court's party to the harbor with the stone and the guest's word. It is written both ways, briefly. Salt A is the party home before the hard cold, with salt. Salt B is the party not home until spring. Where nothing is marked, the year is the same either way.

What the town knew at the opening, as the Keeper's line on Year 3 gave it. The harbor party did not come home before the cold: a boat grounded on a bar under its stone, no ship lay at the mouth, they went round the shore with the samples and were given bread and three nights, and the leader spoke the words on the third night. The harbor house took the samples, sold salt at a standing price below the carriers', and named its order: sixty blocks a year dressed to a cord it gave, to be lifted by its own ship at the river's mouth. The gales shut the shore, and the eight wintered at the harbor and came up at high water, home in month 8, all eight. On the quays the leader heard, and brought home, that at a market town in the western delta a stranger who reads scales for a pinch of barley has been seated as keeper of the weights and says before the market that it is a god. Of the three who sat the winter beside the Night Sitter, the hamlet girl found she could. At the dawns after three households the wall's men saw what the guest had told them they would see, and no more. The dimmings of the star are not known to be anyone's doing. This is what the town says it knows. Where my Year 3 file read otherwise, the Keeper's line is what happened.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

The grain was in and the threshing under way, stubble on the bottom land, mist in the mornings, the river low. It was an ordinary harvest, as in most years.

**The stranger in the delta, the first weeks.** Since the leader's return in the spring of last year the town has told the stranger's tale at the Dusk Table until it is smooth. The leader said it as he heard it on the quays and no more: a man at the market town in the western delta who reads scales for a pinch of barley, seated as keeper of the weights, who says before the market that he is a god. He did not know whether it was true and said so. The seat had it from him in the guest house yard, in front of the guest. The town has made of it what it makes of things. The storytelling households say the old tale of the guest was never one guest but a pair, and that the first came by the river and the second will come by the road. The lowest streets say a keeper who sits at the scales is a keeper who weighs, and that the one at the bend has been weighing them for three years. The elder of the Gate Bench who is against the guest said at the autumn Bench that two gods in a river country are one more than a river country has bread for. The Warden of the Wall said nothing in the Bench and said to his captain that he would keep the wall as it was.

**The yard of the watch, month 1, day 4.** In the morning the new Warden of the Door took the guest to the yard of the watch behind the south-east gate, with two runners. The captain and the four men of the watch who sit her door were there by the Warden of the Wall's order, and the Warden of the Wall himself, standing by. She answered the two questions put to her in this yard a year ago, as her decisions fix.

To the captain, who asked how a man is to know the standing body from the sleeping one: that when she walks a dream her body stands where it was and does not lie down. It is dim and slow, it will not walk more than a step and it will not speak above a murmur. She does not sleep, so a hind lying down is not her in a dream. A standing body that answers only in a murmur is her in a sleeper's dream, and it is not to be moved, startled or held without need. If a man is in doubt he asks her and waits, and if she does not answer above a murmur he lets her stand. To the one of the four who asked whether they are to move her if fire comes into the yard: yes. They are to take her by the cord at her waist and lead her out, and leave the lamp on the cord and on her. Harm that lands on her body while she is in a dream she feels and does not resist, and she would rather be moved than burned. If her body is broken she is thrown out of the dream, and the break stands, and she returns after a season at the nearest house with a lamp burning through the night. She told them this, she said, so they should not be afraid for her past what is true.

What the wall made of it. The Warden of the Wall heard her to the end and asked nothing of the first part. Of the last he asked which house was nearest, and I have no word of hers I can write. The captain said what the wall has seen: that on the lot nights of two winters the wall's men have seen her lie still in the yard with the lamp lit, and the wall wrote it asleep, and the Keeper of the Count tagged it asleep on his cord. If a lying hind is not her in a dream, he asked, what have the wall's men been sitting? Not answered that I can write. The Warden of the Wall said that the answer was the second half of the one he asked for at the start of the year before last, and that the wall would keep it. That same day the captain had the way from the lean-to to the south-east gate staked out and kept clear of carts and sledges, since a hind cannot pass a low door and the gate takes her width, and had the four walk it by day with a cord. The Warden of the Wall carried the part about the break to the seat himself, as a thing the court should know the wall knew.

What the town made of the break, over the next days. The wall's men said that since every house in the town keeps a lamp burning through the night, the nearest house is any house, and so she cannot be killed. The lowest streets nearest the guest house yard said she would come back to their street. Some of the storytelling households said she would come back as a child of the house, and the streets began to say a lamp should be kept bright. The elder of the Gate Bench who is against her said that a god who can be broken and mended is a guest that needs the town, and the two for her said it was said to guard her and not to hold her. The Warden of the Door said only that the guest had said it so that the men would not be afraid, and that she did not know that any of them was.

**The second house, month 1, day 6.** The Warden of the Door took the guest with two runners to the second house of the four who went west, to answer the father who had asked at his door a year ago who told her they had not come home. The Warden of the Door had asked the question about in the days between: of the old Warden of the Door, who said he had told the guest the evening the four came home, two autumns gone, and had not told her otherwise; of the Steward's quay keeper; and of the runners. She had one answer, and she gave it to the guest on the road, and the guest said she would give it to the father as she had given it. It is this. The Steward's quay keeper keeps a roll of the parties that have gone out by the quay, and strikes a name when the party's leader comes to the quay. The four came home by the west road and not by the quay, and were taken at once to the court's table, and no one struck them. A runner, when the guest asked in the spring of last year which parties were out, read her the roll as it stood, and the roll had the four as out. The Warden of the Door said she thought it was the roll, that she could not swear the runner read it so, and that she had not heard the roll read.

At the door the guest said what her decisions fix: that she was told it through the court, by way of the Warden of the Door, and carried it to his door as true; that she did not ask whose word it was first; that it was wrong, and his son came home in the first month of last year, thin and alive; that she should have asked and was sorry he was told so at his door; that she did not know whose word it first was and would not name one; and she gave the Warden of the Door's answer as the Warden of the Door's, a thing she thought and could not swear. She said that from now she asks who gave a thing to her before she carries it to a house. The father heard her to the end. He said it was a plain thing plainly said, and that his son had come home two autumns past and not last year, as the cords at the quay would show if they were kept. His wife brought bread out to the lane and the household set a bench, and the guest took bread at the door, which is the custom's answer. The father said that if the roll was wrong it should be struck. The Steward had the roll struck that week and had his quay keeper and the Warden of the Door's runner read the whole roll over together; the four were the only names that had been left standing. The streets liked it. The elder against her said that a god who asks whose word a thing was is a god who can be told anything once.

**The same dusk.** The guest gave the Keeper of the Count, through the Warden of the Door, her plain list of the days of her third year on which she was afraid, as she felt it, and said she did not know whether it and his cord of the six dim nights agree, and would say so whichever way they fell. The Keeper of the Count said, dry as before, that he would set the cords side by side and say what the two show and no more. He wrote the list onto a cord of his own, a knot to a day.

**The open door, month 2, day 1.** The Keeper of the Count laid the three cords on the Count's table: the cord of the dim nights, last year's cord of her afraid days, and this new one. He said what he said last year, that a count shows how many and not why, and that anyone who wished could set a finger on a dim night and on the day beside it. The town did. The streets read what they read. Those who hold that her fear dims the star said the cords agree; the others said they do not, and each had a night to show. I do not have her list and do not write what it says.

**The harbor's order, month 2.** In the first days of the month, with the grain safe, the guest asked through the Warden of the Door and by the throne's leave that the court judge the harbor house's order for the year: sixty blocks dressed to the cord it gave, to be lifted by its own ship at the river's mouth. She asked that the Steward of the Store and the old cutter judge what stone could be spared and what the wall and the footings need first, and that the throne decide whether to send it and who carries it. She said the people who carry it are the court's and carry its word, that she sends no flame, no lamp and no token of hers with them, that she does not go and sends no spears. She asked the Steward that the leader, if the court sends one, be one who listens as well as trades.

The Steward of the Store and the old cutter walked the stacks at the scar and the footings, three days this time. The old cutter, about sixty-two, said what he said before: the wall's winter footing takes four parts in five of the dry months' cutting and the fifth is what the scar can spare, and no more should be promised. The fifth of last summer's cutting stood stacked at the scar, cut and dressed to the house's cord after the leader came home with it; the journeyman cutter, about thirty-five, laid every block against the cord. Fifty-six were true. Four were a thumb short, and the old cutter replaced them from the stack beyond the footing's need. That made sixty blocks to the cord, about two boats' load and a half more. He said the picks were wearing twice as fast on the soft iron and the harbor's stone cost the town more in iron than the harbor knew. The elder against the guest said the stone is the town's footing and the guest was asking it for strangers again. The seat answered that the court was judging, the town was the one that would sell, and the bargain was the town's. She heard the offices one at a time.

The Warden of the Wall asked a third time for two of his men as rowers, belt knives only, and said that stone is a thing a man will rob a boat for, and that a party that goes on from the harbor into country no one has seen should not go with no one who can hold a spear. The seat refused him a third time, for the guest had asked that no spear go, and said she had heard the reason and kept the refusal. He said he would not ask a fourth. The Keeper of the Count asked what was to go west beside the stone, and when the seat said the guest's word to the one in the delta, carried as the guest gave it, he said a word sent west cannot be called back and that the Count would knot what went. The elder who is against the guest said that she asks the stranger to come to our gate, and that the court would be giving a bed to the second and a table to the third. The two for her said it is the custom and a stranger has three nights. On the ninth day the seat accepted the order and said the party should go and the word with it.

The Steward chose the leader: the same man, about forty-two, chief keeper of the trade cords, who led last year's eight and spoke the words on the third night and came home with a tale from the quays. The Steward said a man who hears a thing on the quays and brings it home whole is a man who listens, and the guest had asked for one. The leader did not ask to be replaced this time. He asked for a better boat for the bars, and the Steward gave him a third boat, a planked boat of the fishing households, lent for the run against pots, so that no boat would sit as low as last year's. The party is nine in three planked boats: the leader; two cutters, the journeyman and a younger man to haul; three runners named by the Warden of the Door, the young woman who has now been twice to the harbor and two of last year's eight; and three boatmen of the fishing households, the man who knows the low-water bars among them. Goods: twenty-five bolts of linen, sixty pots in straw and reed baskets, twelve jars of flax oil, of which the Steward set apart, as a guest's gift for the one in the delta, three bolts of linen, twelve pots and two jars of oil; and the sixty blocks, divided between the three boats. No bronze. No lamp, no wick and no ember from any niche; the Warden of the Door had the baggage looked through at the quay, and the first fire is struck from flint on the river bank. Each carries a belt knife and the cutters their tools and nothing else of iron.

The words they carry are the guest's, and the leader had them by heart after three evenings in the middle of the month in the Warden of the Door's house, where the Warden of the Door gave them and added nothing. The name in them was said aloud in that house, by the leader and by the Warden of the Door, each evening. On the first evening the leader, who counts, said the words put the town "four days down the river from its mouth", which is the way a boat goes from the town and not where the town lies; the town is four days up the river from the mouth, as his cords have it. The Warden of the Door asked the guest the next morning whether she meant it so. I have no word of hers I can write. The Warden of the Door told the leader to say the words as they were given, and the leader said that if the factor asks where the bend lies he will say four days up from the mouth by the town's own count, which is a thing seen and not a thing added.

The orders are the guest's, as given to the court. The party is the court's guest at the harbor: bread, three nights, no questions of its business until the third. The leader gives the guest's words to the factor of the house that took the stone, with the court's own words, and asks that the house's ship or carriers take them on to the delta. If the factor will not, or the house has no road there, the leader may go on by the coast if the court allowed it before he left. The seat allowed it, with her own limits: the leader may go on with two runners and the guest's word, by the house's ship or carriers or on foot, if the factor offers a road he judges safe and if he can be home or settled for the winter before the gales, and he may turn back at any time and say so; the cutters and the boatmen do not go on; he binds the court to nothing; if the stranger is hostile or cannot be found he does not fight and does not argue. If the stranger is found and asks questions, he answers only what was seen and told: the guest woke on the stony ground above the town in the dark before an autumn morning, does not know why, the seat takes her for the awaited, and she keeps a lamp that she lights in a house that asks. He does not say what the lamp does or that it can be taken, does not give or sell a flame, lamp or token of hers to anyone, does not say how many days she walks or which nights she keeps, and if pressed on any of it he says he does not know. If the stranger says it is a god, he says what he heard and says no more, does not say it is false and does not say it is true. He listens, and brings home any answer, and any word of another like her on that coast. The Steward's own orders: the stone is the court's and is to be put into the house's ship's hands at the mouth against the factor's word, buy salt at the standing price with the linen and oil, and bring home the terms.

The way. By the court's boats down the river to the mouth (112.4 miles, 4 days down, 10 days back up). The boatman who knows the low-water bars goes first with a pole, and the leader said they would take the first autumn rain's water and not before. At the mouth he means to wait for the house's ship for ten days. If it comes, the stone goes to it and the party goes on in it or on foot to the harbor with the linen, the oil and the factor's salt to buy. If no ship comes in ten days, he means to stack the stone above the high water on the shore under hides and reed with the three boatmen and the cutters, and go on round the shore on foot with the linen, the oil and two runners (97.9 miles, 7 days on foot, longer laden) to ask the factor why the ship has not come. He will not put the river boats to sea. They set out from the quay on the twenty-fourth day of month 2, the water raised a hand by the rain.

**The star, month 2, night 14.** The star dimmed, the blue deepening for about an hour, and stood dim in the middle of the night, and was as before. No lot had been drawn. She was awake in the yard under it, the wall's two men at her door said, and said nothing. No death fell in the town that night. The Keeper of the Count knotted the cord at dawn with the hour from the watch's turn. The town said it was a sign for the party, which was not yet gone; the other half said it was no such thing.

**The barrow songs, month 3.** On the eleventh day of month 3, at dawn, the oldest of the barrow singers died, a very old woman of the lower town, of her age. She was the last who had the third barrow song whole. The two young households who sat with her through two winters sat all that night, as the table demands that the dying are not left alone. In the last nights she tried to give them the last part of the song, and her voice would not hold it, and they have what they had, two parts in three of the third song, and the last third is not in any living mouth. Her own house had not kept the guest's flame, as she had said the barrows keep their own. No one asked the guest to walk her last sleep, and nothing of the guest was at that bed. She was laid in the burying ground at the foot of the barrows, and the singers' households sang the first and the second songs over her whole and the third in its two parts, and stopped at the gap, as a cord is left with its end unknotted. There were five songs in the grandparents' time and there are three, and the third is now two parts of three. What the storytelling households say now is in the lore.

**The star, month 3, night 23.** It dimmed again, an hour, in the middle of the night. No lot had been drawn. She was awake in the yard, the two men said, and looked up and said nothing. No death fell in the town that night. The households of the barrow singers said that it was for the song. The wall's watch noted the hour as it has since last year: about an hour.

**Late autumn.** The salt carriers came in the last weeks with a small load at last year's price. The Steward of the Store bought a part and no more. Iron came down from the mining town grey and soft as since last winter, and the smiths drew it out. The late pigs were killed after the first frost. In Salt B the Steward asked the carriers hard for any word of the party and they gave none; in Salt A the carriers saw the town less in need and did not say so.

## The salt party, both ways

**Salt A. Home before the hard cold.** The party came up the river in the last days of month 3 or the first of month 4, before the hard cold, with salt in the boats, the harbor's answer to the stone, and what the leader heard on the quays and from the factor of the way to the delta and of the stranger. The court heard it in the guest house yard, the seat, the Steward, the old cutter, the Warden of the Door and the Keeper of the Count, and the guest. What the harbor answered, whether the word went on, and what was heard, are the Keeper's. The late pigs were cured as they ought to be. The Steward's winter count was easier, and the carriers' spring visit met a town that had seen the harbor's price a second time.

**Salt B. Not back until spring.** No word came in autumn or winter. The late pigs were cured lightly and the meat went early. The Steward counted it aloud and said the party might be wintering at the harbor, or elsewhere, or not. The party came up the river at the high water in month 7 or 8, with what salt it could bring and the harbor's answer, and the court heard it on the quay and in the guest house yard. The cutters cut the fifth in the dry months to the harbor's cord without a later word.

The rest of the year is written for either, with the difference marked where there is one.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The winter was cold in the ordinary way, with one hard frost late in month 6 and a sudden warm wind after it. From the first night of winter the wall's men sat her door, two a night by lot, not from kept houses, four on the named nights. Every man had been told by the captain before his first night, in her words, what he sits against and what the standing body is. The tag on the Keeper of the Count's cord for a lot night has been changed since the answer in the yard: the wall's word was asleep, and the Keeper of the Count, who counts what he is shown, now writes lying beside it and says the cord's old word was the wall's and not hers.

**The Steward counts aloud.** In month 4, by his own habit now and not at her asking, the Steward of the Store counted before the council the grain, the fuel and the salt. Grain would reach the new harvest with a thin margin, as in the years before. Fuel was enough for an ordinary winter. Oil was enough. Salt: Salt A, in hand, the meat safe, and the Steward said so shortly; Salt B, none by the second month of winter, and he said that too.

**Keeping Sleep and the three nights.** Two households a night were kept by the Keeper of the Count's lot, deathbeds, newborns, fevers and fresh griefs first. On three named nights of deep winter three households were kept, each named by the Warden of the Door to the Keeper of the Count and to the wall men at the guest's door the same dusk the day before, and told to the town by the seat's crier: month 5, night 16; month 6, night 2; month 6, night 24, the last on the hardest night of the frost. The court asked no plan of her the next day and sent for her about nothing. The wall set four men at her door. What the four saw at those dawns is a thing of her power, and the report sends it to the Keeper. The mornings after were quiet on the streets near the yard. The seventh place of the guest's keeping stayed empty.

**The dying in kept houses.** About eighteen of the winter's deaths fell in kept houses, more than last year as the roll is larger. In the kept houses that asked, she walked the last sleep of the dying and gave no orders there. The households say again that the dying were quiet and some spoke in the last hour of a hind or a lamp. The wall's men kept watch on the nights of those walks as told. What they saw of her body is the Keeper's, and I write only that the captain said to the Warden of the Wall that they had seen what she told them they would see. Nothing was cured.

**The star, month 4, night 7.** It dimmed, a lot night, two households kept. The wall's two men said she was lying still in the yard, with the lamp lit, and not standing. An old man of the lower town died that night in a kept house, quietly. No one had asked her to walk his sleep. The tag went on the cord.

**The Night Sitter, the hamlet girl, month 4, day 16.** The new Warden of the Door took the guest to the Night Sitter's work yard on a night the Night Sitter sat with a child in fever, and asked on the guest's behalf whether the Night Sitter would have her. The Night Sitter said she would take the hands for the kettle and the lamp as before, and would not have more; she said she had her pupil and the hamlet girl, and that the work sat easier with three. The guest sat beside the Night Sitter, the pupil and the hamlet girl, and kept the kettle and the lamp and did not work. The Night Sitter said afterwards that the guest was still good with a kettle. The child lived. The hamlet girl, about eighteen, steadied a second child's breathing on her own for a night that winter with the Night Sitter asleep in the next room, and she has done so since. The Night Sitter sleeps more, and her hands were steadier by the thaw.

At the guest's asking, through the Warden of the Door, the seat heard the girl's household on what she needs. The girl's mother and father came in from the hamlet on the twenty-second day, and the girl herself stood before the seat in the court yard, which no hamlet girl had done. She said that after a sitting she cannot sleep for three nights and her hands shake, as the Night Sitter's do; that the road home is a morning's walk; that she asked to sit no more than two nights in ten, and to have a bed in the lower town on the nights she sits. Her father asked that the court hear that she was her household's field hand. The seat gave her the bed in the Night Sitter's house, the court's bread on the nights she sits and the three after, and the rule that no sitter of the three sits two nights running. The Steward counted the grain aloud and gave it; the lowest streets said the court was feeding the guest's pupils.

**Two lads and the yard, again.** In month 5 two lads, having heard what the wall had been told, came to the yard wall by night to see if she stood. The wall's two men took them. The Gate Bench fined each lad's father a day's labor, and said again that she was a guest and her body a guest's. This time the captain said they had told the lads which house was her door, and that if there was a fire the way to the gate was staked and she was to be led by the cord.

**The barrow households ask.** In the first week of winter a man of one of the two young households of barrow singers asked the Warden of the Door whether the guest would hear what they have of the third song, two parts in three. They had said last year that they would think. The Warden of the Door carried it. I have no answer of hers I can write. The two households sang the two parts at the foot of the barrows on the longest night, and stopped at the gap.

**The hard frost and the warm wind.** In month 6, in the last week, the hard frost came, the hardest of the winter, with fevers. About eleven of the old and small died in the town and the lower hamlets that week, in kept houses and not. The Night Sitter, her pupil and the hamlet girl sat by turns, three nights each. On the twenty-fourth night of the month the three households were kept. Two days after, a warm wind came from the west and the frost went out of the ground in two days; the lowest streets ran with water to the ankle, two roofs of old thatch came in, the ice went off the river early and carried a fish weir below the town away. The town said the warm wind was hers: that the guest keeps the breath of the dying and let go a breath for the thaw. The elder against her said that wind comes after frost in any country. She said, when asked by a woman of the lowest street, that she did not know.

**The Gate Bench.** The elder who is against the guest said at the winter's first dusk that a woman who keeps two lamps may keep two minds, as he has said, and added that a god who tells the wall how to move her and not the town where she goes when she is broken has chosen her hearers. The two for her said the wall asked. The Bench kept its ruling: no house shamed for asking or for not asking.

**The iron.** The iron the mining town sent down stayed greyer and softer than it was. A share or a pick drawn from it blunts or bends in a month where it used to take a season. The carriers said they carry what they are given. The Steward put by the older iron for the spearheads and the cutters' picks and said the stock of it was a third lower than last winter's.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

The river was high in spring, as in most years. It took no seed this year. The flood watch was kept by the headmen and the wall. The Steward lent seed to the lowest hamlet as before and did not press the old debt.

**What the court would do if she were away, month 7, day 12.** The guest asked through the Warden of the Door that the Warden of the Door, the Warden of the Wall, the Steward of the Store and the Keeper of the Count answer, together if the throne would have it, what the court would want done if she were away from the town for a season or more. She asked who names the seven households on a night she is away and tends the chain of flames; who answers a question put to her while she is gone; how the Warden of the Wall wants the days of her absence told to him; and what the town would do if the one in the delta or any other asked for her by name. The seat heard the four together, the first time the four have stood in one room before her, saying that an answer given by four mouths at once is one answer. It was heard in the guest house yard, the seat sitting, the four standing, the guest standing. The Warden of the Door spoke the questions.

The Keeper of the Count answered the first: if she is away no household is kept, so no lot is drawn; the Count knots the nights as away and the nights she comes back; the seventh place stays empty and is named to no one; each household's niche stays lit by its own hearth, as the old lamps are. The Warden of the Door said each house tends its own flame, and where one goes out the household asks a neighbor to light it from a kept niche, by the shrine word, and the Warden of the Door carries the word of who asked. The Warden of the Door answered the second: a question put to the guest while she is away is held, knotted on a cord of questions at the Warden's own door, and answered by no one in her name; the seat added that anyone who says "the guest says" of a thing she has not said answers to the Bench. The Warden of the Wall answered the third: he wants the day she goes and the road and the day she means to be back, and word if the day changes. The wall will hold her door empty a month past the day, and then say so to the seat. He will send no spear after her and none with her, and will tell the court nothing of her in her absence that is not already known. The seat, the Steward and the Keeper of the Count answered the fourth. A stranger who asks for her by name sits at the table three nights as any guest and is asked his business on the third. The wall walks him to the guest house yard from the gate and watches it as it has watched her. The Warden of the Door would carry the table to him as she carried it to her. The Steward said the bread is the court's and the count is his. The seat said the court would tell him the guest is away and where she went only if the guest left word of it, and would not speak for her.

The guest listened to the end. She said what her decisions fix: that she would write what they said down as a rule and keep it, and that she did not leave. The Keeper of the Count knotted a cord of his own, a knot to an answer. The Warden of the Wall said afterward to the captain that a ruler who asks what is to be done when she is gone is a ruler, and that it was an honest thing, and that he had not said it to her. The seat spoke from the wall at dusk that the guest had asked what the court would do if she were away and was not going, and had been told, and that a keeper who asks is a keeper who means to be back. The town did not hear it so. In the lowest streets it was said she was going west to the one in the delta, and the old woman of the lower town who is the last to say the old tale said the guest would walk out one night in the dark as she came.

The new Warden of the Door said to the seat that evening, in front of the old man, that she took the guest for the awaited. She said she had waited for the guest to tell her what she was and had been told nothing, and that a keeper who goes to a father's door and says she was wrong, and asks what the town would do before she needs it, is the one who keeps the empty place. She did not say what the guest is. The old Warden of the Door said he had told her so three years ago.

**The hamlets.** From the second month of spring into the dry months the guest went out to the hamlets within a morning's walk of the wall, with the days told to the Warden of the Wall beforehand and a half score of the watch whom he gave when asked and chose himself. She lit a flame only where asked. She did not go into any pen, net, narrow lane or barred door. At two hamlets the lane to the headman's house was too narrow and the headman came out to her. The headmen asked her about the star, the iron, the salt and the dim nights. The woman at the field's edge who has said she would come out came out and sat on the fence and did not speak, and the guest did not ask.

**The star, month 8, night 17.** It dimmed while she was in one of the western hamlets, a morning's walk from the wall, awake, the headman at the edge of the field. The whole hamlet saw it and the town saw it from the wall. No death fell in the town that night. At dawn the headman asked whether it was she. I have no word of hers this year I can write; the answer she has given the headmen in the years before is the answer the people repeat.

**The carriers.** The salt carriers came in month 8 with iron at last year's price. In Salt A the Steward bought little of their salt and the carriers said they carry what they are given; in Salt B he bought what he could and asked them hard for word of the party until the party came.

**Salt B, the party home.** In month 7 or 8, at the high water, the party came up the river. The town met it at the quay, and the court heard it in the guest house yard.

**The Warden of the Wall.** In the spring the first plough and the first spears from the older iron and the soft were tried again, and he tested the wall's spearheads, a score at a time, against a post. He said that the soft iron bent where the older did not, and that the old heads were fewer than they were. The soft ones are set by for the beacon stores. He told the seat, as last year, that it was the iron and not the guest.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

The river was low and the dry months dry enough for stone cutting. Four parts of the five went into the wall's footing and the town's own work. The fifth was cut for the harbor, to the cord, for next year's order. The old cutter said the picks wore twice as fast and that the harbor's stone would cost the town more iron than it was worth, and the Steward heard him. The cutters left the lowest cuts as they were, with the ring of loose stones round the three. No one told them to.

**The star, summer.** On month 10, night 5, it dimmed, no lot, she awake in the yard, no death in the town. On month 11, night 19, it dimmed, a lot night, she lying still in the yard, two households kept; an old woman of the lower town died that night in a house not kept, quietly. On month 12, night 11, it dimmed, no lot, she awake in the yard, no death in the town. The wall's watch and the Keeper agree the hour each time: about an hour, in the middle of the night. Every night was clear and everyone awake saw it. The town said the star was dimming oftener again.

**The new Warden of the Door.** She kept the guest's asks all year: to the yard of the watch, the second house, the Keeper of the Count, the Night Sitter's work yard, the hamlets and the court's four. She taught the leader the words and looked through the baggage. She told the guest in the spring that the old Warden of the Door still counsels her, and that she keeps two lamps and one mind.

**The Keeper's reading.** At the turn of the year the Keeper of the Count laid the cords on the Count's table. This year seven dim nights, last year six, the year before five, the year before three: twenty-one in all, three remembered and eighteen counted. A dying fell in the town on seven of the twenty-one, two of them this year. By the roll of the town's dead, he said, a dying falls in the town about one night in four, so seven of twenty-one is a little over what the roll alone would give, and he did not know what to make of that. Of this year's seven she was awake on five and lying on two, and he would not say asleep. The roll of kept households has gone from about three hundred and thirty to four hundred and fifteen to five hundred and twenty to about five hundred and ninety, and the dim nights from three to five to six to seven, and he said again that two things that rise together are two things that rise together, and the cord does not say why. The young said again the star pays for the flames. The old said again that more nights are not different nights.

**The wall at year's end.** The Warden of the Wall has not taken the guest for the awaited and has not refused her. His men sat her door a third winter, told what they sit against and what the standing body is, and none asked off. The way to the gate is still staked and kept clear. He said to the seat that the flames near her dip at the same hour every dawn after the three nights and that the wall has stopped being surprised.

**The end of the year.** The grain was cut and brought in, an ordinary harvest again. The roll stands at about 590. The old Warden of the Door is alive and at his lamp, about seventy-three. The seat is well. The seventh place of the guest's keeping is still empty. In Salt A the stone was lifted and the town has its price; in Salt B the town waited the winter. In both the guest has not left.
