# Year 5: the unnamed people of god-dreams

The year opens in autumn. Months are counted from the opening: month 1 is the first month of autumn, months 4 to 6 are winter, 7 to 9 spring, 10 to 12 summer. Offices are named by office; no mortal has said an office holder's name aloud this year. Where a thing turns on what the god's power did, I write only what was seen and said, and the report sends it to the Keeper. Two things this year are attempts the Keeper settles: the court's party to the river's mouth and the harbor, and the guest's walk to the harbor. Each is written both ways, briefly. Party A is the party home before the hard cold; Party B is the party not home until spring. Walk A is the guest at the wall by the day named; Walk B is the guest not yet home when the year turns. Where nothing is marked, the year is the same either way.

What the town knew at the opening, as the Keeper's line on Year 4 gave it. The stone party grounded on a bar and lost three days, found no ship at the mouth in ten days, stacked the stone above the high water, and the leader and a pair of runners went round the shore to the harbor. The factor gave bread and three nights, sold salt for linen and oil at the standing price, said his house's ship lifts the stone at the first fair weather after the gales, and would not have the guest's word carried on to the delta. All nine were home in the second week of month 4 with a little salt. The guest's word did not go on. The oldest barrow singer died in month 3 and no one holds the third song. The dimmings of the star are not known to be anyone's doing. Where my Year 4 file read otherwise, the Keeper's line is what happened.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

The grain came in as in most years, the threshing in the barns, stubble on the bottom land, mist in the mornings, the river low.

**The yard of the watch, month 1, day 4.** In the morning the Warden of the Door took the guest to the yard of the watch behind the south-east gate, with two runners. The Warden of the Wall stood by, with his captain and the four men who sit her door. The captain had asked last year what the wall has been sitting if a lying hind is not her in a dream. She answered him as her decisions fix. She said she does not know. She does not sleep and has no memory of lying down on those nights. She would not say it is a dream and would not say it is not. A body that stands and murmurs is her in a sleeper's dream. A body that lies still is something she cannot name, and it is hers, so the wall sat her and she cannot tell them what she was doing in it. She asked them to keep sitting it as they have and to write what they see: whether it breathes slow or fast, whether the ears turn, whether the lamp on the cord dips, whether it stirs to a hand. She asked that they tell her the next morning, and asked nothing else of them. She said again that if fire comes they are to take her by the cord and lead her out.

To the Warden of the Wall, who had asked which house is nearest to a broken body, she said: the one nearest where it breaks that keeps a lamp burning through the night, and it need not be a house kept for her, since nearly every hearth in the town burns a lamp till dawn and any will do. She said she comes back after a season, whole, and may come back lacking some small thing she knew, a name or a song, and not know which. She said she does not ask them to carry her there and asks them to give her bread.

What the wall made of it. The captain said he had his answer and it was no answer, and that the wall would write what it saw, which was what it was asked for. The Warden of the Wall heard her to the end. He asked nothing. He said to the captain, on the way back, that a god who says she does not know what her own body is has said more than a god who says she does, and that he did not know if that was a comfort. The captain had the four write a tablet for each lot night from the first night of winter. The Keeper of the Count was given the tablets each dawn to lay beside his cord.

What the town made of it, over the next days. The wall's men said the guest had told them she does not know what lies in the yard, and that she asked to be written down. The lowest streets, who had kept their lamps bright since last year over the break, said now that she might not know what comes back, and some said she would come back "lacking" and be a child of the house after all. The storytelling households said a keeper who may come back lacking a song is a keeper who knows what a song is worth. The elder of the Gate Bench who is against her said that a god who does not know her own body is a guest that needs a town that does. The two for her said she had said it plainly and that was the custom.

**The same dusk.** The guest gave the Keeper of the Count, through the Warden of the Door, her plain list of the days of her fourth year on which she was afraid, as she felt it, and said she does not know whether it and his cord of the seven dim nights agree, and will say so whichever way they fall. The Keeper of the Count, dry as before, wrote it onto a cord of his own, a knot to a day, and said he would set it beside the cord with his word of awake or lying against each night, and say what the two show and no more. I do not have her list.

Through the Warden of the Door she told the Warden of the Door that she had meant up and not down: the town lies four days up the river from its mouth, and she had said it wrong or it was heard wrong. She thanked her for finding that the roll at the quay was the source of the other error, and asked the Steward whether the roll is struck now. The Warden of the Door told the Steward at the Store's door the next morning. The Steward said it was struck the week after the second house, and read over by the quay keeper and a runner together, and that since the spring the keeper at the west road gate strikes a party's names when it comes in by that road, so that a party home by either road is struck. He said it plainly and said no more. The Warden of the Door said she had thought as much and wished it had been asked sooner.

**The barrow, month 1, day 9.** By day, with the Warden of the Door to take her through and a man of the young households who had asked, the guest went to the foot of the barrows, where the lanes are wide. She said first what her decisions fix: that she could not have walked the old singer's sleep, for the old singer's house kept no flame and her power reaches no sleeper who keeps none, and that she would not have taken a song from her sleep in any case, since what a sleeper says there is the sleeper's. She asked to hear the two parts. The two young households sang them, the man and a woman of the second house and their children, and she sat on the grass and listened to the end and did not correct them or ask for more. Where they stopped, at the gap, she said nothing for a while. Then she asked the Warden of the Door to send the court's runners through the headmen to ask whether any old singer in any hamlet holds the third part, and to tell the singers' households where the answers are. She told them that if a singer of the barrow households keeps a flame and is dying, and the household asks her, she will sit the last sleep, and that what is sung there fades unless it is sung again awake, so it is best sung awake first. She said she presses no household to keep a flame.

What the barrow made of it. The man of the young household who had asked said they had asked in earnest a year ago and had no answer, and now had one that was not the one they feared and not the one they hoped. His wife said the guest listened as the old woman listened. The storytelling households said the guest had said what the old woman's house said, that a sleeper's song is the sleeper's, and the lowest streets said a keeper who will not take a song is a keeper who will not take a town's. The household of the old woman said nothing; the daughter of the house came to the barrow that afternoon and stood at the edge and went home.

**The runners for the third song, months 1 to 3.** On the twelfth day of month 1 the Warden of the Door asked the seat, and the seat sent the court's runners through the headmen of all the hamlets to ask the old singers whether any holds the third part, and told the singers' households where the answers would be sent. Six runners went, none a spear, two to a road, walking the hamlets within a morning's walk first and the three farther ones last. They were back in month 3, the last in the third week: no singer in any hamlet holds the third part. The oldest of the hamlet people remember there was a third part, and some can give the first words of its second turn, and none can give its last. The runners said that at the farthest hamlet an old woman wept, and said her mother sang it at the harvest. The two young households have what they had, two parts in three. The Warden of the Door gave the answers to the two households at the foot of the barrows, and they heard them and went home. The guest was told through the Warden of the Door and said nothing that is written.

**The star, month 2, night 9.** The star dimmed, the blue deepening for about an hour, and stood dim in the middle of the night, and was as before. No lot had been drawn. She was awake in the yard under it, the wall's two men at her door said, and said nothing. No death fell in the town. The Keeper of the Count knotted the cord at dawn with the hour from the watch's turn.

**The harbor's order, month 2.** In the first days of the month, with the grain safe, the guest asked through the Warden of the Door and by the throne's leave that the court judge this year's standing order for the harbor house. She asked that the Steward of the Store and the old cutter judge what stone could be spared against the wall's and the footings' need, and whether to send the full sixty blocks, fewer, or none, since the picks wear twice as fast and the old iron is a third down. She asked the Steward to ask the factor, by the court's leader, whether his house will sell iron or picks in place of salt for part of the stone, and whether the iron in the harbor is soft too. She said she does not know why the iron is soft and does not claim it or blame anyone for it. She asked that the leader, if the court sends one, learn at the river's mouth whether the house's ship lifted the stone that lay there, and tell the court. She said again that the people who carry it are the court's and carry its word, that she sends no flame, lamp or token, and no spears.

The Steward and the old cutter walked the stacks at the scar and the footings over three days. The old cutter, about sixty-three, said what he has said: four parts in five of the dry months' cutting go to the wall and the fifth is what the scar can spare, and no more should be promised. The fifth of last summer's cutting stood stacked at the scar, sixty blocks to the harbor's cord. He said the picks were wearing twice as fast and the old iron was a third down and would not last another year at this wear, and that the wall's footing took a pick a man a season where it took half a pick. He asked that forty go and twenty be held at the scar against the picks, to go when the iron comes or be set to the footing if it does not. The Steward agreed, and said forty is what two planked boats carry without sitting low on the bars. The elder of the Gate Bench who is against the guest said the stone is the town's footing and the guest was asking it for strangers a third time. The seat answered that the court was judging, the town was the one that would sell, and the bargain was the town's.

The Warden of the Wall did not ask for two of his men. He said he had said he would not ask a fourth time, and that he would say only that stone is a thing a man will rob a boat for. The Keeper of the Count asked what was to go beside the stone. The seat said the guest's two messages, and he said the Count would knot what went. On the eighth day the seat accepted the order for forty and said the party should go.

The Steward chose the leader: the same man, about forty-three, chief keeper of the trade cords, who has led the last three parties and spoken the words on the third night. The Steward said a man who has been to the quays three times knows the way of the bars and of the factor's yard, and the leader did not ask to be replaced. The party is seven in two planked boats of the court: the leader; the journeyman cutter, about thirty-six, to read the stone against the cord and speak for it; three runners named by the Warden of the Door, the young woman who has now been three times to the harbor and two of last year's nine; and two boatmen of the fishing households, one the man who knows the low-water bars. No third boat this year, for forty blocks. Goods: twenty bolts of linen, fifty pots in straw and reed baskets, ten jars of flax oil, the forty blocks, and a guest's gift for the factor's table, four bolts of linen, six pots and two jars of oil, set apart by the Steward, with three bolts and two jars more set apart as a gift for any carrier not of the factor's house who will take the guest's word to the delta. No bronze. No lamp, no wick and no ember from any niche; the Warden of the Door had the baggage looked through at the quay, and the first fire is struck from flint on the river bank.

The words they carry are the guest's, two messages, and the leader had them by heart after three evenings in the middle of the month in the Warden of the Door's house, where the Warden of the Door gave them and added nothing. The name in them was said aloud in that house, by the leader and by the Warden of the Door, each evening. This year the first message said "four days up the river from its mouth" and the leader said it so and said that his cords have it so. The first, for the one in the delta who keeps the weights, asks him to meet her face to face at a gate as a guest meets a house, and tells him where she is so that he may come or not, and says she does not send anyone to bring him. The second, for the factor, thanks his table, asks how many days the delta lies from his harbor by the coast and by ship, who goes there, and whether anyone outside his house would carry a word for pay, and carries the court's own question about iron and picks and whether his iron is soft. The orders are the guest's, as given to the court. The party is the court's guest at the harbor: bread, three nights, no questions of its business until the third. The leader gives the factor the second message and the court's own words, and does not ask him to carry the first. He holds the first, and gives it to any carrier who is not of the factor's house, a coasting ship's crew or a free trader going to the delta, for linen or oil from the Steward's goods, if the factor does not object to that. If there is none he brings it home and says so. He does not go on himself; the seat did not allow it. If the stranger's name is asked of him he says he does not know it and that the one who reads the scales will do. If he is asked questions he answers only what was seen and told: the guest woke on the stony ground above the town in the dark before an autumn morning, does not know why, the seat takes her for the awaited, and she keeps a lamp that she lights in a house that asks. He does not say what the lamp does, does not say it can be taken, does not give or sell a flame or lamp of hers, does not say how many days she walks or which nights she keeps; if pressed he says he does not know. If the stranger says it is a god, he says what he heard and says no more. He brings home any answer and any word of another like her on that coast.

The way. By the court's boats down the river to the mouth (112.4 miles, 4 days down, 10 days back up). The boatman who knows the bars goes first with a pole, and they take the first autumn rain's water and not before. At the mouth the leader means to look for the stone that lay there, and to learn whether the house's ship lifted it. If a ship is at the mouth, the new stone goes to it and he goes on in it or on foot to the harbor. If none comes in ten days he means to stack the stone above the high water under hides and reed with the boatmen and the cutter, and go on round the shore on foot with the linen, the oil and two runners (97.9 miles, 7 days on foot, longer laden) to ask the factor. He will not put the river boats to sea. They set out from the quay on the twentieth day of month 2.

**The star, month 2, night 22.** It dimmed again, a lot night, two households kept. The wall's two men said she was lying still in the yard with the lamp lit. An old woman of the lower town died that night, quietly, in a kept house; no one had asked her to walk her sleep. The tag went on the cord. The party was two days down the river.

**Late autumn.** The salt carriers came in the last weeks with a small load at last year's price. The Steward bought a part and no more, and the late pigs were killed after the first frost. Iron came down from the mining town grey and soft, as for two winters now, and the smiths drew it out. The carriers said they carry what they are given.

**What the leader found at the river's mouth.** I have not been told it; it is the Keeper's. What the town knows at the close of the autumn is only that the party has gone down.

## The harbor party, both ways

The year that follows is written for either, with the difference marked where there is one. What they meet beyond the river's mouth, what the factor answers, and whether any carrier outside his house takes the guest's word on to the delta are the Keeper's, and I write nothing of them.

**Party A. Home before the hard cold.** The party came up the river in the last days of month 3 or the first of month 4, before the hard cold, with the harbor's answer and the terms for the year, what salt it could buy, and what the leader learned at the mouth. The court heard it in the guest house yard, the seat, the Steward, the old cutter, the Warden of the Door, the Keeper of the Count, and the guest. The late pigs were cured as they ought to be and the Steward's winter count was easier.

**Party B. Not home until spring.** No word came in autumn or winter. The late pigs were cured lightly and the meat went early. The Steward counted it aloud and said the party might be wintering at the harbor or elsewhere or not. The party came up at the high water in month 7 or 8 with the harbor's answer and what salt it could bring, and the court heard it on the quay and in the guest house yard; in that case the guest did not hear the leader in winter and heard him in spring.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The winter was cold in the ordinary way. From the first night of winter the wall's men sat her door, two a night by lot, not from kept houses, four on the named nights, each told by the captain before his first night what he sits against. The captain had the four write a tablet for each lot night, and gave the tablets to the Keeper of the Count at dawn.

**What the wall wrote.** On the lot nights of the winter the tablets say the same, night to night, as the wall has seen it before. She lay still in the yard with the lamp on its cord lit, and did not stand. Her breathing was slow and even. The ears did not turn. The lamp did not dip in the night. On the nights a man laid his hand on the flank, as the captain had told them to do once on each of three nights, she did not stir to it. At dawn she was shaken awake, and the flames near her dipped, at the same hour. The captain carried the tablets to the Warden of the Wall, who read them and laid them by. The Keeper of the Count laid them beside the cord and wrote lying against each lot night. The guest, told the next morning by the Warden of the Door as she asked, said she thanked them. I do not have what else she said.

**The Steward counts aloud.** In month 4, by his own habit, the Steward counted before the council the grain, the fuel and the salt. Grain would reach the new harvest with a thin margin, as in the four years before. Fuel was enough and oil was enough. Salt: Party A, in hand, the meat safe, and he said so shortly; Party B, none by the second month of winter, and he said that too. Iron: the older stock put by for spearheads and picks was now down by a quarter more, and the new iron stayed soft.

**Keeping Sleep.** Two households a night by the Keeper of the Count's lot, deathbeds, newborns, fevers and fresh griefs first. Three only on the named nights of deep winter, each told the day before to the Warden of the Door and to the wall men at her door that same dusk, and told to the town by the seat's crier: month 5, night 14; month 6, night 3; month 6, night 25. The seventh place stayed empty. In kept houses that asked she walked the last sleep of the dying and gave no orders there. About nineteen of the winter's deaths fell in kept houses. The households say again that the dying were quiet and some spoke in the last hour of a hind or a lamp. Nothing was cured. What the four saw at the dawns of the three nights is the Keeper's.

**The Night Sitter's yard, month 4, day 18.** The Warden of the Door took the guest to the Night Sitter's work yard on a night the Night Sitter and the hamlet girl sat, with a child in fever and an old man in the lower street. The Night Sitter, asked, said she would have the hands for the lamp and the kettle and no more. The guest sat beside them and kept the kettle, and took no work. She told the hamlet girl plainly that her gift does not reach what the sitting takes from her, as the Keeper ruled. The hamlet girl said she had thought not. Through the Warden of the Door the guest asked the seat whether the girl's household is heard by the court on what she needs. The seat said the court heard it last winter and the bed and the bread stand, and that the Steward would send the girl's father a measure of grain in the sitting winters against the hands the household lacks, since a field hand who sits is a field hand lost. The child lived; the old man died near dawn with his household round him. The lowest streets said again the court was feeding the guest's pupils, and the Night Sitter said she had no pupil of the guest's and the Steward could count.

**Hearing the leader.** Party A: in the first week of month 4 the guest heard the leader in the yard of the watch with the Warden of the Door and the Steward, as she had asked, and asked him what the factor said of the iron, of the stone and of the road to the delta. What he said is the Keeper's, since it is what he heard; the court heard it with her. Party B: there was no leader to hear in winter, and the guest asked the Warden of the Door twice in the dark months whether there was word, and there was none.

**The Gate Bench, month 5.** Two neighbors of the lower town went to the Bench over a niche again: the household of one had let its kept flame go in the hard frost and asked its neighbor to light it from his; the neighbor would, and the old woman of the house would not, and then did. The Bench ruled as before that a flame is lit at a household's own asking and in no other way, and that a household that let it go and asks again is no less asking. The elder against the guest said that a flame that goes out and is asked back is a flame that wants minding, and the two for her laughed, which they do not often.

**The hard cold.** The hard frost came in the last week of month 6, with fevers; about eleven of the old and small died in the town and the lower hamlets in the week. The Night Sitter, her pupil and the hamlet girl sat by turns, the hamlet girl two nights in ten. On the twenty-fifth night three households were kept. No warm wind followed this year. The town said the wind had not come because the guest had kept the three and nobody had asked for it, and the elder against her said that wind follows frost in any country and it had not.

**The iron.** The iron the mining town sent down stayed grey and soft. A pick drawn from it blunts in a month. The Warden of the Wall tested the wall's spearheads in spring, a score at a time, against a post, and found fewer of the old than last year, and the soft ones bent again. He told the seat, as before, that it was the iron and not the guest.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

The river was high in spring, as in most years, and took no seed. The flood watch was kept by the headmen and the wall. The Steward lent seed to the lowest hamlet as before and did not press the old debt.

**The four offices, month 7, day 9.** The guest asked through the Warden of the Door, with the throne's leave, that the Warden of the Door bring the four offices to the hall. They did. The seat sat, the four stood, the guest stood in the hall's wide door, since a hind cannot pass the low inner one. The Warden of the Door spoke her words. She said what she means to do in the summer: to walk to the harbor, no farther, in the dry months, with the lamp on her waist, and be back before the end of the year. She asked the throne's leave to go. She asked the Warden of the Wall for four of the watch who will come of themselves, not spears, to walk beside her and guard her body on the road and at the harbor, and said she would tell him the days beforehand, as he asked, and asked what the road is like. She asked the Steward for a gift for the factor's table, linen and oil at his judgment, and for the leader of last year's party, who knows the road, to come as guide if he will. She asked the Keeper of the Count to name two households a night while she is gone and not more, and to see the chain of flames tended as the four told her they would. She asked the Warden of the Door to hold her place and to answer a question put to her while she is away with that it waits. She reminded them what the four said last spring, and said she keeps to it. If the throne said no, or the Warden of the Wall said the road was not safe, she would not go, and would say so to the whole court, and would walk the hamlets within a morning's walk of the wall instead.

What the four said. The Warden of the Door: she would hold the place and the knotted cord of questions at her door as the rule says, and said she had asked herself whether she was afraid for the guest, and was, and said so. The Keeper of the Count: two a night, drawn from the roll as at home, knotted as away nights and kept nights both, and the lots drawn by his deputy, a woman of the Count's, since he would not be at the guest's door; he asked what the Count should write of a night on which she lies still far off, and the seat said write what is seen. The Steward: the gift would be four bolts of linen, six pots and two jars of oil to the factor's table, which is the court's, and the leader would come as guide if he would; the leader, sent for, said he would, and said he did not know that he was a guide, only a man who had been. The Warden of the Wall: he asked the day she would go and the road and the day she meant to be back, and said the road is not a road he knows. The leader, asked in the hall, said what he had seen of it, and no more: the river to its mouth, the bars, the shore round to the harbor, the factor's yard. The Warden of the Wall said four would come, and chose none: the captain put it to the watch in the yard that night, and four came of themselves, a man of the four who sit her door, two from the south-east gate, and a man of a hamlet family who said his hamlet had had her flame. He told the seat that he would not have gone and would not have stopped them. The seat gave leave from the wall at dusk and said the guest would be back before the year turned, and that the town would keep its own nights.

What the town made of it. The lowest streets said she is going west to the one in the delta, as they said last year, and the old woman of the lower town who keeps the old tale said she would walk out one night in the dark as she came. The seat had said it was the harbor and no farther, and was not heard. The storytelling households said a guest who asks leave to go and names a day to be back is a guest who is not leaving. The elder against her said a woman who walks to the sea is a woman who is a day nearer the other god than she was.

**The star, month 7, night 15.** It dimmed, no lot, she awake in the yard, the wall's two men said. No death in the town. The Keeper of the Count knotted it. It was the third this year, twenty-four in all. A dying fell in the town on eight of the twenty-four.

**Party B, the party home.** In month 7 or 8 at the high water the party came up the river. The town met it at the quay and the court heard it in the guest house yard, as in Year 4. This is written only if Party B.

**The carriers.** The salt carriers came in month 8 with iron at last year's price, grey and soft as before. Party A: the Steward bought little of their salt, and the carriers said they carry what they are given. Party B: he bought what he could and asked them hard for word of the party until it came.

**Preparing.** In the last weeks of spring the guest told the Warden of the Wall the days beforehand. She set out on the fifth day of month 10 and named the twenty-eighth day of month 11 as the latest day she would turn for home, and the twentieth day of month 12 as the day she meant to be at the wall. The Warden of the Wall wrote them in the wall's tablets. He said the wall would hold her door empty a month past the day and then say so to the seat. The Keeper of the Count's deputy was told the first night of the two households. The Warden of the Door had the cord of questions hung at her own door.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

The river was low and the dry months dry enough for stone cutting. Four parts of the five went into the wall's footing and the town's own work; the fifth was cut for the harbor, to the cord, for next year's order, with the twenty blocks held from last year's still stacked at the scar. The old cutter said the picks wore twice as fast as they ought and that he had one pick in three left of the old iron. The cutters left the lowest cuts as they were, with the ring of loose stones round the three. No one told them to.

**The guest sets out, month 10, day 5.** At dawn, from the guest house yard, by the south-east gate, with the four of the watch, the leader as guide, the lamp on its cord on her waist, and the Warden of the Door and the seat's crier to see her to the gate. The seat spoke from the wall the evening before that she goes to the harbor and back, and that the town keeps its own nights. The Warden of the Door walked beside her as far as the river road and said what she says to no one else. The town stood in the street. Some wept; some said she would not be back. The old woman of the lower town who keeps the old tale said nothing and lit her lamp at noon. The Warden of the Door told the seat that evening that she had not seen the guest so quiet and not afraid, and that she did not know what to make of either.

**How the town kept its nights while she was away.** No lot was drawn at her door. The Keeper of the Count's deputy drew two households a night from the roll, deathbeds, newborns, fevers and fresh griefs first, and knotted the nights as away nights with the two households' names beside. Each household's niche stayed lit by its own hearth as the old lamps are; where one went out, the household asked a neighbor to light it from a kept niche by the shrine word, and the Warden of the Door's runners carried who asked. The guest's lean-to stood empty and the way to the gate stayed staked. The wall held her door empty, a man of the watch walking the yard as he would any empty yard, and the captain said that was the wall's rule and not a thing owed her. The Warden of the Door hung the cord of questions at her door and knotted onto it, one to a question, what was asked of the guest while she was away. The lowest streets kept their lamps bright, and said the hind had gone to look for the child of the house.

**Questions put while she was away.** Three were knotted on the cord. A headman of a western hamlet sent to ask whether the guest would come to the flood line this year, since the weir below his field had come down in the winter thaw. The woman of the lowest street who asked last year after the warm wind sent to ask whether the guest had gone to see if the wind would come. A man of the barrow households sent to ask whether she would hear the third song again in the winter, with what they now had and a verse they had had from the far hamlet's old woman. The Warden of the Door answered each as she was told to by the rule: that it waits, and is on the cord. No one answered in the guest's name. The seat said from the wall once, in month 11, that anyone who says "the guest says" of a thing she has not said answers to the Bench. A man of the lower town said it anyway of the weir, and the Bench fined him a day's labor.

**The Keeper's reading.** At the turn of the year, with the guest still on the road or home as the Keeper's pass two settles, the Keeper of the Count laid the cord on the table. This year three dim nights, last year seven, the year before six: twenty-four in all, three remembered and twenty-one counted. A dying fell in the town on eight of the twenty-four, one of them this year. By the roll of the town's dead, he said, a dying falls in the town about one night in four, so eight of twenty-four is a third, which is what the roll alone would give, and he did not know what to make of that. Of this year's three she was awake on two and lying on one, and he would not say asleep. The roll of kept households has gone from about three hundred and thirty to four hundred and fifteen to five hundred and twenty to five hundred and ninety to about six hundred and five, and the dim nights from three to five to six to seven to three, and he said again that two things that rise together are two things that rise together, and that the star has now fallen while the roll rose, and that this is a third thing the cord does not say why. The young said again the star pays for the flames and that it had been paid. The old said again that more nights are not different nights and that fewer are not either. The cord laid beside it of her afraid days, he said, the town could read, and he would not read it for them.

**What the town makes of the star.** The three dim nights were the fewest since Year 1. The storytelling households said the star was resting, and that a keeper who goes out to the sea for a season is a keeper whose star is less afraid. The lowest streets said the star had dimmed fewer times because she had been less afraid, since the Warden of the Wall's men had begun to write what they see and a thing that is written is a thing that is less feared. The Keeper of the Count said the tablets and the cord both show what they show.

**The wall.** The Warden of the Wall has not taken the guest for the awaited and has not refused her. His men sat her door a fourth winter, told what they sit against, and wrote it, and none asked off. He told the seat that the wall has begun to know what it is writing, and that it does not know what it has written. The four of the watch who went with her he did not choose and did not stop.

**The new Warden of the Door.** She held the place and the cord all summer and said to the seat in month 12 that the guest had asked her to hold it and that she did not think she would hold it so easily a second time. The old Warden of the Door, about seventy-four, is alive and at his lamp and said to her that a place held is a place kept, and that his had been held fifty years for one who came.

## The year's end, both ways

The grain was cut and brought in, an ordinary harvest again. The roll stands at about 605. The old Warden of the Door is alive. The seat is well. The seventh place of the guest's keeping is still empty. The dim nights were three.

**Walk A.** The guest was at the wall by the day named, the twentieth of month 12, at dusk, with the four of the watch and the guide, and the Warden of the Wall wrote it in the tablets and the Warden of the Door lit the empty place's lamp beside hers. What she met on the road and at the harbor, and what she says of it, are the Keeper's. The town met her at the gate. She had the Warden of the Door's cord of questions read to her in the guest house yard and answered the three. The weir and the song are written in Year 6.

**Walk B.** The guest was not at the wall by the day named. The Warden of the Wall held her door empty, and on the twentieth of month 1 of the new year he will say so to the seat. The Warden of the Door kept the cord of questions at her door with the three on it and said to the seat that the guest said she would be back before the year turned and that she has not said she would not. The town said again she had gone to the one in the delta, and the old woman said she had walked out in the dark as she came. The seat said from the wall that a guest who is late is a guest who is late, and that the table keeps her place. Whatever kept her is the Keeper's.

**The harbor party at the year's close.** Party A is home and its terms are in the court's hands, and the Steward has the order for next year. Party B is home since the spring and the order is known. In both, the guest's first message has gone as far as the Keeper says it went; the town knows only what the leader said when he came home.
