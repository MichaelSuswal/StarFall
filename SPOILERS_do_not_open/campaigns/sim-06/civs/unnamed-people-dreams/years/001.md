# Year 1: the unnamed people of god-dreams

The year opens in autumn. Months are counted from the opening: month 1 is the first month of autumn, months 4 to 6 are winter, 7 to 9 spring, 10 to 12 summer. Offices are named by office; no mortal has said an office holder's name aloud this year. Where a thing turns on what the god's power did, I write only what was seen and said, and the report sends it to the Keeper.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**The first morning.** The grain was in, the river low, mist on the bottom land. At first light two of the wall watch at the south-east gate saw a figure on the stony ground above the town, walking down: a woman to the waist, a hind below it, a lit lamp on a cord at her waist, alone. She came to the gate at a hind's walk and stopped a rope's length outside it. She asked for the Warden of the Door and asked to be let in as a guest, for the bread and the place the custom gives for three nights. The watch did not open the gate wide. One ran for the Warden of the Door; the other stood with his spear grounded and said nothing. Asked who she was, she said she was called Vael, that she woke on the stony ground above the town in the dark before the morning, that she did not know why, and that she had come to be a guest. She did not push the gate and did not leave. The wall watch say she waited most of a morning in plain sight with the lamp lit in daylight, and that the dogs of the gate street would not come near and would not bark.

The Warden of the Door is old and walked out himself. He asked her name again and she gave it again. He gave her the words of the custom, bread and a place for three nights and no question of business until the third. The gate took her width without trouble; it takes a loaded cart. The Warden of the Door had the Dusk Table of his own household carried out into the yard of the court's guest house that evening, because a hind cannot pass a low door and the custom does not let a guest eat apart. The empty place's lamp, which the Warden of the Door has kept lit at his own hearth since he took the office, was carried out and set beside her. This is the single act of the year that the old tell most: that the Warden of the Door moved the table and not the guest.

**The three nights.** She sat where she was put, ate what she was given, and spoke of nothing. She asked small things: the names of the streets, what the weather does in the second month, what the children are called. She was watched by a great many people from the yard wall. The wall watch changed turns oftener than they needed to. Children were counted at dusk as always and counted twice in the lane of the guest house. The Warden of the Wall came on the second night, looked, and went away without speaking to her.

On the third night, by custom, she was asked her business. She said what her decisions fix: that she was called Vael, that she woke on the stony ground, that she did not know why, that she had come to be a guest. Asked what the lamp was, she said it was a lamp, and that it made a flame which might be set in a house's niche, that she lit it only where it was asked for, and that it was not a due. Asked by the Warden of the Door whether she was the guest the town keeps the empty place for, I have no word of hers I can write as fixed; the Warden of the Door says that she did not say yes and did not say no, and that he took her for the one awaited all the same, quietly, and said so to no one that night but the throne.

**Two nights the star dimmed in this month.** The first fell some nights after the guesting ended, the second some days after that. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour and the star stood dim in the middle of the night, then was as before. The whole town that was awake saw it. Older people said they had seen such nights in other years. But in this month, with the hind in the guest house yard, the streets said otherwise. On the first night she was seen standing up from her lying place and looking at it, and saying nothing. On the second she was asleep, or still, and the yard was quiet.

**The throne.** The seat is a widowed woman of middle years, eleven winters on the throne, patient and well liked. She sent for the Warden of the Door on the fifth day and for the Keeper of the Count, the Steward of the Store and the Warden of the Wall, and she heard them one at a time and not together. The Warden of the Door spoke for her. The Warden of the Wall said she was a guest under the custom and that a guest is a guest, and that he would not call her more than that and would keep the wall as it was. The Steward of the Store said the bread she ate was the court's and the count was his. The Keeper of the Count asked what a household would have to do to ask for a flame, and was told by the Warden of the Door that it need only ask, and that none was to be shamed. The Gate Bench of three elders split: one for her, one against, one undecided, and they said so to the seat and not abroad.

The seat held that the guest's three nights were done and that the empty place was a custom for one guest awaited and not for any traveller. She would not shut the gate against a guest who had kept the custom, and she would not call her the awaited one before the cold. She let her stay on the court's bread in the yard of the guest house "until the Warden of the Door is sure". This was spoken from the wall at dusk, as the seat speaks to the whole people, in a few words: that a guest had come, that she had been given the table, that she lit a flame only where asked, that no house was bound to ask, and that no house which did not ask was to be shamed. The court's runners carried the same word to the hamlets.

**Late autumn, before the first hard cold.** The Warden of the Door asked, by the seat's leave, that a flame be lit at the court's own hearth from the guest's lamp. The seat took a night to think and said yes. She stood at the hearth herself. The flame was lit with the court gathered, the Keeper of the Count, the Warden of the Wall and the Steward of the Store all present; the Warden of the Wall stood last and nearest the door. The seat then spoke from the wall that the court's hearth held the flame, and that she, the seat, took the guest for the one awaited. She did not say that the guest spoke for the god or that the line took any word from her. By the records of the court, the sacred line holds the seat for the awaited one, and the line now says the guest has come and the seat is held in her name. This is the moment the seat and the Warden of the Door took Vael for the awaited. It is a belief of the court, and the Keeper should read it as belief.

After the court's hearth, households began to ask. Those first were the households of a deathbed, a newborn, a fever, a fresh grief, as she had asked. By the end of the month the Keeper of the Count had a roll of forty or so households that had asked, in the town, and a handful in the nearest hamlets. A household brought its clay lamp to the guest house yard, or a child brought a lit wick from a flame already kept, shielded by a hand through the lane, and the flame went to the niche by the hearth. Those who kept the flame call it "the guest's flame", and call their niche "kept".

The Keeper of the Count drew the first lots. Two households were named each dusk from among those that had asked and had not been kept. On the nights of Keeping the guest lay still in the yard with the lamp lit and a pair of the court's runners holding the door of the lean-to she had been given. At dawn she was shaken, and the flames near her dipped. She said when asked that she was afraid. The seventh place stayed empty.

The carriers from the harbor towns came in the last weeks of the season with less salt than was wanted, at last year's price or a little over. They saw the guest in the yard. They ate at the table for three nights, as carriers do, and the town noticed that they said little and watched much. They left with word of her.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The winter was ordinary, with its long nights. The guest stayed inside the walls, in the yard and its lean-to, and did not walk the streets without the Warden of the Door or a runner beside her. The seat's runners kept the lanes clear on the days she was taken to the court.

**The Steward counts aloud.** At her asking, through the Warden of the Door, the Steward of the Store counted before the council the grain, the fuel and the salt. Grain would reach the new harvest with a thin margin and no more; fuel was enough for an ordinary winter; salt was the shortfall, and the Steward said aloud, for the first time before the council, that if the salt did not come the meat would go. The curing of the late pigs was cut to what the salt allowed, and more fresh meat was eaten early than the town would have wished. This is the winter's cost and it is not hers.

**The Night Sitter.** The Warden of the Door took her, at her asking, to the Night Sitter and her pupil. The guest sat beside the Night Sitter through a night with a child in fever and a very sick old man, on the lower street, in the Night Sitter's own work yard, and did not work. The Night Sitter sang low, as she does, and steadied the breathing of the child, and did not cure it; the child lived, as most do. The Night Sitter did not sleep the three nights after and her hands shook, as they do. She said afterwards to the Warden of the Door that the guest had asked her leave and had kept it, and that she had not felt the work take her faster for the guest sitting near. She said also that she liked the company. Her pupil sat on the other side. The Night Sitter's way of being, that she wears out, did not change this year.

**Keeping Sleep, and the dying.** Through the winter, on most nights while she was in the town, the two households drawn by lot were kept. In households that keep the flame, a dying person was, by the households' word, not alone: the household said afterwards that the dying one had been quiet at the last, and that some had spoken in the last hour of a hind or of a lamp. There were in the town, in the winter, about a dozen deathbeds in households that kept the flame. People died in them as they would have; nothing was cured. What the households say was seen is in the report as a matter of the god's power, not mine.

The guest told the court, more than once, that a dream is a dream and not a command, and gave no word through any dream. Some households did not believe this and sat up for orders. None came.

**The Gate Bench's first dispute.** In month 5 a household of the lower town that had not asked for the flame was refused a place at the Dusk Table by its neighbors, who said a house that would not keep the guest's flame had something to hide. The household went to the Gate Bench. The Bench ruled, the two for and the one against agreeing on this though on little else, that no house was to be shamed for not asking, since the guest herself had asked that it be so and the seat had said it from the wall. The household was given its place. The elder who was against the guest said he ruled with the guest's word and not for it.

**The third night of the dimming.** One night in month 5, in the middle of the winter, the star dimmed again, with the same deepening blue, for about an hour. The same night a kept household in the town lost an old woman, quietly. By morning the street said the star dims for the dying. The Warden of the Door said nothing to this. The old ones who had seen such nights in other years said it had come in their grandfathers' time on nights when nobody died. No one persuaded anyone.

**The mood in the winter.** There were two roads of feeling. Many households were glad: the first deathbeds in a long hard winter that had a quiet in them. A smaller number, mostly in the lowest streets, kept their ancestors' lamps lit from the old flame only and said the hind was the keeper of the dark come to the table at last, and that a keeper comes for a reason. The Warden of the Wall's six hundred were told nothing new, and he told the wall watch to keep the turns, to keep the beacon laid, and to take no spears out for the guest and none against her.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

The river rose as it does and took no hamlet's seed this year. The flood watch was kept by the headmen and the wall watch. The lowest hamlet planted late in the second week of the sowing window; the seat's Steward lent seed again against last year's debt and did not press for the old balance.

**The hamlets.** In the third week of the spring, when the road could be walked, the guest went out with an escort of the Warden of the Wall's choosing, a score of his wall watch, along the road to the nearest hamlets. She walked the bottom land and the flood line with the headmen. She asked each hamlet what it wanted. She lit a flame only where asked. She learned which hamlets had a Night Sitter; of the hamlets she visited, one in three or four had one. She did not go into any pen, net, narrow lane or barred door. At two hamlets the lane that led to the headman's house was too narrow and too barred for her to pass, and the headman came out to the bridge or the edge of the field and she stood there. The headmen told her what they feared: the river, the late seed, salt, and a hard winter. They also told her what they thought of her. At one hamlet a woman would not come to the field's edge and said so; the headman told the guest, and the guest said she would not ask her to.

The headmen said among themselves that she did not ask for anything. They did not know what to do with that.

The Warden of the Wall kept the beacon watch as it was and told her of no stranger on the roads, for there was none. The Steward of the Store went with her part of the way and asked, with the headmen, where salt might be had from neighbours and what the town had to trade. Linen, pots, cut stone and grain were named. Nothing new was learned, and the Steward said so.

By the end of spring the roll of households that had asked stood at about two hundred and fifty in the town and about sixty in the hamlets. The households that keep the flame have begun to pass it themselves, from niche to niche, to kin and neighbours. The Keeper of the Count enters these when told.

**The carriers.** The salt carriers came again in the spring with less than the winter had cost. The Steward asked them what the harbor towns said of salt. They said the price would hold or rise. They said they had told the harbor towns of the guest, and that the harbor towns had asked what she was. The Steward said she was a guest.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

The dry months were dry enough for the stone cutting.

**The scar.** In the first month of summer the guest went up to the scar where stone is cut, with the Warden of the Door and two runners, and sat with the cutters through a day. A cutter, an older man, asked if she knew who made the lowest cuts. I do not have her answer from her; the cutters say she looked at the cuts a long time. Another asked whether she was a giant's kin. The cutters laughed, and some did not laugh. She walked the wall afterwards with the Warden of the Wall and asked what it lacked. The Warden of the Wall said it lacked a settled stretch of footing on the river side to be taken up and relaid, and the beacon platform's timber, which is rotted at the foot of two posts. The Steward of the Store gave the cut stone and timber. The cutters and the wall watch did the work in the dry months, and it was finished before the first rain of the autumn.

**The runners.** At the guest's asking, put through the Warden of the Door to the seat, two small parties of the court's runners were sent out in the second month of summer. One set out west by the road toward the Rudd Hills [r18]. One went by boat down the River Ellow [f07] toward its mouth. Each was of four runners, none a spear. They carried linen and pots as gifts, a word that the town keeps a table for travellers, and an instruction to learn what neighbours lie there, what salt they have, and to carry back no quarrel. Neither was back at the time the year's record was closed; what came of them is for the Keeper to price and is in the report.

**The Warden of the Door.** In the third month of summer the Warden of the Door told the seat, in front of the Keeper of the Count, that he would name his successor before the first frost of the new year and not before. He did not say whom.

**The Warden of the Wall.** At year's end he has not taken the guest for the awaited one and has not refused her. He keeps the beacon and the watch, tells her of strangers as he said he would, and chooses her escort. He told the seat in the last weeks of summer that the flames near the guest dip when she is afraid and that the wall watch have seen it, and that a guest who is afraid in the dark is a thing the wall must think about.

**The end of the year.** Grain was cut and brought in the last weeks of summer and the first of autumn, an ordinary harvest, the threshing to come in the barns. The lowest hamlet's seed was made good. Salt is short as ever. The roll of households keeping the flame stands at about three hundred and thirty, roughly two hundred and fifty in the town and eighty in the hamlets, and the households that do not keep it are not shamed, by the seat's word, the Bench's ruling and, on most streets, by custom. The seventh place of the guest's keeping is still empty. The court's hearth holds the flame.
