# Report 003: unnamed people of god-dreams, Year 3, pass one, for the Keeper

## How the god was met
Autumn, month 1, day 5. In the yard of the watch the guest answered the Warden of the Wall's three asks of last year through his captain: his four rules are hers to keep, and she keeps them; then she told the four men who sit her door what they sit against (the mornings after three households, and the dipping lamps) and what her body does when she walks a sleeper's dream (stands where it was, pale, slow, nearly without voice, easy to overlook and move), and asked that they let it stand, not wake her by force, and guard it. Warden of the Wall: heard her to the end; said the wall saw those mornings three times last winter and was not told; had the captain tell every man who sits her door before his first night, in her words; has not taken her word as the awaited and has not refused her. Captain: asked how a man is to tell the standing body from the sleeping one. One of the four: asked whether, if a fire came into the yard, they were to move her. Not answered that I can write.

Same dusk. Seat: spoke from the wall that a flame a household lights from its own kept flame is a shrine and not a household kept that night, and the cap of two a night stays. Keeper of the Count: received it and the guest's list of her afraid days, wrote the list onto a cord, said he had waited a year for one line. Warden of the Door: carried both. Households: kept houses glad; the lowest streets said a keeper that lets her flame pass from hand to hand wants it everywhere. Gate Bench elder against her: a flame that spreads of itself spreads where no one asked.

Month 2. The guest asked that the court judge the harbor house's offer. Seat: heard the offices one at a time and accepted on the twelfth day, holding the bargain as the town's. Steward of the Store and the old cutter: judged the stone; one part in five of a year's cutting is what can be spared, and a boat's load already stacked as the first. Elder against the guest: said the stone is the town's footing. Warden of the Wall: asked again for two of his men; refused again. Warden of the Door: named the runners and taught the leader the words, three evenings.

Winter. Night Sitter: took the guest's hands for the kettle and the lamp, said a sitter is not taught, said the seat might call for girls to sit; did not ask for her household's sleep. Seat: had the crier call for young women. Keeper of the Count: kept the cap of two and the three named nights, the roll, the cord. Steward of the Store: counted aloud in month 4. Wall: held her door from the first night of winter; ordered the watch to note the hour of each dimming. Gate Bench: fined two fathers for their lads over the yard wall.

Spring. Warden of the Door: told the guest at once that the four who went west had been home since the first week of last autumn. Headmen of four near hamlets: asked her to come. Warden of the Wall: gave the half score and chose them.

Summer. New Warden of the Door: told the seat she is nearer; has not said she takes the guest for the awaited. Keeper of the Count: read the cord at the turn of the year.

Where each office stands at the year's end: old Warden of the Door, taken her word; the new Warden of the Door, carries it, has not said she takes her for the awaited; the seat, taken it; Keeper of the Count, taken it; Steward of the Store, taken it grudgingly; Warden of the Wall, has not; Gate Bench, split, agreed on the custom of the table.

## What was done in the god's name
By the Warden of the Door: carried the guest's answers to the yard of the watch, the Keeper of the Count and the seat; carried her ask on the harbor's offer to the court; taught the party's leader the words and looked through the baggage at the quay for lamps and embers; named the runners; took her to the Night Sitter, to the four houses and to the hamlets; named the three hardest nights to the Keeper of the Count the day before.
By the seat: spoke the shrine word from the wall, accepted the harbor's offer, sent the party, had the crier call for young women to sit with the Night Sitter, refused the wall's two men.
By the Keeper of the Count: kept the cord of the dim nights and the cord of her afraid days, held the cap of two, drew the lots (three households on three named nights), kept the roll (about 520), entered about seventy shrines.
By the Steward of the Store: judged the stone with the old cutter, chose the leader and the goods, counted the winter aloud in month 4.
By the old cutter: judged what stone could be spared; chose the journeyman cutter.
By the Warden of the Wall (as the wall's own work, not as her helper): sat her door from the first night of winter, had every man told before his first night, had the watch note the hour of each dim, gave the half score as escort.
By the court's runners: carried to the hamlets that no household is to set a night's lamp in the guest's name beyond the lot, and the shrine word.
Nothing was done in the god's name by the Gate Bench.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper
1. The harbor party. Eight of the court in two of the court's planked river boats: the leader (a man of about forty-one, the Steward's chief keeper of the trade cords, who led last year's six and gave the words to the factor on the third night), two cutters (the old cutter's best hand at dressing, about thirty-four, and a younger man to carry and haul), three runners named by the Warden of the Door (two of last year's six and the young woman of last year), two boatmen of the fishing households (one the man who knows the low-water bars). Goods: twenty bolts of linen, sixty pots in straw and reed baskets, twelve jars of flax oil, and about a boat's load of the scar's grey stone, thirty cut blocks and two dressed pieces as samples. No bronze. No lamp, wick or ember; the first fire struck from flint. Each carries a belt knife. They set out from the quay on the twenty-fifth day of month 2.
   The way. By the court's boats down the river to the mouth (112.4 miles, 4 days down, 10 up). At the mouth the leader means to ask the crew of the coasting ship that fed the river party and last year's party, if a ship is there, to carry the stone, the pots and the party to the harbor town west along the coast for linen, and go on by the ship. If no ship is there or none will take stone, he means to leave the boats, the stone and the pots at the mouth with the two boatmen and the younger cutter, and go round the shore on foot with the linen, the oil, the two dressed samples and four carrying (97.9 miles, 7 days on foot, longer laden), to speak for the stone and learn what the house wants; the house may send a ship for the rest. He will not put the river boats to sea. Coming home the same in reverse, the boats up the river with the salt.
   The words, exactly as given: "I am Vael, the guest at the bend who keeps a lamp. Your answer reached me by the six who came home at high water, and I thank you and your table for the bread and the three nights. I have given your offer to my court, who set the price and hold the stone, for it is theirs and not mine to accept. If they accept, the people who bring this are theirs and carry their word and not mine. They keep a guest's custom: bread, three nights, and no questions of your business until the third. If the terms you set are fair to both tables, they will bring them home." The leader speaks it from memory on the third night to the factor of the house that sets the price of salt for the bend, the one who heard last year's. Orders: if no one will hear it, buy salt as any trader would and come home; if hostile, do not fight, do not argue, keep the goods already promised to the court, come home with what can be had or winter where it is safe; if asked questions, answer only what was seen and told: the guest woke on the stony ground above the town in the dark before an autumn morning, does not know why, the seat takes her for the awaited, she keeps a lamp she lights in a house that asks; do not say what the lamp does or that it can be taken; do not give or sell a flame, lamp or token of hers; if pressed, say he does not know; if anyone asks whether another like her stands on that coast, say he has seen none and has heard of none, and listen, and bring home anything he hears of one. The Steward's own orders: bind the court to nothing, bring home the terms and the order for stone, buy salt at the harbor's price with the linen and oil, and the stone is earnest and sample until the court has heard the terms.
   Success: received, bread and three nights, the words given and heard, the order and the terms for stone learned, salt bought, the party home with salt and the harbor's answer. Failure: refused, robbed (stone is a thing a man will rob a boat for, the Warden of the Wall said), lost on the coast, sick, drowned, a boat grounded on a low-water bar under the load, or the harbor answers badly or withdraws its offer. Partial: salt bought and the answer carried home late, or the stone left at the mouth and not fetched.
   Circumstances from my record that bear on it: last year's six reached the harbor on foot round the shore, were given bread and three nights, the leader spoke the words on the third night to the factor of the house that sets the price, they bought salt from another house's pans, the gales shut the coast road, they wintered and came home at high water in month 8, all six; the answer was a fair standing price by the year for stone of the scar cut to its order; the harbor towns know of the guest from the carriers and asked what she is; the carriers have an interest in the price and the party undercuts them (my inference); the party has no spear and no flame; the boats carry stone this year and sit lower on the low-water bars than last year's did, in the low water of autumn; the leader is not at ease with a boat or the sea; he asked to be replaced and was not; the harbor's order for stone is not known to the town, and the court has promised no more than one part in five of a year's cutting; upstream with a load takes 10 days by the table and may be worse in rain or low water; the cold begins in month 4 and winter closes coastal sailing and the coast road.
   The year both ways: Salt A, home before the hard cold, in the last days of month 3 or the first of month 4, with salt, the harbor's answer and the order for stone; the late pigs fully cured, the Steward's winter count easier, the cutters cut the fifth to the harbor's order in the dry months. Salt B, not back until spring, no word all winter, the late pigs cured lightly and the meat gone early, the party up the river at high water in month 7 or 8, the cutters cut the fifth without an order and reckon it later. If the party is lost or never returns, the court has no word of why, the town grieves eight and sends none before the next autumn, and the fifth of the stone is set aside.
2. The Night Sitter's second sitters. The guest offered a second sitter be taught; the Night Sitter said a sitter is not taught, she sits and finds she can or does not, most go home the first night. The seat's crier called on month 4, day 24 for young women to sit beside her. Nine came and sat at least one night, five came back a second, three sat the winter (two of the lower town, one of a hamlet), beside the pupil (about twenty-one). Success: one finds she can, steadies the breathing of a child in fever, and the Night Sitter sits fewer nights. Failure: none finds it. Circumstances from my record: the Night Sitter is about fifty-six and wears out, cannot sleep three nights after and her hands shake; the pupil came by sitting beside her and many cannot; perhaps one such woman in a few hamlets; the guest's gift does not reach what the work takes from her. Sitter A: one of the three, the hamlet girl, found she could by the thaw, and the Night Sitter's hands were steadier by spring. Sitter B: none found it, and the Night Sitter sat as she did last year.
3. No fight, search, persuasion of anyone outside the people, or craft beyond what the people know was attempted by anyone else this year.

## What turns on a power
1. The three-household nights: month 5, night 18; month 6, night 4; month 6, night 26. The Warden of the Door named them to the Keeper of the Count the day before, the seat's crier told the town, the court asked no plan of her the next day, and the wall set four men at her door, who had been told in the yard what they sit against. What the four saw at those dawns, and whether anything the guest told them was seen, is the Keeper's. I write nothing of it.
2. The guest's body in the yard when she walks the last sleep of the dying in kept houses (about fifteen in kept houses this winter, more than last year). The wall's men kept watch on those nights as told. What they saw of her body is the Keeper's. The households say again the dying were quiet and some spoke of a hind or a lamp. Nothing was cured.
3. The dim nights: month 1 night 12 (no lot, she awake in the yard, no dying in the town), month 5 night 10 (lot night, asleep, an old woman died in a kept house), month 5 night 26 (no lot, awake, no dying), month 7 night 6 (lot night, asleep, a man died in a house not kept), month 7 night 20 (no lot, awake, no dying), month 8 night 13 (no lot, awake in a western hamlet a morning's walk from the wall, hamlet and town both saw it, no dying in the town). The wall's watch and the Keeper agree the hour: about an hour each time. Whether any is hers is not known to anyone, as the Keeper's line on Year 2 says. Fourteen in all; a dying in the town on five. Her list of afraid days, as she gave it, sits beside the cord; the Keeper reads the dawns after lot nights as afraid, and the town divided on the rest. What her list says of the dawns after the three nights with no lot is hers and the Keeper's, and I do not have it.
4. The flames near her dipping at dawn: the wall's men say it is at the same hour every dawn after the three-household nights.
5. The Night Sitter's work yard in month 4: the lamp near the guest did not dip, by the Night Sitter's word. The guest's hands on the kettle and the lamp, and nothing more, were seen.
6. The shrine word: whether a flame lit from a kept flame costs her anything or counts toward the seven is the Keeper's; the cap of two a night was kept.
7. The lowest cuts at the scar: the cutters ringed the three with stones again, unasked.

## What touches anyone else
- The harbor party (above) is the only party sent out. It carries the guest's words, the court's word and stone, linen, pots and oil to the harbor town west along the coast where the salt is boiled. Nothing else was sent out. No spears went out. The seat sent no one west.
- The salt carriers came twice (late autumn and spring) at last year's price, ate the three nights, watched, and said little. In Salt B the Steward asked them hard for word of the party and they gave none.
- Iron came down from the mining town as in other years, greyer and softer from late winter on. The carriers were asked and said they carry what they are given. The smiths say the mining town sends it down before it is worked enough. I give no cause; I do not know one.
- No stranger came to the gate but the carriers and the ordinary traders.
- Nothing was given or sold that carries a flame, a lamp or a token of the guest. Nothing of the guest was carried out of the town except the words the leader has by heart. The shrine word means a household may light a neighbor's niche in the town at the neighbor's asking; no flame went out of the town by it.

## Asked of the god
In the mortals' words as the people repeat them, and whether answered in front of them. Where her decisions fix an answer I say so; elsewhere I write none.
- Warden of the Wall, last year through his captain, answered in the yard: his four rules are hers to keep. Answered as her decisions fix.
- Captain of the wall, in the yard: "How is a man to know the standing body from the sleeping one?" Not answered that I can write.
- One of the four, in the yard: whether, if a fire came into the yard, they were to move her. Not answered that I can write.
- Keeper of the Count, last winter: whether a household that lit its own niche at night was a third household kept. Answered as her decisions fix: a shrine, not a household kept; the cap of two stays.
- Night Sitter, in her work yard: a sitter is not taught, a girl sits and finds she can or does not. Not a question. She took the hands for the water and the lamp. Answered by the guest sitting and keeping the kettle.
- Father at the second of the four houses: "Who told you they had not come home?" Not answered that I can write.
- Headmen: "Was it you?" (the star, month 8 night 13). Answered as her decisions fix: she does not know, does not know that she makes it dim, will take neither the credit nor the blame.
- Households: "May we have the flame?" Answered by her lighting it where asked.
- Households: "May we light our neighbor's niche from our flame?" Answered by the shrine word from the wall.
- A man of a young household of barrow singers, to the Warden of the Door: whether the guest might be asked to hear what they have of the third song. Not put to her; the household said it would think.
- No one asked her about the harbor beyond the leader's orders, or about the iron.

## Names said aloud
"Vael," by the leader and by the Warden of the Door, to each other, each evening of the three evenings in the Warden of the Door's house in the middle of month 2, as part of the words the leader has by heart. If the leader reaches the third night, he says "Vael" aloud to the factor of the house that sets the price of salt; that is the Keeper's if he gets there. No name was said aloud for the people, the home, or any office holder. The people still call her "the guest" and "the guest with the lamp"; some in the lowest streets call her "the keeper". These are not names.
