# Year 3: the unnamed people of god-dreams

The year opens in autumn. Months are counted from the opening: month 1 is the first month of autumn, months 4 to 6 are winter, 7 to 9 spring, 10 to 12 summer. Offices are named by office; no mortal has said an office holder's name aloud this year. Where a thing turns on what the god's power did, I write only what was seen and said, and the report sends it to the Keeper. Two things this year are attempts the Keeper settles: the court's party to the harbor, and the young women who sat beside the Night Sitter. Each is written both ways, briefly. The party is Salt A (home before the hard cold, with salt) and Salt B (not home until spring). The sitters are Sitter A (one found she could) and Sitter B (none did). Where nothing is marked, the year is the same either way.

What the town knew at the opening, as the Keeper's line on Year 2 gave it: the salt party did not come home before the cold. It wintered at the harbor and came up the river at high water, all six home in month 8 of last year, with salt bought at the harbor's price from another house's pans and this answer, that the house which sets the price will set a fair standing price by the year for stone of the scar cut to its order. The Night Sitter did not sleep on the kept night, though the rest of her household did. A flame a household lights from its own kept flame is a shrine and not a third household kept, and the cap of two a night held. On the three mornings after three households the wall's men saw the guest shaken past the other mornings, quick to act and slow to hold a thought, and she would not enter a shut room. The dimmings of the star are not known to be anyone's doing.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

**The grain.** The grain came in as in most years and the threshing went on in the barns. There was no flood loss to make good. The town began a third salting season short. The salt carriers came in the last weeks of the season with a small load at last year's price; the Steward of the Store bought a part and no more, and the late pigs were killed after the first frost.

**The yard of the watch, month 1, day 5.** In the morning the new Warden of the Door took the guest to the yard of the watch behind the south-east gate, with two runners. The Warden of the Wall received her standing, with his captain and four men of the watch whom he had named for her door on the first nights of winter, by lot, none from a kept house. She said what her decisions fix, to him, the captain and the four together. That the four rules he gave her a year ago are hers to keep and she keeps them: no flame of hers within the watch's yards, none lit for a man on duty, nothing asked of the wall but through him, and the days she walks beyond the gate told to him beforehand. Then she told the four what they sit against. That on a night when she keeps three households she acts in the morning on her first impulse, cannot hold a plan across an hour and cannot enter a room that is shut or crowded, and the lamps near her dip when she is afraid, so they are not to ask a plan of her on those mornings and not to take it ill if she refuses a shut room. And that when she walks in a sleeper's dream her body stands where it was, pale, slow and nearly without voice, easy to overlook and easy to move; she asked that they let it stand, not wake her by force, and guard it.

What the wall made of it. The Warden of the Wall heard her to the end and said that what she told the four about the mornings the wall saw three times last winter and had not been told, and that this was the answer he asked for through his captain at the start of last year, given at the start of this. He said the wall would keep her door as it had, and that every man who sat it would be told by the captain before his first night, in the guest's own words. The captain asked how a man was to know the standing body from the sleeping one. One of the four asked whether, if a fire came into the yard, they were to move her. These were put to her. I have no answer of hers I can write. The Warden of the Wall asked nothing more. He has not taken her for the awaited and has not refused her. The captain said afterward to the Warden of the Door that the Warden of the Wall had asked him on the way back whether he thought that was the whole of it, and that he had said no, and the Warden of the Wall had said nor did he.

**The same dusk.** The Warden of the Door carried two things to the Keeper of the Count and the seat, and the seat spoke the first from the wall to the whole people. The first was her answer to the Keeper's question of last winter: a flame a household lights from its own kept flame is a shrine kept for her and is not a household kept that night; the cap of two a night stays. The second she gave to the Keeper of the Count alone: a plain list of the days of her second year on which she was afraid, as she felt it, that his cords of the dim nights might be set beside it. She said she does not know whether the two agree and will say so whichever way they fall. The Keeper of the Count said, dry as ever, that he had waited a year for one line, and wrote the list onto a cord of his own, a knot to a day.

What the town made of the shrine word, the same night and the days after. In the kept houses it was glad: a man with a dying father, a woman with a neighbor's sick child, could light the neighbor's niche from their own, at the neighbor's asking, without going to the lot or the Count. In the lowest streets it was said that a keeper that lets her flame be passed from hand to hand is a keeper that wants it everywhere. The elder of the Gate Bench who is against the guest said that a flame that spreads of itself spreads where no one asked. The Keeper of the Count entered each shrine on the roll as he was told of it, and kept the roll and the lot as before.

**The star, month 1, night 12.** The star dimmed, the blue deepening for about an hour, and stood dim in the middle of the night, and was as before. No lot had been drawn that night. She was awake in the yard under it, the wall's two men at her door said, and looked up and said nothing. No death fell in the town that night. The Keeper of the Count knotted the cord at dawn with the hour from the watch's turn. The town said what it said last year.

**The open door, month 2, day 1.** The Keeper of the Count opened the Count's door as he does on the first of each month and laid two cords on the table, the cord of the dim nights and the cord of her afraid days. He said that the dawns after the nights of the lot are on her cord as afraid and on the wall's men's word, as they must be, and that on the rest he would say nothing the town could not read for itself, because the two cords do not say why. The town read them. Those who hold that her fear dims the star said the cords agree. Those who do not said they do not. Each street kept its own reading.

**The harbor's offer, month 2.** In the first days of the month the guest asked through the Warden of the Door, with the grain safe, that the court judge the harbor house's offer of a fair standing price for salt by the year, for stone of the scar cut to its order, and that the Steward of the Store and the old cutter judge what stone could be spared and what the wall and the footings need first, and the throne decide. She said she would not go and would send no spears, that no house's quarrel was to be taken on, and that the party if one went should be free to winter as its leader judged. She gave a message to go with it. The seat heard the offices one at a time.

The Steward of the Store and the old cutter walked the stacks at the scar and the wall's footings over four days. The old cutter, a man of about sixty-one, said what he said in the court: that the wall's winter footing work takes four parts in five of the dry months' cutting and the fifth is what the scar can spare in a year, and that no more than that should be promised to any house, for the wall is the town's and the sea is not. There was, besides, stone already cut and stacked beyond the footing's need, about a boat's load, and that could go as the first. He said also that the cutters cut to what their hands know and not to any cut they have not seen, and asked that a cutter go with the party to read the order. The elder of the Gate Bench who is against the guest said the stone was the town's footing, and the guest was asking it for strangers. The seat answered that the court was judging and the town was the one that would sell, and that she would hold the bargain as the town's and not the guest's. On the twelfth day she accepted the offer. She said the harbor's house had fed the town's six and told them its answer, and a house that does that is owed an answer.

The Steward of the Store chose the leader. It was the same man as last year, the Steward's chief keeper of the trade cords, about forty-one winters now. He asked the Steward to send another, saying he was no better at the sea than he had been. The Steward said the factor had seen his face and the words had been given through his mouth, and the seat said the same. He went. The party is eight in two of the court's planked river boats: the leader; two cutters, the journeyman the old cutter chose, a man of about thirty-four who is his best hand at dressing, and a younger man to carry and haul; three runners named by the Warden of the Door, two of them of last year's six and one the young woman of last year, who has now been to the harbor and back; and two boatmen of the fishing households, one the man who knows the low-water bars. The goods: twenty bolts of linen, sixty pots in straw and reed baskets (fewer than last year, for the weight of the stone), twelve jars of flax oil, and the stone, about a boat's load, thirty cut blocks of the scar's grey stone and the old cutter's two dressed pieces as samples. No bronze. No lamp, no wick and no ember from any niche. The Warden of the Door had the baggage looked through at the quay, and the first fire is to be struck from flint on the river bank.

The Warden of the Wall asked again for two of his men as rowers, belt knives only. He said that stone is a thing a man will rob a boat for. The seat refused him as she did last year, for the guest had asked that no spear go and a man of the wall is another sort of party in a stranger's eyes. He said then that he could not make eight into ten and would not try. Each of the eight carries a belt knife, as any traveler does, and nothing else of iron but the tools of the cutters.

The words they carry are the guest's, and the leader had them by heart after three evenings in the Warden of the Door's house in the middle of the month: the Warden of the Door gave them and added nothing, and the leader said them back each evening. The name in them was said aloud in that house, by the leader and by the Warden of the Door, each evening, as part of the words. The orders are the guest's: bread and three nights and no business asked until the third; the leader speaks the words on the third night to the factor of the house that sets the price of salt for the bend, the one who heard them last year; if no one will hear them, buy salt as any trader would and come home; if the harbor is hostile, do not fight, do not argue, keep the goods already promised to the court and come home with what can be had or winter where it is safe; if asked questions, answer only what was seen and told, that the guest woke on the stony ground above the town in the dark before an autumn morning, does not know why, the seat takes her for the one awaited, and she keeps a lamp that she lights in a house that asks; he does not say what the lamp does or that it can be taken, and does not give or sell a flame or a lamp or a token of hers to anyone; if pressed, he says he does not know; and if anyone asks whether another like her stands on that coast he says he has seen none and has heard of none, and he listens, and brings home anything he hears of one. The Steward's own orders to him: bind the court to nothing, bring home the terms and the order, buy salt at the harbor's price with the linen and oil, and the stone is for earnest and sample until the court has heard the terms.

The way. The leader means to take the two boats down the river to the mouth. At the mouth he means to ask the crew of the coasting ship that fed the river party, and last year's party, if a ship is there, to carry the stone, the pots and the party to the harbor for linen, and to go on by the ship. If no ship is there, or none will take stone, he means to leave the boats, the stone and the pots at the mouth with the two boatmen and the younger cutter, and go on foot round the shore with the linen, the oil, the two dressed samples and four carrying, to speak for the stone and learn what the house wants; if the house wants it, it may send a ship for the rest. He has said he will not put the river boats to sea. Coming home he means the same in reverse, and the boats up the river with the salt. They set out from the quay on the twenty-fifth day of month 2.

**A quarrel over a niche, month 3.** Two neighbors of the lower town, one of a kept house and one not, quarreled at the Gate Bench over a niche. The kept neighbor, in the night, with a sick child in the other house, had lit the other's niche from his own at the mother's word; the father, coming home, said he had not asked and put it out. The Bench heard both. The two for the guest and the one against agreed this once on all of it: a flame is lit at a household's asking, and in no other way, and the household's own mouth is the asking; a kept neighbor who lights it on a stranger's word has done wrong though he meant well. The Bench fined no one and told the kept neighbor to ask the father's pardon, which he did. The elder who is against the guest said he ruled with the guest's word and not for it, as he had said before.

**Late autumn.** The carriers had no word of the party. Salt A: the party came up the river in the last days of month 3, or in the first of month 4, before the hard cold, with salt in the boats and the harbor's answer, which is the Keeper's to price, and the court heard it from the leader in the guest house yard with the Steward and the old cutter and the seat; the late pigs, killed after the first frost and not yet cured, were cured as they ought to be. Salt B: no word came in autumn or winter, the late pigs were cured lightly again, and the Steward said before the council that a party may be wintering at the harbor, or may not.

## The salt party, both ways

**Salt A. Home before the hard cold.** The party is home with salt and the harbor's answer. The terms and the order for stone are what the Keeper gives; the court hears them and decides whether to bind itself; the cutters know before the cold what the harbor wants, and will cut to it in the dry months. The Steward's winter count is easier. The carriers, hearing it in spring, know the town has seen the harbor's price a second time.

**Salt B. Not back until spring.** No word all winter. The meat goes early. The party comes up the river at the high water, in month 7 or 8 as the Keeper prices the ice and the flood, with the harbor's answer and what salt and word it could bring. The cutters cut in the dry months without an order, and cut the harbor's order late.

The rest of the year is written for either, with the difference marked where there is one.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

The winter was cold in the ordinary way. From the first night of winter the wall's men sat her door, two a night by lot, not from kept houses, four on the three named nights. Every man had been told by the captain before his first night, in her words, what he sat against.

**The Steward counts aloud.** In month 4 the Steward of the Store counted before the council the grain, the fuel and the salt. Grain would reach the new harvest with a thin margin. Fuel was enough. Oil was enough. Salt: Salt A, in hand, and the meat safe; Salt B, none, and the Steward said so.

**Keeping Sleep and the three nights.** Two households a night were kept by the Keeper of the Count's lot, the deathbeds, newborns, fevers and fresh griefs first. On three nights of deep winter three households were kept, each named by the Warden of the Door to the Keeper of the Count the day before and told to the town by the seat's crier, and the court asked no plan of her the next day and sent for her about nothing: month 5, night 18, a hard frost with fevers; month 6, night 4; month 6, night 26. The wall set four men at her door. What the four saw at those dawns, and whether anything the guest told them in the yard was seen, is a thing of her power and the report sends it to the Keeper. The mornings after were quiet on the streets near the yard.

**The dying in kept houses.** About fifteen of the winter's deaths fell in kept houses, more than last year as the roll is larger. The households say again that the dying one was quiet and that some spoke in the last hour of a hind or a lamp. The guest had said she walks the last sleep of the dying in the houses that ask, and gives no orders there; the wall's men kept watch on those nights as they were told, and what they saw of her body in the yard is a thing of her power and goes to the Keeper. Nothing was cured. The town has begun to say that she walks in the sleep of the dying and leaves her body standing in the yard like a ghost's.

**Two lads and the yard.** In month 5 two lads of the lower town, having heard from a man of the watch's household that she stands pale in the yard on certain nights and can be moved, went over the yard wall to see. The wall's two men took them before they were within a rope's length of her. The Gate Bench fined each lad's father a day's labor on the wall and said she was a guest and her body a guest's. The Warden of the Wall said the town should know now what the wall had been keeping.

**The Night Sitter.** In month 4, on the twentieth day, the new Warden of the Door took the guest to the Night Sitter's work yard on a night the Night Sitter sat, with a child in fever and a woman in a childbed fever, and the pupil beside her. The guest sat beside the Night Sitter and did not work. She said what her decisions fix: that the Keeper has ruled her gift does not reach what the Night Sitter's work takes from her, and that she offered her hands for the lamp and the water and a word to the seat that a second sitter be taught beside her pupil, so the nights are shared and the Night Sitter may sleep after, and if refused she would ask no more.

The Night Sitter said she would take the hands for the water. She had the guest keep the kettle at the heat she wanted and hold the Night Sitter's own clay lamp where she could see the child's face, and said afterward that the guest was good with a kettle, which she had not expected of a god, if that was what she was. She said the lamp near the guest did not dip that night, and that she, the Night Sitter, had not looked for it to. She said, of the second sitter, that a sitter is not taught. A girl sits beside her through a night and either finds she can or she does not, and most go home the first night. The pupil came by sitting. If the seat wanted more than one of her kind it could say so from the wall and let girls come and sit, and she would turn none away and call none. She did not say she would take her household's sleep kept on the nights after. The child and the woman lived. The Night Sitter did not sleep the three nights after, and her hands shook, as they do.

The seat's crier said on month 4, day 24, from the wall: that any young woman of the lower town or the hamlets who would might come to the Night Sitter's work yard at dusk on a night she sits and sit beside her, that none was bound, and none was to be shamed for going home. Nine came and sat at least one night. Five came back a second. Three sat through the winter, two of the lower town and one of a hamlet. The pupil, now about twenty-one, sat beside the three and was quieter than before and not less afraid. This is an attempt for the Keeper, in the report.

Sitter A: by the thaw one of the three, the girl from the hamlet, found she could. She steadied the breathing of a child in fever on her own for a night, with the Night Sitter asleep in the next room, and the Night Sitter sat fewer nights after and her hands were steadier by spring. Sitter B: none of the three found it. The Night Sitter sat as she did last year and her hands were as they were, and the girls went home in the thaw without shame, as the seat had said.

**The star in deep winter.** In month 5, on the tenth night, the star dimmed, a night of the lot, with her asleep in the yard and the wall's two men at her door. An old woman of the lower town died in a kept house that night, quietly. The tag went on the cord. In the same month, on the twenty-sixth night, it dimmed again. No lot had been drawn. She was awake in the yard and said nothing. No death fell in the town that night. The Warden of the Wall had by now ordered the watch to note the hour of each dimming from the turns, and the watch's hours and the Keeper's agreed: about an hour, in the middle of the night, each time.

**The barrow songs.** At the long winter tables the oldest of the barrow singers said again that the young cannot keep the third song. The two young households who sat with him last winter had half of it, and sat again, and by the thaw they had a little more. A man of one of them asked the Warden of the Door whether the guest might be asked to hear what they had, and the Warden of the Door said she would carry it if the household wished. The household said it would think. It has not asked. The songs stand at three of five.

**The Gate Bench.** The elder who is against the guest said at the winter's first dusk, as he had said before, that a woman who keeps two lamps may keep two minds, and added that a god who tells the wall what it sits against and not the town what she is has chosen her hearers. The two for the guest said she had told the wall because the wall asked. The Bench kept its ruling: no house shamed for asking or for not asking.

**The iron.** From late winter on the iron the mining town sent down was greyer and softer than it has been. The town's smiths said so in month 6, and that a share or a pick drawn from it blunts or bends in a month where it used to take a season. The carriers who bring it were asked and said they carry what they are given and know nothing of the smelting. The Steward put by the older iron for the spearheads and the cutters' picks.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

The river was high in spring as in most years. It took no seed this year. The flood watch was kept by the headmen and the wall, and the Steward lent seed to the lowest hamlet as before and did not press the old debt.

**The star, a pair.** In month 7, on the sixth night, the star dimmed, a night of the lot, with her asleep in the yard. A man in the lower town died that night in a house not kept. The tag went on the cord. On the twentieth night of the same month it dimmed again, and she was awake in the yard, the wall's two men said, with no lot drawn, and no death fell in the town. The town said the star had dimmed twice in a fortnight and that it was oftener than the year before.

**The families, month 7, day 4.** The guest asked through the Warden of the Door to go to the families of the four who went west and have not come home. The Warden of the Door told her at once that they were home: all four came home along the west road in the first week of the first month of last year, thin, having found no one and no smoke, and she had been told it the same evening, by the Warden of the Door himself, before the new one held the office. The guest went to the four houses all the same, with the Warden of the Door and two runners, and the four were at their own tables. At the first house a mother said from the door that her son was at the table. At the second the father asked who had told the guest they had not come home. At the third a woman said her daughter had been fed at the court's table for three days when she came home and that the court had the word of it from the first evening. At the fourth the four themselves came to the door, one by one, and stood before her with their hands at their sides. What the guest said at the doors is hers and the Keeper's, and I do not write it. The families did not ask for a lamp at the Dusk Table or a stone or the telling of the names at the wall, since the four were not dead. The seat sent no one west. The town said it was an odd thing for a keeper of the dark to ask after four who had been home for two seasons, and the Warden of the Door said only that she carries what she is given and that it is not always given right.

**The hamlets.** The headmen had heard from the runners what the guest meant, and word came to the Warden of the Door that all twelve hamlets within a morning's walk of the wall had been walked by the guest in the two years, and that the three that remain lie farther. Four headmen of the near hamlets sent word asking her to come again, for the flood line, a failing weir, a Night Sitter's hamlet and a field's edge at which a woman had said she would come out and had not. From the second month of spring into the dry months the guest went out to those four with a half score of the wall watch whom the Warden of the Wall gave when asked and chose himself, the days told to him beforehand. She lit a flame only where it was asked. She did not go into any pen, net, narrow lane or barred door. At one hamlet the bridge was too narrow and the headman came out to her. At the field's edge the woman who had said she would come came out and stood at the fence and said nothing and went in. The headmen asked her about the star, the iron, the salt and the dim nights, and she answered them as she has answered before.

In month 8, on the thirteenth night, the star dimmed while she was in one of the western hamlets, a morning's walk from the wall. She was awake, with the headman at the edge of the field, and the whole hamlet saw it. The town saw it from the wall. No death fell in the town that night. The hamlet's dying that night are not known; the headman said none. At dawn the headman asked whether it was she, and she said what she has said: that she does not know, does not know that she makes it dim, and will take neither the credit nor the blame.

**The carriers.** The salt carriers came in month 8 with iron, at last year's price. The Steward asked them what was the matter with the iron and they said they carry what they are given. In Salt A the carriers found the town less in need and did not say so. In Salt B the Steward asked them hard for any word of the party and they gave none.

**Salt B, the party home.** In month 7 or 8, at the high water, the party came up the river with the harbor's answer and what salt it could bring. The town met it at the quay. The terms are the Keeper's.

**The Warden of the Wall.** In the spring the first plough and the first spears from the older iron and the soft were tried against the town's needs, and the smiths drew the soft iron out more often. The Warden of the Wall tested the wall's spearheads, a score at a time, against a post, and said that four in ten from the soft iron bent where one in ten of the older would. He had them set aside for the beacon stores and the old heads issued to the watch. He told the seat that this was the iron, not the guest, and that he said it so that no one would say otherwise of him.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

The river was low and the dry months dry enough for the stone cutting. Four parts of the five went into the wall's footing and the town's own work. The fifth was cut for the harbor. Salt A: the cutters had the harbor's order and cut to it, and stacked it at the scar to wait for a ship. Salt B: the order came late and the cutters cut the fifth as they judged, and set it aside to be reckoned against the order when the court had settled it. The old cutter said the picks were wearing twice as fast as last year and that the harbor's stone would cost the town more in iron than it knew. The cutters left the lowest cuts as they were, with the ring of loose stones round the three. No one told them to.

**The new Warden of the Door.** In month 10 she told the seat, as she told no one else, that she carries what she is given and was nearer than she was. She did not say she takes the guest for the awaited. She said the guest had gone to four houses and found the four at the table, and had stood at the fourth door and not gone in, and that a keeper who does that is not a keeper who is sure. She did not say what she took from it.

**The barrow songs.** In month 12 the oldest of the barrow singers, who is failing, sang the third song through to the two young households at his own hearth, and they have it by two parts in three. He said it was as far as he could give it.

**The Keeper's reading.** At the turn of the year the Keeper of the Count laid the cord on the Count's table. This year six dim nights, last year five, the year before three, fourteen in all: three remembered, eleven counted. A dying fell in the town on five of the fourteen, two of them in kept houses this year. By the roll of the town's dead, he said, a dying falls in the town on about one night in four, so five of fourteen is a little above what the roll alone would give, and he did not know what to make of that. He said also that the roll of kept households has gone from about three hundred and thirty to about four hundred and fifteen to about five hundred and twenty, and the dim nights from three to five to six, and that two things that rise together are two things that rise together, and the cord does not say why. The town heard him out. The young said the star pays for the flames. The old said again that more nights are not different nights.

**The wall at year's end.** The Warden of the Wall has not taken the guest for the awaited and has not refused her. His men have sat her door for a second winter, told what they sit against, and none has asked off. The flames near her dip at the same hour every dawn after the three nights, he told the seat, and the wall has stopped being surprised, which is not the same as no longer minding.

**The end of the year.** The grain was cut and brought in, an ordinary harvest again. The roll of households keeping the flame stands at about 520. The old Warden of the Door is alive and at his lamp, about seventy-two. The seat is well. The seventh place of the guest's keeping is still empty.
