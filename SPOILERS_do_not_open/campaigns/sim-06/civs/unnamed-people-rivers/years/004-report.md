# Report to the Keeper: unnamed people of god-rivers, Year 4, pass one

## How the god was met
Spring, first days after the thaw. A weir hand at dawn work saw her come down the stream path and called the gate house. The Seat Warden left the cup where it stood at the low seat, put her on the guest bench and fed her. She did not offer the cup. The Warden took her as a guest.
Spring, the first evening. The children, the ferry child (twelve) at the front, and the small boy, a mill hand's youngest, who said last spring that a spring cannot walk. She told him before the others that he was right: a spring cannot walk, she is not a spring, she woke at one and walks, and does not know why the one came with the other. She said nothing of any working. The boy said "I said." The ferry child asked "Then what are you?" and was told what had been said. The old women said afterward that a spring that walks is the spring's going down, as the brook is. The Seat Warden kept the cord of it at her asking, and said she would, as she kept the rest. Nobody took it as a working.
Spring, month one, the day after the stones showed. The Ferry Elder stood with her and the Reckoner at the gate stone at her asking, read the flood marks and the low-water cord, and said the deep water had moved again, a little, the same way, the seventh time. He did not take the cords as more than the town's own. The Reckoner said the cord said the same.
Spring, sixth day after the flood went down. The council called back the Speaker of Year 2, the head of the middle kin house, a woman of about 54. That is the council's, not the god's.
Spring, about the tenth day. The sitting. The Speaker said the council had had what she said at the autumn sitting and in the Warden's cord; the guest said that was so and said it again. The head of the oldest kin house said that the shoal behind each weir was a thing the water said and the town had not measured; the Reckoner said the same; the weir hands said a bed always shoals behind a weir. The head herd keeper, a hill pasture woman of about fifty one, heard the answer to her question of last spring and said, without heat, that she had watched the guest sleep at the water below the gate in front of half the town, so a person who sleeps is not a person who does not; the guest said it was so; the herd keeper said she would put it in the cord as a woman who does not tire and who sleeps when the river asks, and that the pasture's word stood (look an hour, then send to the water gate). She took the guest's offer as an offer. The Reckoner asked whose count the weir measure was; the council settled it as the town's. The head of the oldest kin house asked "A guess, you say. Which weir?" and was told she did not know and that was why the measure. The head of the weir hands, a man of about 56 with two fingers gone to a weir stake, said it left them whether or not they stood there, that they would measure because the council said so and because it was their pole, and that nobody was to hold it for them. The council gave its go to the measure and said it would answer on the opening of a weir when it had the figures. None took her word as more than a guest's counsel.
Spring, month two. The Ferry Elder gave the ferry child a pole at the landing, as he had said he would when she was twelve with a pole of her own, and told her before the landing that she would go to the ford and no farther. He told the guest after, and did not ask her. That is the Elder's, not the god's.
Summer, month four. The council sat three evenings for her leave. The head of the oldest kin house stood against it and did not stand against it by the third evening. He asked what she would do if the salt she found was some other people's; she said what she asked, where the salt joins the river and whether the ford is needed; he said that was two things. The Ferry Elder counted the days (five down, twelve and a half up, call it thirteen, three weeks and a few days in all) and said five was two days past anyone of the town and he would have said four; he set the turn at dusk of the fifth, sooner at the salt, sooner for the water rising or a storm, never later, and not to be decided on the water. The older ferry keeper of the ford landing said she had said three last year because she knew three, and would go five if the water stayed low and no storm showed. She also told the guest at once that no runner of the council's could be had below the ford (the god's file says to send for the council's runner if anything comes against them). The Elder said the party was to blow the horn and hold the barge and not wait for anyone's runner. The Reckoner named his cord walker on the condition that the knots come to him first. The Speaker gave the council's go. No oath either way.
Summer, the ford, first night. The ford keeper who asked last year what another like herself would be had his answer at the ford fire: she does not know; she has met none and felt none; she would know one at close quarters by a weight in the air and not before; if one came she would sit it by a hearth and ask its news after it had eaten. The ford keepers were quiet, and one said that was the town's own code, said by someone who had not been told it. The ferry child heard it. They took her answer as plain.
Summer, month six. The weir hands measured with their own pole at the low water; the guest stood on the bank and was told it was their pole.
Autumn, month eight. The council heard her in one sitting and talked three evenings. The head of the oldest kin house asked whether the star was hers. The Seat Warden, who had kept the question since the night in the summer when it was left with her, said it back. The head of the oldest kin house, who had asked in the spring "which weir," said the oldest weir was his house's, that his house's grandmothers had set it, and that if it was wrong it was his house that should unset it. The Reckoner said the cord stood at ten nights across the two years of marks, eight in the town and two away. The Seat Warden said back the cord of her sayings.
Winter. The eldest midwife, as asked, sent word of a girl of about four with a fever; the mother asked "Will you hold her?"; the guest held her hands. She did not listen in the ice. The Warden filled the cup each morning and the guest did not drink it.
Whether each took her word as the god's helper: none did. No office holder has taken her for the one awaited or for a god. The Seat Warden holds the nearest to it, saying only that the cup has not been empty of her. The Keeper rules whether any office holder is a helper under the record's rule. I have written none as one.

## What was done in the god's name
Nothing was done in her name. Done at her asking, and kept by the people as their own: the Ferry Elder read the flood marks and the low-water cord with her; the ferry keepers cut the eighth flood mark and four more low-water marks at the gate stone and the ford stone, twelve each now; the council set the weir measure as the town's own, the weir hands measured with their own pole, the Reckoner's cord walker knotted it and the Reckoner keeps the cord beside the marks; the Seat Warden kept the cord of her sayings and said it back; the council gave leave for the going, the Ferry Elder named two ferry keepers and the Reckoner his cord walker, the ford keepers gave back the barge left with them and kept the ferry; the eldest midwife sent word of the child in the fever; the Reckoner kept the cord of the dimmed nights with the marks for where she stood. The council opened a gap in the oldest weir of its own choosing, on the town's own figures and the head of the oldest kin house's word, after she had asked whether it would try opening one weir for a season; I count that as the council's, not hers. The council set aside again one part in twenty of the barley and millet, its own habit by now; not at her asking.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper
1. The journey down the larger river, summer, month four. Who tries: the god, the older ferry keeper of the ford landing (about 42, knotted hands, went last year and the year before, said three last year because she knew three, will go five if the water stays low and no storm shows), the younger ferry keeper who cuts the marks (about 27), and the Reckoner's cord walker (about 24, exact counts). Four. They carry the low-water cord, a bag of the town's cords for the ford keepers, a bundle of linen, six pots, two bronze knives and dried eel as gifts for hearths below, and bread for twenty one days. Set out: about the tenth day of month four from the town by barge down the brook, one day to the ford; two days at the ford; cast off down the larger river at dawn of the third day after they reach the ford. A twelve year old girl (the ferry child) goes as far as the ford and stays there with the ford keepers; she does not go on the larger river. How many days down: five, agreed by the council at the Ferry Elder's counsel and by the older ferry keeper; the turn is made at dusk of the fifth day wherever they lie, or sooner at the salt if it is met, or sooner if the water rises or a storm shows, and never later, and not decided on the water. Back: the Elder counts twelve and a half days up for five down, call it thirteen if the water runs strong; the ford about the last day of month four; the Brook Path two days after; home in the first days of month five. What they ask: where the salt joins the larger river and whether the ford is needed; where the carriers and the reed cutters come to the river. They stop at each hearth and landing the river gives, give and ask as the code gives, and the cord walker knots it and what is given. The god means to stand in the larger river to the knee each dusk and each dawn, and not shift in anyone's sight, and to listen once at the farthest reach. What is the Keeper's: what lies below the reed country where they turned last year, who is met, what the carriers and reed cutters say of the salt, what the river tells her, and whether anything comes against the party. Circumstances from my record that bear on it: no one of the town has been below where the barge turned last year, three days down; the Keeper's line says that was about ninety miles, into the reed country of many channels, with reed cutters at their staithes and a band of the boat people met at dusk; the flat river barge carries ten people or two tons and the people pole it across shallows, and the larger river is deeper and faster than the shallows they pole; the ferry keepers work the ford ferry on the larger river and know it at the ford and three days below; the party is three mortals and the god, who works at 1.6 of a mortal at the sweep; a barge goes down about 30 miles in a day and up about 12; the water is low, in the dry weeks of summer; two of the five days down are beyond anything the town knows; there is no runner and no path of the town's below the ford; the record gives no hostile neighbor on the larger river below the ford; my record's table gives the river's mouth as 164.4 miles by boat downstream and 6 days, which the keepers do not hold as a figure they have run. Success: the party goes down to the fifth dusk, or to the salt if met sooner, turns, comes up in about thirteen, is at the ford about the last day of month four and home in the first days of month five, with news of where the salt joins and whether the ford is needed. Partial: delayed, or short of knowledge, or home with news that costs the town (a quarrel, a debt, a visitor following). Failure: the barge lost or harmed in the current, a keeper or the cord walker lost to the river (one death at most from the river alone, more if someone comes against them), or the party held below the ford. Written both ways in the year file: population differs by three at most.
2. The watch over the listening at the farthest reach, the evening of the turn. Who tries: the older ferry keeper, the younger ferry keeper and the cord walker. What: keep a fire, a mat, the dogs and the cold off through about two hours of sleep after an hour's listening, and blow the horn and hold the barge if anything comes against her. What threatens: strangers, beasts, anything that hunts a sleeping god, a band met below, and the bank itself, which is strangers' ground and no one of the town's. Circumstances: she lies at the water's edge of the larger river at the farthest reach, five days below the ford or less; the party is three mortals and a barge; there is no runner to send for and no ford keepers' horn at hand, only the barge's horn; it is the dry weeks of summer. I wrote it both ways in the year file. If something came, the older ferry keeper sounds the horn and the party sees what it cannot name.
Nothing else is contested: no fight, no persuasion of another people, no craft beyond what my people know. The ferry child on the brook barge is the Elder's own arrangement and settled. The gap in the oldest weir is ordinary work, about thirty worker-days: what the water does at the gap is not mine; I wait for the flood and the Keeper's line.

## What turns on a power
1. The pull in the larger river. Seen: at the ford, on the second dusk and the third dawn, and by the party on the journey each dusk and each dawn, she waded in from the bank up to the knee in the woman's shape, stood facing upstream with her hands loose, and came out. What she felt, if anything, is for the Keeper; her file asks that the Keeper tell her in the next release. My year does not turn on it beyond what the ferry keepers and the cord walker say of it, and the ford keepers' name for her.
2. The listening at the farthest reach, the evening of the turn. Seen, if done: she knelt in the running water about an hour, went still, did not answer when spoken to, lay down and slept about two hours, and woke asking for water. What she heard is for the Keeper. My year turns on it at the autumn sitting: the council's course on the ford, the second ferry, a mark to read by, a salt party, and the weir, in part, depends on what she told it of the water and of the salt. Written both ways in the year file. The Keeper sets her actual words beside this.
3. Warm hands, asked once. A girl of about four in the lower town, with the winter cough gone to a fever, month ten. Seen: her hands went cold and white while she held the child's, and she warmed them at the hearth after and said little. What it did to the child is for the Keeper. Written both ways in the year file: population differs by one.
4. The answer to the ford keeper, "a weight in the air" at close quarters. That is her own saying of what she would know, which the people took as plain and strange. If it is a claim about a power, the Keeper settles it. I wrote no more of it.
5. The unsigned reading: the town reads her holding as the midwives' craft in a larger measure and the answer to the boy as the plain word of a guest who does not know; she claimed no working. Nothing in my record contradicts it.
6. The star dimmed seven nights, in months 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, 11 and 12. It is the Keeper's, not mine. The Reckoner cut a mark beside each knot for where she stood: in the town six times, away once, the third, on the climb, when she was on the water. The people tie it to her sometimes, wrongly, and I know no cause. The Keeper's line says the dimmings are not known to be anyone's doing; my people do not know that and say otherwise.
7. The cold in her hands after warm hands: seen as cold hands, if the Keeper rules there is nothing more.
8. The god's decisions say she does not tire and does not sleep. The people have seen her sleep three times (a day and a night, two hours, two hours). The head herd keeper said so before the council and she said it was so. I wrote nothing beyond that.

## What touches anyone else
Salt carriers: crossed fewer times again, and at a higher price. A party crossed at the ford on the second day of the god's stay and ordinary travelers. She asked them what she asked; I wrote nothing of their answer, being of other people and other ground. Word of a barefoot woman who stands in the river may travel with them; I wrote none of that.
Boat bands: traded at dusk as before. They may have seen the barge go down the brook; I wrote no meeting. Whether the boat people are met below is the Keeper's.
Namers: none came.
Parties sent out by my people: one, four of them, the god, the older ferry keeper of the ford landing, the younger ferry keeper who cuts the marks, and the Reckoner's cord walker, with the ferry child as far as the ford. Set out for the ford by barge down the brook, about the tenth day of month four, one day, two days at the ford, then down the larger river for five days or to the salt, and back as above, then the Brook Path two days home. The stops on the larger river and who is met there are the Keeper's to price and to give. The ford keepers gave back the barge left with them and kept the ferry, and took the child on at the landing. The ford keepers sent their low-water marks up the Brook Path by a runner in winter after the ice held. If the party was not at the ford by the third day of month five, the Ferry Elder sends the second barge down the brook with three poles and a runner ahead by the path to the ford; that is in the year file's second branch.
Anything carried: the low-water cord, a bag of the town's cords for the ford keepers, a bundle of linen, six pots, two bronze knives and dried eel, given at hearths below as the code gives to a stranger, the cord walker knotting what is given. The cord walker's knots of the landings and hearths below the ford are the Reckoner's before the town's.
Searchers: none; no stock strayed.

## Asked of the god
The small boy, last spring and answered this spring in front of the children: "A spring cannot walk." Answered: he is right; a spring cannot walk; she is not a spring; she woke at one and walks and does not know why the one came with the other.
The ferry child, at the fire: "Then what are you?" Answered in part: she is not a spring, she woke at one and walks, and does not know.
The head herd keeper, last spring and answered this spring in front of the council: "Why would you search for our goats, and what would you do that a boat and a dog cannot?" Answered: she does not tire and does not sleep, so she could walk the reed edge through a night and a day with the dogs and count heads when the boats must rest; beyond that nothing a boat and a dog cannot do; she would be one more pair of feet; she would search if sent for. The herd keeper's reply, that she had watched her sleep, was answered by the guest only with "it is so."
The Ferry Elder, at the gate stone: "Has the deep water moved, or has the ford?" Not answered by the god; the Elder answered it himself. The god had asked him.
The Reckoner, at the sitting: "Whose count is the weir measure?" Answered in part: the council settled it as the town's; she asked that he keep the second cord of the measures with the marks.
The head of the oldest kin house, at the sitting: "A guess, you say. Which weir?" Answered in front of him: she did not know; that was why the measure.
The Speaker, at the sitting: "What do you ask of the council about the weirs?" Answered, as her file gives: the measure, and whether the council would try opening one weir for a season; the council may refuse.
The head of the weir hands, at the sitting: "Did the water tell you our weirs are at fault?" Answered in part: the water told her what is and never why; the weirs were a guess.
The head of the oldest kin house, at the council before the going: "What will you do if the salt you find is some other people's?" Answered in front of him: she asked where the salt joins the river and whether the ford is needed. He said that was two things, and she said it was.
The older ferry keeper, at the council: "Who do you send for if something comes against you below the ford, when there is no runner?" Not answered by the god in my files; the Elder answered it: horn, barge, and not wait for anyone's runner.
A ford keeper, at the ford fire, "What is another like yourself?" asked last year and answered this year in front of the ford keepers: she does not know; she has met none and felt none; she would know one at close quarters by a weight in the air and not before; if one came she would sit it by a hearth and ask its news after it had eaten.
Some of the weirs' people, to the Seat Warden, the morning after the third dimmed night, while she was away: "Does the star dim for her?" Met silence since she was away; the Warden said she would put it in the cord, and it was pending. The head of the oldest kin house put it again at the autumn sitting: "Is the star yours?" Answered by the god in front of him: not answered in my files; her words are the DM's.
The mother of the sick girl, in winter: "Will you hold her?" Answered: yes, she held the child's hands.
The ferry child, at the landing, in the second month: "Can I go to the ford now?" Answered by the Ferry Elder, not the god: yes, to the ford and no farther.
Any further question the children or the keepers put on the journey and any answer I did not write; the god's words are the DM's.

## Names said aloud
The Counting Woman: still said by the children and many in the town. Said at the fire in the spring.
Knee Deep: said by the ford keepers and the ferry keepers at the ford and after.
Nessa Ferry: not said this year.
Names of the four reed hamlets: not asked this year, since the party did not stop at them.
No name for the people or their home was said. No office holder was named, the Speaker included. The older ferry keeper, the younger ferry keeper, the cord walker, the head herd keeper, the head of the weir hands, the small boy and the ferry child are not named. If a name is said aloud below the ford, by the party or by anyone met, the Keeper writes who said it, when and to whom; I write none.

## What the people tell their god
These are the town's words, put together by office as each would say it. They are the town's telling of its year at home, not of the going.

The Ferry Elder says: The flood mark on the gate stone is the eighth. The deep water has moved again from the ford stones, on the side away from the weirs, a little, the seventh flood running; I read it with you and said so, and I have no better word than that it is so. The low-water cord stands at twelve marks at the gate stone and twelve at the ford stone. The weir hands measured with their own pole at the low water, and I stood on the bank with my knee. Behind the oldest weir the bed is shoaled nine hands over the footing logs, behind the second seven, behind the third five, behind the newest of the four three, behind the first small fish weir one, behind the second a finger. The older the weir, the deeper the shoal. That is two things that go together, and two things that go together are two things. I gave my girl a pole when she was twelve and sent her to the ford and no farther, and she held to it. I counted five out at the landing post, and I count twice. The larger river is not the town's.

The Reckoner says: The cord stands at seven knots of the dimmed star this year, and three, five, six and seven for the four years; it does not count by ones. Six of the seven in the town, one away, the third, on the climb. Across the two years of marks the cord stood at ten nights at the sitting in the autumn, eight in the town and two away, and stands at thirteen now, eleven in the town and two away; you were away about one night in fifteen of the two years, and the star came while you were away two in thirteen, which I do not think is nothing and do not think is something. I keep the cord of the weir figures beside the marks as you asked, and it is the town's. Your counts and mine still never differ. A cord says what, and not why.

The head of the weir hands says: We measured because the council said so and because it was our pole. The silt behind a weir is the brook's own, which was there before the weirs and settles wherever the water slows. The oldest weir has the most years. If the deep water comes back to the ford stones I will eat my pole. The oldest weir takes about one part in five of the town's eel, three parts in four of it in the run of the autumn dark moon, and the gap costs about one part in six of that weir's year, which is about one part in thirty of the town's eel, and the town has made it good by cord. It is the first time the town has unset a weir, and my hands did it because nobody else knew where the pegs were.

The head of the oldest kin house says: The oldest weir is my house's. If it was wrong it was my house that set it wrong, and my house that should unset it. I asked which weir, and you did not know, and that was a plain answer. I asked whether the star is yours, and I would have the answer from you. I still ask what you want.

The Speaker says: I was called back, and I ask the plain question. The council heard you in one sitting and talked three evenings, and answered what it could answer on its own figures, which was the weir, and said that the rest will wait for the flood.

The Seat Warden says: The cup was filled each morning and has not been poured out. I kept the cord of the boy's word and your answer, and the herd keeper's, and the pasture's word, and said it back at the autumn sitting. I kept the question the weirs' people left with me the morning the star dimmed while you were away, and said it back.

The head herd keeper says: Nothing strayed. The pasture counted at dawn and dusk and kept three hands. The herd is nearly back to what it was before the loss, and the winter's milk and fat were less short. I watched you sleep and I have put it in the cord that you walk without tiring and sleep when the river asks. The offer stands, one more pair of feet.

The eldest midwife says: About seventy five were born and about sixty died, the old and the infants, as in other winters. I sent word of the child in the fever, and you held her. The star's pains come faster, and it has not borne.

The children say: The small boy said it first. The ferry child has a pole and has been to the ford. The old women say a spring that walks is only the spring going down the long way.

The town says: Salt is out. The carriers came less often again and asked more, and the council took what was offered and rationed it by house cord. We set one part in twenty of the barley and millet by again and smoked fish and eel with little salt, because salt is what we buy. The harvest was ordinary and the flood was ordinary in height. The Wary say the star dims when you stay, and the ferry keepers say it dimmed on the climb, and the Counters say the cord is the town's. The weir hands are sore, and the ferry keepers are glad, and the council has said it will speak again after the flood. We ask nothing of you that we did not ask last year. We are curious, steady and a little worried, more about the salt and the weirs than the star.
