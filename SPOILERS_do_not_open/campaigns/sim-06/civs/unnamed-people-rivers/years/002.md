# Year 2: unnamed people of god-rivers

Months are counted from the opening of the year. Spring is months one to three, summer four to six, autumn seven to nine, winter ten to twelve. Everything below is what the people did and met. What a power did is marked and left for the Keeper (see 002-report.md). Where the year turns on an uncertain outcome, it is written both ways, briefly.

## Spring

**The thaw and the flood (month one).** The ice went out of the channels as it does. The spring flood came ordinary in height and went down. After it the deep water lay a little farther from the ford stones again, on the side away from the weirs: the fifth flood running. The ferry keepers said so at the water gate on the first day the stones showed, and cut this year's mark on the gate stone, the sixth on it. Salt was out. The town's last salt went before the thaw, as the council had said it would, and what was left was kept for the sick and for the first curing of the spring fish. The council did not argue it; it had argued it all winter.

**The guest comes down the path (first days after the thaw).** Before the flood peaked, a weir hand at dawn work saw the woman come down the stream path from the hills above the town, barefoot, stooped, in the grey wrap and hemp cord, the pouch at her waist, as she had gone up at the turn of the year. He called the gate house. The Seat Warden had filled the cup at the low seat that morning, as every morning through the winter, and she left it where it stood and showed the guest to the guest bench beside the low seat. She was fed barley porridge and dried eel at the Warden's hearth, and asked nothing until she had eaten. The town knew where she had been: the Warden had seen the bench empty at the turn, and the weir hand said she came from the stream side. Nobody said more of it.

**She asks for a hearing (the day after).** She asked the Ferry Elder and the sitting Speaker, together, at the gate house, for a hearing at the council about the ford. The Speaker said the council hears a guest at a full sitting, and its first work after the flood is the flood and the choosing of the Speaker; she should have her sitting after. The Ferry Elder said that was as it should be, and that the ford was his trouble and he would sit for it.

**The choosing (sixth day after the flood went down).** The council chose its Speaker, by long talk until no one stood against it: the head of a middle kin house, a woman of about 52, who spoke little at the guest's first hearing last year and was heard out when she did. She is not named in this record (see people/the-speaker.md).

**The hearing about the ford (about the tenth day after the flood went down).** The council sat. The woman sat at the guest bench, not on the low seat. She said what the ferry keepers and the salt carriers had told her, and what the water had told her in autumn: that the deep water has lain farther from the ford after the floods. She said plainly that she did not know why, that she asked and did not order. She asked the Ferry Elder to have the ferry keepers cut the flood marks again this year, and to add a second cord of marks: the depth at the ford in low water in each season, taken at the same stone, so the town has its own count of the ford and does not rest on hers. (The people call this the low-water cord, to tell it from the second cord of her counts, which the Reckoner keeps beside his own.)
- The Ferry Elder said yes at once, to both; the flood mark was already cut.
- The Reckoner asked which stone, who cuts, who keeps the cord, and whose count it is. The council settled it by evening: the ferry keepers cut at the gate stone and at the ford stone at which the ferry's first post stands, each season at low water; the Reckoner keeps the cord of it; the town's count is the town's.
- The Speaker asked why a guest cared for the ford. She said her reason: a town that sees its own ford move decides better than one that is told. The Speaker said that was an honest answer and an odd one.
- The head of the oldest kin house asked: "The water told you? Which water, and what did it say?" She named the keepers first and the water second. He said that then the water had told her what the keepers saw, and a cord of what the town already knew. The Reckoner said a cord is cheap. The old man did not stand against it.
The council gave its go by the second evening, as it does. No oath was asked or given. The Ferry Elder sent a barge down the brook in the second month with the order to the ford keepers, and they cut the first low-water mark at the ford stone when the water fell after the flood.

**The children, and the Warden (month two, before the first market).** One fine evening she sat with the children at the fire in the yard of the gate house, about thirty of them, the ferry child, now ten, at the front. She told them plainly, as what she knew and not as a story: she woke at the spring head up the stream in the thaw, and before that she did not remember; she did not know why she came. The children took it hard and liked it. The ferry child said "then you are a spring's," and it was said in the town by the next night. Five of the older ones went up the stream path the next day, about two miles, to see the spring head. They found a spring like any other. They drank, and one said it tasted like the town's spring, which it does. The Ferry Elder said there was nothing in a spring but water. The Warden, that evening, heard the guest say at her own hearth that she would not ask again whether the cup had waited for her, that the cup was the Warden's and the town's to keep as it liked, and that she thanked her for the first hearth. The Warden was dry: the cup would be filled in the morning, as it always was. After a while she said the first hearth was a small thing and wanted no thanks. She did not say more, and did not say she believed anything of the guest. She said she would keep the cord of the guest's sayings as before.

**The first night of the dimmed star (month two).** On a clear night the blue deepened for about an hour and the star stood dim, and then it was as before. The weir hands on night watch and the ferry keepers up with the first barge saw it, and so did anyone who woke. The Ferry Elder said it was the fourth he had counted in two years. The Reckoner cut a knot for it on a cord of its own.

**The first market and the planting (months two and three).** Few carriers came; a handful of salt carriers crossed. The council took what salt was offered at the price asked, and rationed it by house cord. Planting went as it does: the terraces by the springs, the drained flats between the channels. The woman was in the town through the planting, at the guest bench and at the council as the days gave. The ferry child asked to go with her to the ford in the summer, and the Ferry Elder said no, since the child is ten and the ferry keepers are not a nursery. The child sulked and carried messages.

## Summer

**The going to the ford (month four).** She asked the Ferry Elder to name two ferry keepers and the Reckoner to name one to walk with her, and she would carry the low-water cord. The Ferry Elder named an older ferry keeper, a woman of about 40 with hands knotted from the pole, who has worked the ford landing most summers of her life, and a younger man of about 25 who cuts the marks cleanest. The Reckoner named his cord walker, a young man of about 22 who has walked behind him four years. The Ferry Elder stayed home with his knee. Four set out by barge down the brook, with the low-water cord and a bag of the town's cords for the hamlets.

**The hamlets on the way (month four).** They stopped at the reed hamlets of the nearer channels as the way allowed, which together hold a little over 150 people. At each she asked the hamlet's news and its count, stooped at the door, ate at the hearth and sat. The hamlets gave their names aloud and I do not set them down, my record giving none. The cord walker kept the count; it agreed with last year's within the births and deaths. At the second hamlet a man with a reed stump gone into his foot, fevered, was brought out by his wife, who asked whether the guest would hold his hands, and the guest did, in the evening, as before. What was seen: her hands went cold and white. What it did is a thing of a power and is for the Keeper.

Written both ways, briefly: if the fever broke, the hamlet says what it said last year, that the guest has the midwives' touch in a larger measure, and word of it goes down the channels ahead of her; if it did not, they say a visitor gave what she could, the foot is the foot's to heal or not, and they are kind to her all the same. Population differs by one at most.

**At the ford (month four or five).** The barge came to the ford after the hamlets, and the party stayed about six days. The ford keepers, who work the ferry there, showed the ford stones, the ferry's first post and the flood marks they had cut in the spring, and cut the summer low-water mark at the ford stone with the cord walker standing by, so that the low-water cord had its second knot from both ends. The woman asked the ford keepers what they had seen of the deep water. They said what the town says: farther, again. She asked the carriers who crossed during the six days (a party of salt carriers on the fourth day, and a few ordinary travelers) what they had seen of the larger river's bed, its floods and its crossings, and where they go instead when they cross less. The carriers said, as the town had been told, that others now go a southern way. What else they told her of the larger river is hers to tell and the Keeper's to give; I write none of it. She was told no place and no distance for the southern way, and I give none.
On the fifth morning she waded into the larger river from the ford stones, in the woman's shape, up to the knee, and stood facing upstream, her hands loose at her sides, until the sun cleared the reeds. The two ferry keepers and the cord walker watched from the bank; the ford keepers watched too and said afterward that it was a cold river for standing in. She came out and sat on the bank with her feet in the sun and said nothing of it then. She did not go in again. She took no step toward the water that anyone could see besides standing in it. What she felt, if anything, is for the Keeper. The ford keepers have since called her Knee Deep, among themselves, and it travelled up the brook with the barge.

**The return (month five).** The party walked home by the Brook Path, two days, the barge left at the ford for the ford keepers' use. The cord walker kept the low-water cord against the ford's marks. She walked as a mortal walks and ate at the hamlet hearths on the way where the path met them, and she was cold in the hands for days after the hamlet night, as before.

**The count (month five).** The cords agreed: a little over two thousand, about 345 hearths. This year's births to midsummer were as in other years.

**The second dimmed night (month six, in its first half).** A clear night. The blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, then as before. She was in the town. The weirs said it comes oftener now. The midwives, who count by pains, said the star was coming in its labor faster, and no one asked what it was in labor with.

**The listening below the gate (month six, late summer, in the low water).** She asked the Seat Warden, a ferry keeper and a well keeper, as in autumn, to sit by her while she knelt in the running water below the gate and listened for about an hour and no more, and to keep a fire, a mat and the dogs off through the sleep that followed. They agreed. Harvest was opening, so the bank was thinner than last year; a few dozen watched, among them the head of the oldest kin house, who stood apart. What was seen: she knelt in the water with her hands in the current, went still, did not answer when the Warden spoke to her, and after about an hour lay down at the edge and slept. The three kept the fire and the mat. She slept about two hours (this is the god's own file; the Keeper may rule otherwise). When she woke she asked for water; the Warden had it from the town's spring in a jug.
What she heard is not told to the people (it is the god's and the Keeper's).

Written both ways, briefly: if nothing came against her, nothing was seen but the watchers and the fire, and the town said the short listening was easier than the long one; if a dog or worse came, the ferry keeper sounded the horn and the Warden drove it off or the council learned more than she meant.

**Harvest (months six and seven).** Ordinary weather, ordinary harvest; every hand went to the barley and millet, the beans and flax. The woman reaped for a few days with the hamlet women.

## Autumn

**The herd that strayed (month seven).** In the first month of autumn, with every hand at the harvest and the hill pasture thinly kept, a herd of the town's goats with a few cattle, about 60 goats and 5 cattle by the Reckoner's cord, strayed from the hill pasture down into the reed and the channels. The pasture hands count at dusk by custom. How soon the loss was seen, who went and what came of it is an attempt for the Keeper (see report). What is written here is what the town did. The pasture hands raised the horn. The council sent eight on foot and by reed boat: three herders from the hill pasture, two weir hands, two ferry keepers with one reed boat, and a dog handler with three dogs. They were to seek in the reed and along the channel margins for up to four days, and the council would call them back at the end of the fourth day, since the harvest needed hands and the autumn rains had begun. The woman, in the town, was told as any was told. I did not write her into the search, the god's file not sending her.

Written both ways, briefly: if the herd was found, most of it came home within the days given, a few goats lost to the reed, and the town laughed at the pasture hands for a month, kindly; if it was not, the herd was lost in the reed or taken by the water, the town was without about 60 goats and 5 cattle for the winter's milk and fat, the Wary said the pasture was ill kept, and the old said the goats had gone to drink at the Dry Braid. A searcher lost to the channels in the second case would be a death: one at most.

**Two small alarms (month seven).** After the first autumn rains one well in the upper town ran cloudy, and a well keeper told the council at dawn and told the guest after, as asked. The keepers drew it and found silt from the rain wash, nothing fouled; it cleared in three days. The weir hands said the channel water ran muddy and thick behind the great weirs after the same rains, and told the council. Nothing came of either. The Wary said nothing, and it was noted that last year they said a great deal.

**The one sitting (month eight, after the harvest).** The council sat for her in one sitting, as she had asked, and she asked nothing but that they hear it. She told what the keepers had measured at the gate stone and the ford stone across the summer, what the carriers had said, what she had seen at the ford, and what the water had told her below the gate. She gave her counsel as counsel and said the council might answer no. She named no one to do any of it. The council heard her to the end, as the code requires, and talked three evenings. The Seat Warden said back the cord of her sayings at the close. What the counsel was, and what the water told her, is in the god's file and for the Keeper to set beside this; I write the council's way of meeting it. The council did not decide the large things in the one season. The Ferry Elder asked what she would do about the ford, and she answered as counsel. The head of the oldest kin house said the cords were the Reckoner's now and not hers, and that he would say nothing against a cord that was the town's. The council ended where it ends: the winter marks and the next flood will say more; it will answer at the thaw before the flood; and the smallest thing counselled it would do now.
Written both ways, briefly: if her counsel was a mark to read by, the ferry keepers set it in the autumn low water; if it was a new crossing or a second ferry, the council put it off, since it costs a season of the town's labor and the town does not move its weirs or its ford on one guest's word; the talk of the weirs, a year old, was kept for after the next flood. If what the water told her was that the deep water would not return, the talk is harder and longer and the Wary say "I told you the ford was going," meaning something else; if it was that the bed was quiet and the water would come back, the council holds easier and the Wary say a guest brought a good word.

**Salt and the store (month eight).** The council set aside again about one part in twenty of the barley and millet for the flood store at the water gate, turning the older grain to the table, and smoked fish with little salt. Salt was thin all year. It said again it would not set salt aside, since salt is what the town buys.

**The third dimmed night (month nine).** As the others. The ferry keepers said it was the third this year and that last year there were three in the whole year; the Reckoner's cord showed five knots by winter.

## Winter

**The ice (months ten to twelve).** The ice came as it does and the channels slept. She sat at the council through the winter, told stories at the fire and learned the new children's names, about 75 born in the year. She counted again with the Reckoner at the turn of the year, with the second cord, and the low-water cord's fourth mark was cut through the ice at the gate stone by the ferry keepers; the ford keepers sent theirs up the brook path by a runner after the ice held. She did not listen in the ice, and she did not go to the water.

**The mother with the fever (month eleven).** The eldest midwife, as the guest had asked, sent word of a young mother in the town with fever after a hard birth, whom the midwives' quieting touch had not eased. The guest held the mother's hands through an evening. What was seen: her hands went cold and white, and she warmed them at the hearth and said little. The child was put to a wet nurse. What it did is for the Keeper.
Written both ways, briefly: if the mother recovered, the midwives say that hands that can hold a fever are a gift they would learn from, and the eldest midwife says so at the council; if she did not, the town buried her in the frozen ground and no one blamed the guest, and the midwives say the quieting touch has its limits as well. Population differs by one at most.

**The dimmed star, twice (months eleven and twelve).** Two more nights, as the others. By the turn of the year the Reckoner's cord held five knots for the year. The salt carriers' word, which came at the last market in the autumn, that a sky god blinks, was said again at the fire.

**The weavers and the pots.** Spinning, weaving, pottery and long stories as in other winters. No raids, no strangers, no quarrel beyond the usual at the fire. One quarrel over a boat between two house heads was sat by the Reckoner.

**The turn (end of month twelve).** In the early thaw she went up the stream path before first light to the spring head, alone, as she had gone the year before. The Warden noticed the guest bench empty, and filled the cup anyway. The people made ready for the spring flood and the choosing of the next Speaker, which falls on the first days of Year 3.

## What the people did, in short

They kept the cords: the flood marks on the gate stone, six now, and the low-water cord at the gate stone and at the ford, four marks each by the turn. They heard a guest about the ford and answered by their own custom: yes to marks, long talk to the large things. They sent four to the ford and back. They searched the reed for a strayed herd. They watched a woman kneel in the water and sleep, and held a fever with her in the winter. They heard the star dim five nights. No one took her for a god. No office holder acts for her in her absence.
