# Year 1: unnamed people of god-rivers

Months are counted from the opening of the year. Spring is months one to three, summer four to six, autumn seven to nine, winter ten to twelve. Everything below is what the people did and met. What a power did is marked and left for the Keeper (see report-001.md).

## Spring

**The flood and the Speaker.** The spring flood came ordinary in height and went down. The deep water lay a little farther from the ford than before, the fourth flood running, and the ferry keepers said so at the water gate on the first day the stones showed. The council chose its Speaker on the sixth day after the flood went down, as it does, by long talk until no one would stand against the choice. The Speaker is unnamed in this record.

**The stranger at the water gate (first day).** On the first morning of the year a woman came down the stream path from the hills above the town, barefoot, stooped, in a grey wrap with a hemp cord, carrying a small pouch at her waist. A ferry child, a girl of nine who was at the water gate landing with a pole, saw her first and called the Seat Warden. By the town's code a stranger is fed at the first hearth reached. The first hearth was the Seat Warden's, in the gate house. The Warden had filled the cup at the low seat that morning, as every morning, and, seeing a stranger come in from the water's side on the first morning of the year, held it out to her as the custom's courtesy. She drank. Nobody said anything about it. She was fed barley porridge and dried eel and asked nothing until she had eaten. Afterward she gave her news as she had it: a woman from the water's side, who wished to stay a while, who asked for no rite, no shrine and no obedience. Asked what to call her, she said the water had called her the Lady of the Ford, or only the river, and that they might call her what they liked.

**Hearing at the council (about the ninth day).** She asked who kept the council and who spoke for the town, and asked to be heard once. The Speaker gave her the sitting. She said who she was as far as she knew it, said plainly that she asked and did not order, and that she would count the hearths, the heads, the children and those missing, aloud, so that no one thought she counted in secret. The council heard her to the end, as the code requires, and talked the evening and the next morning. The head of the oldest kin house stood against her counting, saying counting is what a salt carrier's master does before he takes a share, and the Reckoner was uneasy for the same reason. The council ended where it ends: she may stay as a guest of the water gate, at the guest bench beside the low seat (not at the low seat, which the Warden said is not for sitting), she may count, and the Reckoner walks with her and keeps a second cord of every count, so the town's count is the Reckoner's and hers is a copy. The old house head stood down on that condition. No oath was asked of her and none given.

**Two nights of the dimmed star (about the twelfth and the twentieth nights).** Most of the town was asleep; the ferry keepers and weir hands on dawn work saw it. The blue deepened for about an hour and the star stood dim. The town has seen it before. This year it came twice in the month the woman arrived, and the talk at the weirs linked the two. The Ferry Elder said it had dimmed last year too and nobody came.

**Her first weeks.** She sat where she was put, ate what was given, learned who held each office and how each house stood, and counted. She stooped to the doors, asked each head their house and children, and was fed at each. The ferry keepers took to her first, for she asked about the water. The midwives watched her hands: they were cold as stream water in a warm room and did not shiver.

**The Seat Warden takes her cord (month two).** She asked the Warden to keep a cord of what she said and did at each sitting and say it back. The Warden agreed at her own hearth, saying she would do as much for any guest who asked it, that she did not know whether the cup waited for this woman and would not say so before the council did. This is a trust given to a guest's word, not the taking of her for anyone awaited.

## Summer

**Walking the lowland (month four).** The Ferry Elder sent two ferry keepers with her, by barge down the nearer channels and on foot along the banks. They reached four reed hamlets, which together hold about a hundred and fifty people. At each she asked the hamlet's name, its people and its worst flood, stooped at the door and ate at the hearth. The hamlets gave their names aloud. This record does not set them down because my record gives none (see report). She learned where the water goes: which channels the flood fills first and which hamlets stand lowest. The hamlet people were glad of a visitor who asked about floods and not about the town's debts.

**Counsel on the flood marks and the store (month four, at the council fire).** Through the Ferry Elder and the Reckoner she asked the council to cut a mark for each flood height by the water gate, to keep a store of grain, dried fish and salt against a bad flood, and to have the well and spring keepers tell the council at once if any water turned foul. The council took it up in its own way. The ferry keepers cut marks on the gate stone for this year's spring flood and, by recital of the old ferrymen who had stood there, for the previous four, though two old ferrymen disagreed about the third flood's mark and the Reckoner had to sit the dispute (see below). The council agreed the well rule at once, since it is the code. The store it argued over until the harvest.

**The count (month five).** The Reckoner's cord and hers agreed on the town. By the cords the people at midsummer stood at a little under two thousand, in about three hundred and forty hearths. She then offered to carry the count back to the council. The council said it already held its cords, and took her copy only as a check.

**The third dimmed night (month five).** Seen from the hamlets as well as the town, since it was a clear night and she was at the farthest hamlet at the time. The dimming was as before. The hamlet folk said the woman had brought it from the town; the ferry keepers who were with her said it had come last year when she had not.

**Warm hands (months five and six).** A child of the second hamlet was pulled out of a channel by a hamlet woman, as the code requires, and was fevered and coughing afterward. The mother, who did not expect an answer, asked the visitor whether she would hold the child. She sat with the child's hands in hers through the evening. What was seen: her hands went cold and white while she held the child, and she warmed them at the hearth afterward and said little. In the same two months she did the same for one fevered woman in another hamlet and for a man in the town with a reed knife cut gone bad, asking nothing and taking no thanks. What each did to the one held is a thing of a power and is for the Keeper (report). The people's own account is in the lore file.

Written both ways, briefly: if the sick one's fever broke, the hamlets say the visitor has the midwives' quieting touch in a larger measure, and the demand on her grows; if it did not, they say a visitor gave what she could and the child was left to the ordinary course, and they feel kindly toward her all the same.

## Autumn

**Harvest (months six and seven).** Ordinary. Every hand went to the barley and millet, the beans and flax, in the frantic weeks of late summer. The counting woman reaped with the hamlet women and was no better and no worse than a willing townsperson. The goats and geese came off the hill pasture as usual. The ordinary harvest filled the barns to the next harvest in an ordinary flood.

**The well that turned (month seven).** After the first autumn rains one well in the lower town, near the channel side, ran brackish and foul. A well keeper told the council at dawn and told the woman after, as asked. The well was closed. For a few days the Wary said the stranger had fouled it, since it was the first well to turn since she came. The well keepers drew the lining and found it had slipped and let the midden wash in, no one's act. The council cleared her without being asked and relined the well. She helped haul stone for the lining, as anyone would.

**The store (month eight).** After the harvest the council set aside about one part in twenty of the barley and millet, by each house's cord, into a store at the water gate, and dried fish and eel for it, smoked and with little salt, since salt is short this year. It would not set aside salt. It said salt is what the town buys, and the carriers cross less each year.

**The new fish weir (month eight).** One small fish weir was set in a side channel, twenty worker-days of work in all, to try the position the council has talked about for a year. The great weirs were not moved. The council said the weirs would move after the next flood showed whether the deep water kept leaving.

**The long listening at the water gate (month nine).** She asked the Seat Warden, as one she trusted, to sit by her while she knelt in the running water below the gate, and a well keeper and a ferry keeper with a horn came too. The town gathered at the bank and watched. She knelt with her hands in the current for about half a day. What was seen: she went still, and did not answer when the Warden spoke to her, and then lay down at the edge of the water and slept, and did not wake for about a day and a night, though the Warden called her and the ferry keeper shook her shoulder. The three kept her under a reed mat shelter with a fire, and drove off a stray dog that nosed her. Nothing else came against her. The Ferry Elder wanted her carried to the gate house. The Warden said she had asked to be left, and she was left. When she woke she asked for water and the cup, and the Warden offered the cup, and she drank. What she heard is not told to the people (the god's and the Keeper's).

## Winter

**The ice (months ten to twelve).** The ice came as it does. The channels slept. She sat at the council through the winter, told stories at the fire, and learned the names of the children. The stories were of water. The children took to her; the ferry child named her and the name spread (see report). The Wary grew quiet and the Fond grew fond. She kept the Warden's cord and the Warden said it back at two sittings. She did not listen in the ice, and the people noticed she did not go to the water.

**The weavers and the pots.** Spinning, weaving and pottery as in other winters. No raids, no strangers, no quarrel beyond the usual at the fire. One quarrel over a boat between two house heads was sat by the Reckoner, who has had several this year.

**The second count.** At the turn of the year she and the Reckoner counted again, hearth by hearth, with the second cord. The count stood a little over two thousand.

**The turn (end of month twelve).** In the early thaw she went up the stream path before first light to the place where she had first come, alone. The Warden, who noticed the guest bench empty, filled the cup anyway. She was back at the gate before the choosing of the Speaker. The thaw was ordinary. The people made ready for the spring flood and the choosing of the next Speaker, which falls on the first days of Year 2.

## What the people did, in short

They fed a stranger at the first hearth, heard her out, fenced her counting with a second cord, let her walk their water, made a small mark on the gate stone and a small store, argued over the well and cleared her, and watched her sleep in the river. No one took her for a god. No office holder acts for her in her absence.
