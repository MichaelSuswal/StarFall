# Year 4: unnamed people of god-rivers

Months are counted from the opening of the year. Spring is months one to three, summer four to six, autumn seven to nine, winter ten to twelve. Everything below is what the people did and met. What a power did is marked and left for the Keeper (see 004-report.md). Where the year turns on an uncertain outcome, it is written both ways, briefly.

The Keeper's line on Year 3 is taken as what happened: the party went three days down the larger river, about ninety miles, into the reed country of many channels below the hills, and was back at the ford by the counted day; reed cutters were met at their staithes, and one dusk a band of the boat people, who traded reed work for a knife; the reed cutters say the salt for the stone towns now comes up from the sea in the south and does not need the ford; she felt nothing pull in the larger river; nothing came against the listening at the ford and she slept about two hours; the council's course after the autumn telling is the one written for it (it held to the marks, the small weirs and the thaw); the dimmings of the star are not known to be anyone's doing. The Keeper's weather and sky for Year 4 are taken as given: an ordinary flood, the deep water a little farther from the ford stones again on the side away from the weirs, an ordinary summer, a winter that froze the channels as it does, and the star dimmed seven nights, in months 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, 11 and 12.

## Spring

**The thaw and the guest's return (first days after the thaw).** The ice went out of the channels as it does. Before the flood peaked a weir hand at dawn work saw the woman come down the stream path from the hills, barefoot, stooped, in the grey wrap and hemp cord, the pouch at her waist, as she had gone up at the turn of the year. He called the gate house. The Seat Warden had filled the cup at the low seat that morning, as every morning of the winter, and left it where it stood, and showed the guest to the guest bench. She was fed barley porridge and dried eel at the Warden's hearth, unsalted, and asked nothing until she had eaten. The town knew where she had been, as in the years before. The weir hand said only that she came from the stream side.

**The children's fire, and the small boy (the first evening after).** She sat with the children at the fire in the yard of the gate house, about thirty of them, the ferry child, twelve now, at the front. The small boy who had said last spring that a spring cannot walk, a mill hand's youngest, sat nearer the front than he had, having been told by the older children all winter that he was the one who had made a guest say she did not know. She said to him, before the others, that he was right. A spring cannot walk, and she was not a spring. She had woken at one, and she walked, and she did not know why the one had come with the other. She said nothing of any working. The boy said, "I said." The children laughed, and this time the laugh was on the grown people and not on him, and he took it. The ferry child asked, "Then what are you?" and was told what had been said: that she was not a spring, that she woke at one and walked, and that she did not know. The ferry child said that was twice she had not known, and was told it was. The old women at the back said afterward that a spring that walks is the spring's going down, as the brook is, and that she had only said it the long way. The guest asked the Seat Warden, who had come to the gate of the yard, to keep the cord of it as she kept the rest, and the Warden said she would and went in to fill the lamp.

**The flood and the marks (month one).** The spring flood came ordinary in height and went down. After it the deep water lay a little farther from the ford stones again, on the side away from the weirs: the seventh flood running. The ferry keepers said so at the water gate on the first day the stones showed, and cut this year's mark on the gate stone, the eighth on it. The runner from the ford keepers came up the brook path with their spring low-water mark, and it was cut at the ford stone and at the gate stone as the council had set. Salt was out; the thaw took the last. The council did not argue it.

**The Ferry Elder reads the marks with her (month one, the day after the stones showed).** At her asking the Ferry Elder stood with her and the Reckoner at the gate stone and read the flood marks and the low-water cord. He said the deep water had moved again, by the stones and by the cord, a little, the same way as before: the seventh time. He said it was not the ford alone, since the weir hands say it left them whether or not they stood there, and he had still not been able to disprove it. The Reckoner said the cord said the same, in fewer words.

**The choosing (the sixth day after the flood went down).** The council chose its Speaker, by long talk until no one stood against it: the head of the middle kin house, the woman of about 54 who was Speaker in Year 2, called back from her seat, the first the council has called back. She is not named in this record (see people/the-speaker.md). The Speaker of Year 3 went back to his seat. It was said at the fire that the council wanted a Speaker who asks the plain question in a year when the plain question was a hard one.

**The sitting (about the tenth day after the flood went down).** The council sat. The guest, at the guest bench, said what she asked, and that she asked and did not press.
- The Ferry Elder said again before the council what he had read at the stone: the deep water had moved again this flood, a little, the same way. The Reckoner said the cord said the same.
- She said what last year had given: that the deep water lies farther again from the ford stones on the side away from the weirs; that the bed behind each weir is shoaled; that fewer laden beasts crossed; and that the reed cutters say the salt for the stone towns now comes up from the sea in the south and does not need the ford. The Speaker said the council had had all of it at the autumn sitting and in the Warden's cord. The guest said that was so, and that she said it again so that it was said in the same room as this flood. She said plainly that this might be the whole of it, that it was for the council to say what the ford was worth to the town if the salt went round it, and that she asked and did not press.
- The head of the oldest kin house said that the bed behind each weir was shoaled was a thing the water said and not a thing the town had measured. The Reckoner said the same: a thing the water says and the cord does not is a thing the town does not have yet. The weir hands, at the back, said a bed always shoals behind a weir, that is what a weir is for. The guest did not dispute it.
- The head herd keeper, the hill pasture woman of about fifty one, who had asked last spring why the guest would search for the goats and what she would do that a boat and a dog cannot, had the answer this spring, before the council. The guest said that she does not tire and does not sleep, so that she could walk the reed edge through a night and a day with the dogs and count heads when the boats must rest; beyond that, nothing that a boat and a dog cannot do, and she would be one more pair of feet. She said she would search if she was sent for, that a word to the water gate was enough, and that the keepers might say no. The head herd keeper said, without heat, that she had watched this guest sleep at the water below the gate in front of half the town, a day and a night the first year and two hours the next two, and that a person who sleeps is not a person who does not. The guest said that it was so. The head herd keeper said then she would put it in the cord as a woman who does not tire and who sleeps when the river asks, and that the pasture's word stood: look an hour, then send to the water gate, and the guest might come as one more pair of feet. The Seat Warden said it back, so it was in the cord.
- The guest then said what she asked about the weirs. She asked the Ferry Elder to ask the mill and weir hands to measure the shoal behind each weir at the low water. She said this was a guess and not a finding: the weirs may slow the water, the bed behind them may silt, and the silt may push the deep water away from the stones; the water tells what is and never why, and she could not say it was so. She said the measure cost the town only a few hands and a cord. She asked the Reckoner to keep a second cord of the measures with the marks. She asked the council whether it would try opening one weir for a season, and said that was the council's to grant or to refuse, and that she would not blame it for refusing a thing that costs the eel catch.
- The Reckoner asked whose count it was. The council settled it in the evening: the weir hands measure, with their own pole; the Reckoner's cord walker knots the figures and the Reckoner keeps the cord; the town's count is the town's. The head of the oldest kin house asked, "A guess, you say. Which weir?" She said she did not know, and that was why the measure. He said that was a plain answer. The head of the weir hands, a man of about 56 with two fingers gone to a weir stake, who works the oldest weir, said it left them whether or not they stood there, and that they would measure because the council said so and because it was their pole; and that nobody in the room was to hold it for them. The ferry keepers grinned. The weir hands did not.
- The council gave its go to the measure by the second evening. On the opening of a weir it said it would answer when it had the figures, and not before. The Speaker said that a thing the town had not measured was not a thing the town opened a weir for, and that a thing the town had measured might be.

**Planting and the pole (months two and three).** Planting went as it does, terraces by the springs and the drained flats. A handful of salt carriers crossed. The council took the salt offered at the price asked and rationed it by house cord. In the second month, on the first day the ferry opened for the summer, the ferry child came to the landing and the Ferry Elder gave her a pole cut to her height, which he had had by since winter. He told her in front of the landing that she was twelve and had a pole, and that she would go to the ford with the guest and the keepers by the brook barge, and stay at the ford landing with the ford keepers while they went on, and come home by the Brook Path with them, and that she would not put a foot on the larger river's barge. The child said she would not. He told the guest after, and did not ask her. The ferry child carried her pole about the town for a week.

**The dimmed star, first and second (months two and three).** A clear night in the second month: the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut a knot and beside it a mark: in the town. In the third month a second night, as the first. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut the second knot of the year and the second mark: in the town.

## Summer

**The council's leave (month four, the first days after the planting).** She asked the council, in a sitting, for leave for a going. She said what she asked: that two ferry keepers and the Reckoner's cord walker go with her, by barge down the brook to the ford and then down the larger river past where they turned last year, five days down or until they met the salt, whichever came first, and then back, before the autumn low water; she said the day she would turn. She said she would ask at the reed cutters' staithes and wherever the carriers came to the river where the salt joins it and whether the ford is needed. She said that the council might answer no. She asked also, of the keepers who would sit by her at the farthest reach, that they keep a fire, a mat and the cold and the dogs off through about two hours of sleep after a listening of about an hour, and send for the council's runner if anything came against them.
- The older ferry keeper of the ford landing said at once that no runner of the council's could be had below the ford: there was no path of the town's down the larger river and no one of the town to run it, and what a party of four had below the ford was a horn, a barge, a fire and three poles. She said it as a thing the guest ought to be told, and the guest said she had been told. The Elder said that if anything came against them they were to blow the horn and hold the barge and not wait for anyone's runner.
- The Ferry Elder counted the days aloud, as he does: a barge goes down about thirty miles in a day and comes up about twelve, so every day run down costs two and a half coming home. Five days down was twelve and a half days back, call it thirteen if the water ran strong. With the day by barge down the brook, two at the ford, five down, thirteen up, and two on the path, it was three weeks and a few days, and the ferry keepers must have bread for twenty one days. He said that five was two days past anyone of the town, and that he would have said four. He said the turn was to be made at dusk of the fifth day wherever they lay, or sooner at the salt, if it was met sooner; sooner for the water rising or a storm showing, never later; and not decided on the water by anyone tired and curious. The older ferry keeper, asked, said that she had said three last year because she knew three; five was two days she did not know, she would go five if the water stayed low and no storm showed, and she would turn sooner if either did.
- The head of the oldest kin house stood against it. He said the larger river was not the town's, that this was two days farther than last year's, and that a guest who took the town's keepers where no one came home was owed a hard question. He asked what she would do if the salt she found was some other people's. She said what she asked: where the salt joins the river and whether the ford is needed. He said that was two things, and she said it was. The Reckoner said his cord walker would knot every landing and hearth again, and bring it, and the town would not rest on hers. The council talked three evenings. The head of the oldest kin house did not stand against it by the third.
- The council gave its go on the Elder's terms: five days down and the turn at dusk of the fifth, or sooner, never later; home before the harvest was well opened; the cord walker's knots to the Reckoner before anything else was told. If the party was not at the ford by the third day of the next month the Elder would send the town's second barge down the brook with three poles, and a runner ahead by the path to the ford keepers.
- The Ferry Elder named the two ferry keepers, the same two: the older woman of the ford landing, about 42, and the younger man who cuts the marks, about 27. He asked the older woman whether she would go again, and she said yes, if she counted the days herself, and the Elder said she was the only one he would let. The Reckoner named his cord walker, about 24. The Elder stayed home with his knee. The council sent, by the party, a bundle of the town's linen, six pots, two bronze knives and dried eel, to be given at hearths below as the code gives to a stranger, and the cord walker was to knot what was given.

**The setting out (about the tenth day of month four).** The party went down to the brook landing at dawn, the four, with the low-water cord, a bag of the town's cords for the ford keepers, the gifts, and bread for the days counted and a few over. The ferry child came with her pole and a day's bread, not hidden, and the Ferry Elder, at the landing post, counted five aboard and said it was one more than he had counted and that he knew why. She stood in the stern with her pole and said nothing. The barge went down the brook one day and made the ford at dusk.

**At the ford (the first days).** The party stayed two days. The ford keepers showed the stones, the ferry's first post and the marks, and the summer low-water mark was cut at the ford stone with the cord walker standing by, so that the low-water cord had its next knot from both ends. The ford keepers gave back the barge left with them last summer and kept the ferry; the ford keepers took the child on at the landing, to learn the ferry under the oldest of them, who said she was quick and bad at keeping still. At the ford fire, the first night, the ford keeper who had asked last year what she was looking for in the river, and had asked after what another like herself would be, had his answer. She said that she did not know what another like herself is; that she had met none and felt none; that she would know one at close quarters by a weight in the air and not before; and that if one came she would sit it by a hearth and ask its news after it had eaten. She said nothing of any working. The ford keepers were quiet, and one said that was the town's own code, said by someone who had not been told it. The ferry child, on the bench by the fire, heard it. A party of carriers crossed on the second day, few, and ordinary travelers; she asked them what she asked, and what they said is for the Keeper, not mine, since it is of other people and other ground. She stood in the larger river to the knee at dusk on the second day and at dawn on the third, facing upstream, her hands loose, as she had stood before. The ford keepers called her Knee Deep again.

**The going down the larger river (about the thirteenth day of month four, five days down, the turn, up in about thirteen).** The barge cast off from the ford in the dawn of the third day after the party reached it, the child on the landing with her pole. The two ferry keepers poled and rowed, the cord walker kept the knots, the guest took the sweep as any would. They stopped at each landing and hearth the river gave and gave and asked as the code gives and asks: news, a count, where the carriers cross now, where the salt joins the river and whether the ford is needed. She stood in the larger river to the knee each dusk and each dawn, and nowhere else, and did not shift in anyone's sight. They were to turn at dusk of the fifth day wherever they lay, or sooner at the salt, and come up in about thirteen days, to be at the ford about the last day of the month, as the Elder had counted at the town landing.
This is an attempt. Who goes: four, of whom three are mortal. What they ask: where the salt joins the larger river and whether the ford is needed. What lies below the reed country, who is met, what the river tells her, and whether anything comes against her are the Keeper's. See the report. I write nothing of what was found.
Written both ways, briefly:
- If they went down to the fifth dusk, or to the salt if it was met sooner, turned, and came up as counted, they were at the ford about the last day of month four and home on the Brook Path in the first days of month five, the Elder having counted aloud at the landing post: five down, twelve and a half up, call it thirteen, the ford, two on the path. The ferry child came home with them, having poled the ford ferry for twenty days and been told by the ford keepers she was quick. The cord walker's knots went to the Reckoner before the town. The ferry keepers said, as they said last year, that you learn a river by going up it, and said it twice. The Elder grumbled that the larger river was not the town's, and asked to hear it again.
- If they did not, because the river held them, or the barge was lost or harmed, or someone came against them, or they were late, the town waited without word past the days counted. On the third day of month five the Elder sent the second barge down the brook with three poles and a runner ahead by the path to the ford, and the harvest opened without those hands. The ford keepers would have kept the child. The Wary said a guest takes keepers where no one comes home. If a ferry keeper or the cord walker was lost, that is a death, population one less, and the council shut the larger river below the ford to its own keepers and the Reckoner kept a cord of it. If a party was held or came against, more may have been lost. If all came home late, the town forgave it with a grumble.
Population differs by three at most.

**The dimmed night, third (month four, on the climb).** A clear night, the third night of the climb. The blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. The town saw it from the gate and the weirs. She was away from the town. The Reckoner cut the third knot of the year and a mark: away. The ferry keepers at the landing said at dawn that it had dimmed with her away, and that this proved nothing, as the Wary had said of theirs. The Wary said the cord still stood two in the town to every one away, three to one, for what it was worth. A question was left with the Warden that morning by some of the weirs' people, for the guest: whether the star dimmed for her. The Warden said she would put it in the cord for when the guest was home, and that it was not hers to answer. It met silence, since the guest was away, and stayed pending. If the party was on the water, they saw it from the river and the cord walker cut a knot of his own and told it when he came.

**The listening at the farthest reach (the evening of the fifth day, at the turn, if the party went so far).** At the turn she asked the two ferry keepers and the cord walker to sit by her while she knelt in the running water of the larger river and listened about an hour and no more, and to keep a fire, a mat, the dogs and the cold off through the sleep that followed. No one of the town could watch from a bank: the bank was strangers' ground, and there was nothing but the three, the barge and the horn. What was seen, if it was done: she knelt with her hands in the current, went still, did not answer when the older ferry keeper spoke to her, and after about an hour lay down at the edge and slept. She slept about two hours, as the Keeper has ruled of the last two. When she woke she asked for water, and the older ferry keeper had it from the barge's jug. What she heard is not told to the people.
This is an attempt too. The watch over the sleeper is the Keeper's to roll, and the circumstances are in the report.
Written both ways, briefly: if nothing came against her, nothing was seen but the fire and the barge, and the party started up at dawn; if a stranger, a beast or worse came, the older ferry keeper sounded the horn and held the barge, and what the party saw and lost is as the Keeper gives it.

**The return (the first days of month five, if the party was back).** They walked home by the Brook Path, two days, the child among them, the barge left at the ford for the ford keepers' use. She held no one's hands on the way. The party came to the gate house in the evening, and the Ferry Elder counted them aloud from the landing post, five, and was seen to count them twice. The cord walker took his knots to the Reckoner before he ate.

**The counts (month six).** The Reckoner's cord and hers agreed: a little over two thousand, about 350 hearths, births to midsummer as in other years. The pasture hands counted at dawn and dusk and lost nothing.

**The measure behind the weirs (month six, at the low water).** The low water came when it does. The mill and weir hands measured the bed behind each weir with their own notched pole, in hand-breadths, from the footing logs that each weir was set on when it was made, which the old hands know. The head of the weir hands poled; the Ferry Elder stood on the bank with his knee; the Reckoner's cord walker knotted what was called; the guest stood on the bank at the Elder's side and did not wade, and was told by the head of the weir hands that it was their pole. They found it shoaled behind every one, more behind the older: behind the oldest weir, set about sixty floods ago, nine hands; behind the second, about forty floods old, seven; behind the third, about twenty five, five; behind the newest of the four, about twelve, three. Behind the first small fish weir, a hand; behind the second, the finger's width that one winter and one flood lay on it. The Reckoner kept the cord of the figures beside the marks, as asked. At the fire after, the ferry keepers said the oldest weir had the most silt, so the weirs were the cause. The weir hands said the oldest weir had the most years, that a brook's silt settles wherever the water slows, that it was there before the weirs, and that the shoal was the brook's and not theirs. The Elder said two things that go together are two things. The Reckoner said the cord said what and not why, and the guest had said the same.
The weir hands' count of the eel: the four great weirs take about nine parts in ten of the town's eel; the oldest alone about one part in five; the oldest weir's year is about three parts in four the run of the autumn dark moon and the rest the spring and summer yellow eel; the two small weirs take the rest, a little.

**Harvest (months six and seven).** Ordinary weather, ordinary harvest. Every hand went to the barley and millet, the beans and flax. She reaped for some days with the hamlet women. The ferry keepers cut the autumn low-water mark at the gate stone, and she stood with them and read it, as the council had set, and said she would bring it to the sitting.

## Autumn

**Two small alarms, and none (month seven).** After the first autumn rains the upper well ran cloudy again; the keepers drew it and found the rain wash, and it cleared in three days. The weir hands said the channel water ran muddy behind the great weirs, and told the council as asked. Nothing came of either. No stock strayed beyond the ordinary.

**The dimmed night (month eight).** One night, in the town, a few nights before the sitting. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut the fourth knot and the fourth mark of the year: in the town.

**The one sitting (month eight, after the harvest).** The council sat for her in one sitting, as she had asked, and she asked nothing but that they hear it. She told what the keepers measured at the gate stone and at the ford stone across the summer; what the weir hands measured; what the carriers said at the ford and the reed cutters below it; what she saw on the farther river; and what the water told her at the farthest reach. She gave her counsel as counsel, whether the ford was worth a second ferry, a mark to read by, an opened weir, or a letting go of it, and said the council might answer no. She named no one to do any of it, and said she would ask the Ferry Elder, the Reckoner or the keepers the council named, and not before. She told the council and the Reckoner the count of the dimmed nights on the notched cord with where she stood each time: four so far this year, three in the town and one away, the third, on the climb. The Reckoner said he kept the same, with the marks, and that across the two years of marks the cord stood at ten nights, eight in the town and two away, and that she had been away from the town about one night in fifteen of the two years and the star had come while she was away two nights in ten, which he did not think was nothing and did not think was something. The head of the oldest kin house asked whether the star was hers. The Seat Warden said that the question had been left with her in the summer and she had kept it for the sitting, and said it back. What was answered is the god's words, not mine. The Seat Warden said back the cord of her sayings at the close. The council heard to the end, as the code requires, and talked three evenings, as it does.
- The weirs. The council did not wait for the Keeper's telling of what the water said. The figures were the town's own, and the cord of them lay beside the marks. The head of the weir hands said what the weir hands had said at the fire and said the eel were the oldest weir's winter. The head of the oldest kin house, who had asked in the spring which weir, said that the oldest weir was his house's; that his house's grandmothers had set it; that if the weir was wrong it was his house that had set it wrong and his house that should unset it; that he said no more than that, and that he would say nothing against a cord that was the town's. He said he would not have it said in the town that the guest's guess was tried on a weir that was not his. The Fond said he had never said anything so fair. The young Wary said he had given his house's weir to a guest's guess.
- The council ended where it ends. It would open a gap, a third of the width of the oldest weir, after the eel run of the autumn dark moon had been taken; it would leave it open through the winter, the spring flood and the low water after it, and the ferry keepers would cut the next low-water marks with the gap open; and it would then speak again. The loss to the oldest weir's households, the spring and summer yellow eel and the winter's catch at the gap, about one part in six of that weir's year, which is about one part in thirty of the town's eel, the other houses would make good by cord, a small share of dried eel and barley through the winter. The head of the weir hands said it was the first time the town had unset a weir and that he would say nothing against the council, and that his hands would do the unsetting because nobody else knew where the pegs were.
- Written both ways, briefly, for what the council took of her telling. If the party met the salt and came back with where it joins the river and a way to it that does not touch the ford, the council talks of sending its own salt party by the new way in spring, and the Wary say the town is spending its keepers on a stranger's errand, and talk of the ford grows small. If the party turned sooner or came back with little, the council holds to the marks, the small weirs, the gap and the thaw, and the ferry keepers say a ford that is crossed less is a ford that costs less, and the Elder says he will not be told that by anyone with sound knees. If the party was lost in part or whole, the council will not hear of the larger river below the ford and the talk is of the ford alone. If what the water told her was that the deep water would not return, the talk is harder and longer; if it was that the water would come back, the council holds easier. The Keeper's file settles which.

**Salt and the store (month eight).** The council set aside again about one part in twenty of the barley and millet for the flood store at the water gate, turning the older grain to the table, and smoked fish and eel with little salt. The carriers came less often again. The council took the salt offered at the price asked, which was higher, and rationed it by house cord. It said again it would not set salt aside, since salt is what the town buys.

**The gap in the oldest weir (month nine, after the run).** The weir hands took the autumn dark moon's run at all four great weirs as in other years, and at the dark of the moon after it, eight of them, with the head of the weir hands and the head of the oldest kin house standing by, pulled the stakes and the stone from a third of the width of the oldest weir in four days, about thirty worker-days. The head of the oldest kin house put his own hand to the first stake and was told by the head of the weir hands that it was a bad stake to start with. The Reckoner's cord walker knotted the day and the width. What the water does at the gap is not mine; I wait for the flood.

## Winter

**The ice (months ten to twelve).** The ice came as it does and the channels slept. She sat at the council through the winter, told stories at the fire, learned the new children's names, about 75 born in the year. She counted again with the Reckoner at the turn of the year, with the second cord, and the low-water cord's fourth mark of the year was cut through the ice at the gate stone by the ferry keepers, the ford keepers sending theirs up the brook path by a runner after the ice held; twelve marks each at the two stones now. She did not listen in the ice, and she did not go to the water. The cup stood at the low seat each morning, filled by the Warden, and she did not drink it.

**The dimmed star, three (months ten, eleven and twelve).** A night in each, as the others. She was in the town each time. The Reckoner cut the fifth, sixth and seventh knots of the year and the fifth, sixth and seventh marks: in the town, in the town, in the town. By the turn of the year the cord stood at seven for the year, and three, five, six and seven for the four years. The Reckoner said it did not count by ones. The midwives said the star's pains came faster and it had not borne.

**The child with the fever (month ten, the first hard cold).** The eldest midwife, as the guest had asked, sent word of a girl of about four in a crowded house of the lower town with the winter cough gone to a fever, whom the midwives' quieting touch had not eased. The mother asked, "Will you hold her?" The guest held the child's hands through an evening at the hearth. What was seen: her hands went cold and white, and she warmed them at the hearth after and said little. What it did is a thing of a power and is for the Keeper.
Written both ways, briefly: if the child recovered, the midwives say that hands that can hold a fever are a gift they would learn from, and the eldest midwife says so at the council, as she did last time; if she did not, the town buried her in the frozen ground and no one blamed the guest, and the midwives say the quieting touch has its limits as well. Population differs by one.

**The weavers and the pots.** Spinning, weaving, pottery and long stories as in other winters. No raids, no strangers, no quarrel beyond the usual at the fire. One quarrel over a boat between two house heads was sat by the Reckoner. The weir hands and the ferry keepers quarrelled kindly over the gap through the dark months, and the head of the weir hands said at the fire that if the deep water came back to the ford stones he would eat his pole.

**The turn (end of month twelve).** In the early thaw she went up the stream path before first light to the spring head, alone, as she had gone the three years before. The Warden noticed the guest bench empty, and filled the cup anyway. The people made ready for the spring flood, which will say what the gap does, and the choosing of the next Speaker, which falls on the first days of Year 5.

## What the people did, in short

They kept the cords: the flood marks on the gate stone, eight now, the low-water cord at the gate stone and at the ford, twelve marks each by the turn, and a new cord of the weir measures beside them. They heard a guest answer a small boy and a herd keeper. They called back a Speaker who asks the plain question. They measured the bed behind every weir with their own pole and found it shoaled, most behind the oldest. They gave a guest leave for a going down the larger river, five days down and the turn at dusk of the fifth, sooner never later, and sent three of their own with her, and let a girl of twelve go as far as the ford. They opened a gap in the oldest weir, a third of its width, the first time the town had unset a weir, and shared the loss by cord. They saw the star dim seven nights, three of the seven in the winter, and cut a mark by every knot for where she stood. No one took her for a god. No office holder acts for her in her absence.
