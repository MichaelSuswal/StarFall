# Report to the Keeper: unnamed people of god-rivers, Year 5, pass one

## How the god was met
Spring, first days after the thaw. A weir hand at dawn work saw her come down the stream path and called the gate house. The Seat Warden left the cup where it stood at the low seat, put her on the guest bench and fed her. She did not offer the cup. The Warden took her as a guest. She asked the sitting Speaker (the woman of Year 4, term running to the choosing) for a place at the first full sitting after the choosing; the Speaker said the council hears a guest at a full sitting and its first work is the flood and the choosing. She asked the Warden to carry her word to the herd keepers; the Warden said she would. Neither took her word as more than a guest's.
Spring, the first evening. The children, the ferry child (thirteen) at the front and the small boy (eight) nearer. The small boy said she had said she did not know twice and would say it a third time and that he would wait. The Warden kept the cord of it. Nobody took it as a working.
Spring, month one, the day after the stones showed. The Ferry Elder stood with her and the Reckoner at the gate stone at her asking and read the marks: the deep water moved again, a little, the same way, the eighth time. He asked the head of the weir hands, at her asking, to measure at the flood's fall; the head said it was the council's gap and his own pole and he would measure when the footings could be reached and not for any guest. He did not take the asking as more than a guest's. The Reckoner kept the figures on the second cord.
Spring, sixth day after the flood went down. The council chose a new Speaker, the head of the second oldest kin house, a man of about sixty one. That is the council's, not the god's.
Spring, about the tenth day. The sitting. She answered the three questions that had stood. The Reckoner said it was the first time a guest had agreed with a cord. A weir hand said "that she knows" was a small door. The head of the oldest kin house said a thing can be done and not known and that he had heard her say what she knew and no more, which was plainer than he had been given by guests who knew more; asked what she wants, he said it was a plain answer and that he would still ask it next year. Some of the young Wary said a guest who does not know is a guest who does not say; the Fond said a liar picks one. The old women took the first part of her wanting as said for them. The head of the oldest kin house said his house's gap had cost one part in six of the weir's year, that a hand in a year was not a ford, that one flood was one flood, and that the gap stayed open another year because it was his house's and his house said so. The head of the weir hands said his pole stayed on its peg and that his hands would not pull another third of anything on one flood. The head herd keeper said the pasture's offer stood and that the guest would come at the dark of the moon in month three. The council kept the gap open through the summer and autumn and opened no second gap. None took her word as the god's.
Spring, month three, the dark of the moon. The head herd keeper and three hands took her on the pasture one night, as an offer and not an order. They counted beside her; she walked the bounds; the counts agreed. The head herd keeper said it to the cord and not to her. The Warden said the cord back.
Summer, month four. The council sat three evenings for her leave. The head of the oldest kin house asked what she wanted of a road, was told three things, said that was three things, and did not stand against it; the young Wary did. The Ferry Elder said he could count a river and not a road, set the turn at dusk of the eighth day out, sooner if the road left the road to the south town or the water rose or a storm showed or the family asked what the party could not pay, never later, and not decided by anyone tired and curious. The older ferry keeper of the ford landing said she knew the carriers from the ford and not from the road, would not go, and named the younger ferry keeper the better walker. The Reckoner named his cord walker on condition that the knots come to him first and that no debt be taken in the town's name. The new Speaker gave the council's go. No oath either way. None took her word as the god's.
Summer, month five, the ford. The ford keepers took her at the ford fire as before and called her Knee Deep. The head of the carrier family asked, by hearth, after eating, agreed to have her and two of the town walk with the family for the town's goods and work at the loads. The older ferry keeper of the ford landing took the town's side of the telling to the family; the ford keepers said the terms were fair.
Summer, month five, while she was away. A pasture hand left the head herd keeper's question with the Seat Warden; it met silence and stayed pending; the Warden said it was not hers to answer. When the guest came home the Warden gave it her and she said yes, as the decisions give.
Summer, month six. The weir hands poled again at the low water; the guest stood with the ferry keepers at the autumn mark and read it, as the council had set.
Autumn, month seven, the dark of the moon. The second night on the pasture, at the herd keepers' asking.
Autumn, month eight. The council heard her in one sitting and talked three evenings. The head of the oldest kin house said his house would carry the autumn run's loss and would not be thanked for it. The Seat Warden said back the cord. The council kept the gap open through the autumn run and the winter and opened no second gap.
Winter. The eldest midwife sent no word of a sick child or mother. The Warden filled the cup each morning and the guest did not drink it. At the run of fish the oldest weir hands stood at the gap and said so; the Reckoner sat the quarrel of the shares; the head of the oldest kin house sent shares back by cord.
Whether each took her word as the god's helper: none did. No office holder has taken her for the one awaited or for a god. The Seat Warden holds the nearest to it, saying only that the cup has not been empty of her. The Keeper rules whether any office holder is a helper under the record's rule. I have written none as one.

## What was done in the god's name
Nothing was done in her name. Done at her asking, and kept by the people as their own: the Ferry Elder read the flood marks and the low-water cord with her; the ferry keepers cut the ninth flood mark and four more low-water marks at the gate stone and the ford stone, sixteen each now; the head of the weir hands measured with the weir hands' own pole at the flood's fall and again at the low water, the Reckoner's cord walker knotted it and the Reckoner keeps the second cord; the Seat Warden kept the cord of her sayings and the question left with her, and said it back; the herd keepers took her on the pasture twice; the council gave leave for the going, the Elder named the younger ferry keeper and the Reckoner his cord walker, the ford keepers asked the carrier family; the Reckoner kept the cord of the dimmed nights with the marks for where she stood. The council kept the gap open another year of its own choosing, on the town's figures and the head of the oldest kin house's word, after she had asked for no weir to be unset on hers; I count that as the council's. The head of the oldest kin house returned shares of the fish by cord to the houses that had made good the gap's loss; his own. The Reckoner sat the quarrel; his own. The council set aside again one part in twenty of the barley and millet, its own habit by now.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper
1. The salt road, summer, month five. Who tries: the god, the younger ferry keeper who cuts the marks (about 28), and the Reckoner's cord walker (about 25, exact counts), with a carrier family of about a dozen persons and their laden beasts, the family the ford keepers know best, which has crossed at the ford every dry season in their memory. Three of the town, two mortal. The older ferry keeper of the ford landing (about 43) keeps the ford landing; the ferry child (thirteen) stays at the ford landing under the ford keepers and does not go on the road. Set out: the town by barge down the brook about the tenth day of month five, one day to the ford; the family came to the ford on the twelfth day; the party leaves the ford with the family at dawn of the thirteenth day, on the carrier family's road toward the south town of the stone towns. The council's terms: eight days out, the turn at dusk of the eighth day out (the twentieth day), wherever they lie; sooner if the road leaves the road to the south town, or the water rises, or a storm shows, or the family asks what the party cannot pay; never later; not decided by anyone tired and curious. Back: eight days by the same road, at the ford at dusk of the twenty eighth day, the Brook Path two days after, home on the first day of month six. If not at the ford by the third day of month six the Ferry Elder sends the second barge down the brook with three poles and a runner ahead by the path. No debt is to be taken in the town's name. What they ask, of the family and whoever they meet, by hearth, after they have eaten: where the salt road runs, what the carriers pay at each stage, whether the town could be a place the road comes to. They carry the low-water cord, a bundle of the town's linen, six pots, two bronze knives and dried eel as the fee and for what the road asks, and bread for the days counted and some over. The god means to stand in any running water the road crosses, to the knee, at dusk and dawn, to show nothing, not to shift in anyone's sight, not to listen. What is the Keeper's: where the road runs beyond the ford, what is paid at each stage, who is met, what is said, what the family does at each stage, whether the family keeps to what it agreed at the ford, and whether anything comes against the party. I give no distance beyond the ford. Circumstances from my record that bear on it: no one of the town has walked any road beyond the ford; my people know water, not a road, and two of the three are ferry and cord people with no arms; there is no runner, no horn and no barge on a road, and the council's go rests on the Elder saying he can count a river and not a road; the record gives no hostile neighbor on the carriers' road; the carriers cross less each year and ask more; the carriers have told the ford keepers, three years running, only that others go a southern way, with no place, distance or reason, so what the family will say at hearth is unknown to the town; the town's code gives a stranger a hearth, and the family took the party in at the ford on that footing and for the town's goods and work at the loads; the Brook Path is two days on foot from the ford and was walked home each year; the reed cutters of the lower channels say the salt for the stone towns is landed far to the west and goes up by land (the Keeper's line on Year 4); it is the dry weeks, at the pace of a laden family. Success: the party goes out eight days with the family, turns at the eighth dusk or sooner as above, comes back by the same road as counted, is at the ford at dusk of the twenty eighth and home on the first day of month six, with the road's stages, what the carriers pay and whether the town could be a place the road comes to. Partial: the family will not have them, or parts from the road to the south town early, or the party turns sooner, or comes home with little, or comes home late, or comes home with news that costs the town (a quarrel, a debt taken against the council's word, a visitor following). Failure: a mortal lost, hurt or held (population one or two less), or the party not back by the third day of month six, in which case the Elder sends the second barge and the runner. I wrote the year both ways, briefly, in the year file, and the autumn sitting both ways.
2. Whether the carrier family will have her. The family agreed at the ford, by hearth, after eating, for the town's goods and work at the loads, in my file's telling, because the release gives me the family and the day they leave the ford. That is a persuasion of someone outside my people. If the Keeper rules the family would not, the party waits at the ford up to the fifth day for another family and comes home by the path, and the Wary say the town should have known. Written briefly in the year file.
Nothing else is contested: no fight, no search, no persuasion of another people beyond the above, no craft beyond what my people know. The pasture nights are ordinary: nothing strayed and nothing was seen to come. The gap in the oldest weir is ordinary work and the measures are the town's. The ferry child on the brook barge and at the ford landing is the Elder's own arrangement and settled.

## What turns on a power
1. The pull in running water on the road. Seen, if done: she stood in any running water the road crossed, to the knee, at dusk and dawn, facing upstream. What she felt, if anything, is for the Keeper; her file asks that the Keeper tell her in the next release if any water she stood in was of the larger river, its feeders or its outflow and a pull was there. My year does not turn on it beyond what the ferry keeper and the cord walker say of it and the ford keepers' name for her. At the ford, on the twelfth dusk and the thirteenth dawn, the water was the larger river's; seen by the ford keepers.
2. The answer about the star. She said she does not know what dims it, does not do it that she knows, would say if she did. If that is a claim about a power the Keeper settles it; I wrote no more of it. The people read it as a guest's plain answer. My year turns on it a little: the weirs' people and the head of the oldest kin house took it as plain and partial, and the young Wary did not.
3. The dimmed nights, three, in months 2, 2 and 7. The Keeper's, not mine. The Reckoner cut a mark beside each knot for where she stood: in the town each time (the third, month seven, if the party was home as counted; if not, away). The people tie it to her sometimes, wrongly, and I know no cause. The Keeper's line on Year 4 says the dimmings are not known to be anyone's doing; my people do not know that and say otherwise. The star did not dim in the summer, while she was away, or in the winter; the Wary make something of the summer and the ferry keepers answer.
4. The run of fish, month eleven. The Keeper's, not mine. Nobody did anything to bring it. She stood on the bank with the Ferry Elder and did not go to the water. The people make of it what they make of it (the lore file). A weir hand's child asked whether she had sent them; I wrote no answer. My year turns on it a little: the gap's second-year case, the shares, the winter's food, and what the council makes of the weirs at the next flood.
5. The god's decisions say she does not tire and does not sleep. The pasture nights: seen, she walked the bounds dusk to dawn and was not tired, and said she had eaten; the head herd keeper put it in the cord again. I wrote nothing beyond that.
6. No warm hands this year: none asked.
7. No listening this year. She did not go to the water in the ice.

## What touches anyone else
Salt carriers: crossed fewer times again, and at a higher price. The family the ford keepers know best came to the ford on the twelfth day of month five and agreed, in my file, to have the guest and two of the town walk with it for the town's goods and work at the loads. What they said of the road at hearth is the Keeper's, being of other people and other ground. Word of a barefoot woman who stands in the river may travel with the carriers; I wrote none of that.
Boat bands: traded at dusk as before. Nothing seen of them at the town this year.
Namers: none came.
No stranger came this year but the carriers and the ordinary travelers at the ford.
Parties sent out by my people: one, three of the town, the god, the younger ferry keeper who cuts the marks and the Reckoner's cord walker, with the carrier family from the ford. Set out for the ford by barge down the brook about the tenth day of month five, one day, two days at the ford before the family came and left, then out with the family from the ford at dawn of the thirteenth day for eight days toward the south town of the stone towns, then the turn at dusk of the eighth day, and back as above, then the Brook Path two days home. The road beyond the ford, and what is paid, who is met and what is said, are the Keeper's to price and to give. The ford keepers asked the family and the older ferry keeper of the ford landing took the town's side of the telling; the ford keepers sent their low-water marks up the Brook Path by a runner in winter after the ice held. The ferry child went to the ford and no farther.
Anything carried: the low-water cord, a bundle of the town's linen, six pots, two bronze knives and dried eel, as the fee for the family and for what the road asks of those who walk it, and no debt in the town's name. The cord walker's knots of the stages, hearths and everything given are the Reckoner's before the town's.
Searchers: none; no stock strayed.

## Asked of the god
The weirs' people, to the Seat Warden, the summer of Year 4, and the head of the oldest kin house at the autumn sitting of Year 4: "Does the star dim for her? Is it hers?" Answered in front of them at the spring sitting, as her file gives: she does not know what dims it, does not do it that she knows, would say so if she did; she was away about one night in fifteen of the two years, as the Reckoner counted; she agrees with the Reckoner that it is not nothing and not something.
The head of the oldest kin house, every year: "What do you want?" Answered in front of him at the spring sitting: that they be fed, the children not drowned and the grandmothers not buried in a bad year; a council that can say no to her, and it has; to find others like her, slowly and by choice, and she has found none. He said it was a plain answer and he would ask it next year.
The small boy, at the fire: said she would say she did not know a third time, and that he would wait. Not answered in my files.
The Ferry Elder, at the gate stone: "Has the deep water moved, or has the ford?" Not answered by the god; the Elder answered it himself. The god had asked him.
The Reckoner, at the sitting: "What is the figure worth?" Answered in part by the cord: what and not why.
The head herd keeper, at the sitting: "Will you come to the pasture?" Answered in front of her through the Warden's carrying: yes, at the dark of the moon in month three. Again, left with the Warden in month five while the god was away: "Does the offer stand for the dark of the moon in the first month of the harvest?" Met silence, since she was away, and stayed pending; answered when she came home: yes.
The head of the oldest kin house, at the council before the going: "What do you want of a road?" Answered in front of him: where the salt road runs, what the carriers pay at each stage, and whether the town could be a place the road comes to. He said that was three things, and she said it was.
The young Wary, at the council: "Why does a guest walk a road?" Answered, as her file gives: the council cannot weigh a ford it does not know the worth of against a salt road it has not seen.
The Ferry Elder, at the council: "How many days, and when do you turn?" Answered by the council on his counsel; the settling was theirs.
The ferry child, at the landing, to the Elder: "Can I go on the road?" Answered by the Ferry Elder, not the god: no, it is a road and not a ferry; she poles the ford ferry under the ford keepers and comes home with the party.
The head of the carrier family, at the ford, by hearth, after eating: "What would you have of us?" Answered by the guest, in the telling above; the terms were the town's goods and work at the loads.
A weir hand's child, at the weir, in the last of the ice: "Did you send the fish?" Not answered in my files; her words are the DM's.
Any further question the children, the keepers or the carriers put and any answer I did not write; the god's words are the DM's.

## Names said aloud
The Counting Woman: still said by the children and many in the town. Said at the fire in the spring.
Knee Deep: said by the ford keepers and the ferry keepers at the ford.
Nessa Ferry: not said this year.
The name of the carrier family: said aloud by the head of the family to the ford keepers, at the ford fire, on the twelfth day of month five, when she was fed. I do not set it down, since my record gives none.
Names of the four reed hamlets: not asked this year, since the party did not stop at them.
No name for the people or their home was said. No office holder was named, the Speaker included. The older ferry keeper, the younger ferry keeper, the cord walker, the head herd keeper, the head of the weir hands, the small boy and the ferry child are not named. If a name is said aloud on the road, by the party or by anyone met, the Keeper writes who said it, when and to whom; I write none.

## What the people tell their god
These are the town's words, put together by office as each would say it. They are the town's telling of its year, and of the going with the carriers they say only that it was tried.

The Ferry Elder says: The flood mark on the gate stone is the ninth. The deep water has moved again from the ford stones, on the side away from the weirs, a little, the eighth flood running; I read it with you and said so. The low-water cord stands at sixteen marks at the gate stone and sixteen at the ford stone. The weir hands measured with their own pole at the flood's fall and again at the low water, and I stood on the bank with my knee. Behind the oldest weir, along the gap, eight hands; to either side nine; behind the second seven and a half, the third five and a half, the newest three and a half; behind the first small fish weir a hand and two fingers, behind the second two fingers. The gap is a hand lower and the deep water is not back, and those are two things that go together, and two things that go together are two things. My girl poled the ford ferry again, as I told her, and went no farther than the ford. You went on the road with the carriers and I named a ferry keeper to go; it was tried. A road is not a river and I cannot count it.

The Reckoner says: The cord stands at three knots of the dimmed star this year, and three, five, six, seven and three for the five years, twenty four, and it does not count by ones. Three this year, with a mark for where you stood at each; the marks are yours to know. Across the three years of marks the cord stands at sixteen, fourteen in the town and two away. You were away about twenty four nights of the year and the star did not come in the summer, which I do not think is nothing and do not think is something. You said you agreed with me. I keep the cord of the weir figures beside the marks, now at the flood's fall and the low water, with the season written, and it is the town's. No debt was taken in the town's name. The cord walker's knots came to me before the town. A cord says what, and not why. Your counts and mine still never differ.

The head of the weir hands says: We measured because it was our gap and our pole. The gap is a hand lower along its line, as a gap is, and every other weir is a half hand fuller. The deep water is not back at the ford stones and my pole is on its peg. The oldest weir's autumn take was less than in other years by about a fifth, and the gap cost the oldest weir about one part in four of its year, about one part in twenty of the town's eel, made good by cord. In the eleventh month the fish stood at the gap four days, more than I have seen in my life, and I do not know why, and I will say it before the ferry keepers do.

The head of the oldest kin house says: The oldest weir is my house's, and the gap was open a second year because my house said so. One flood is one flood. You said what you want, and it was plain, and I will ask it next year. I sent shares of the fish back by cord to the houses that had made the loss good. I asked whether the star is yours and you said you do not know. I still ask what you want.

The Speaker says: I was chosen, and I say little. The council heard you in one sitting and talked three evenings, and kept the gap open through the autumn and the winter and opened no second, and will speak again after the flood.

The Seat Warden says: The cup was filled each morning and has not been poured out. I kept the cord of the boy's third and of your answers to the weirs' people and the head of the oldest kin house, and the herd keeper's question that waited while you were away, and I said it back at the autumn sitting.

The head herd keeper says: Nothing strayed. The pasture counted at dawn and dusk and kept three hands. You came to the pasture twice at the dark of the moon and the counts agreed. The herd is back to what it was before the loss. I have put in the cord that you do not tire.

The eldest midwife says: About seventy five were born and about sixty died, the old and the infants, as in other winters. No one sent for you this winter. The star dimmed three nights, as few as in the first year, and none in the winter, and a labor that slows ends one way or the other.

The children say: The small boy said you would say you did not know a third time and he waited. The ferry child has been to the ford again. The fish came to the gap and we took them by hand.

The town says: Salt is out. The carriers came less often again and asked more, and the council took what was offered and rationed it by house cord. We set one part in twenty of the barley and millet by again. The harvest was ordinary and the flood was ordinary in height. In the eleventh month a great run of fish came up the river and stood at the weirs four days, thickest at the opened gap, and the children took them by hand; we ate fresh, froze, smoked and salted what we could, and a part will not last the thaw without salt. Nobody brought them that we know. The Wary say the star dims when you stay, and the ferry keepers say you were away twenty four nights and it is no sort of count, and the Counters say the cord is the town's. The weir hands are less sore, and the ferry keepers are waiting. We ask nothing of you that we did not ask last year. We are curious, steady and a little worried, more about the salt than about the star.
