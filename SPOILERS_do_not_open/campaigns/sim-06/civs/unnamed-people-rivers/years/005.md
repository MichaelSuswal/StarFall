# Year 5: unnamed people of god-rivers

Months are counted from the opening of the year. Spring is months one to three, summer four to six, autumn seven to nine, winter ten to twelve. Everything below is what the people did and met. What a power did is marked and left for the Keeper (see 005-report.md). Where the year turns on an uncertain outcome, it is written both ways, briefly.

The Keeper's line on Year 4 is taken as what happened, and where it differs from my Year 4 file it wins: the party went five days down to where the larger river meets the sea in a shifting delta of reed and channels, and was about twelve days coming home, not thirteen; all four came back; no salt comes up that river; the reed cutters of the lower channels say the salt for the stone towns is landed far to the west and goes up by land; nothing came against the party; the girl's fever came down; the dimmings of the star are not known to be anyone's doing; what the water does at the gap in the oldest weir shows at the spring flood. The council's course after the autumn telling is the one Year 4 wrote for a party that came home with little of salt: it held to the marks, the small weirs, the gap and the thaw, and set no party of its own. The ferry keepers who went say the larger river ends in a sea, and that the sea is salt, and that the salt still does not come up it.
The Keeper's weather and sky for Year 5 are taken as given: an ordinary spring flood, the deep water a little farther from the ford stones again on the side away from the weirs, an ordinary summer with the low water when it comes, a winter that froze the channels as it does, the star dimmed three nights (months 2, 2 and 7), and in the eleventh month, in the last of the ice, a great run of fish.

## Spring

**The thaw and the guest's return (first days after the thaw).** The ice went out of the channels as it does. Before the flood peaked a weir hand at dawn work saw the woman come down the stream path from the hills, barefoot, stooped, in the grey wrap and hemp cord, the pouch at her waist, as she had gone up at the turn of the year. He called the gate house. The Seat Warden had filled the cup at the low seat that morning, as every morning of the winter, and left it where it stood, and showed the guest to the guest bench. She was fed barley porridge and dried eel at the Warden's hearth, unsalted, and asked nothing until she had eaten. The town knew where she had been. The weir hand said only that she came from the stream side.
She asked the sitting Speaker, the woman of Year 4, whose term runs to the choosing, for a place at the first full sitting after the choosing, because three questions had stood unanswered a year and she would rather answer them where the weirs' people and the head of the oldest kin house sat. The Speaker said the council hears a guest at a full sitting and its first work after the flood is the flood and the choosing, as in other years; she should have her place after. She asked the Seat Warden to carry her word to the herd keepers, since the head herd keeper's offer of one more pair of feet had stood a year and she would take it. The Warden said she would.

**The children's fire (the first evening after).** About thirty children in the yard of the gate house, the ferry child, thirteen now, at the front, the small boy, eight, nearer than he used to sit. Nobody asked her a question. The small boy said that she had said she did not know twice last year and was going to say it a third time, and that he would wait. The children laughed. What she said to it is not in this record. The ferry child said nothing and watched her hands. The Warden, at the gate of the yard, kept the cord of it.

**The flood and the marks (month one).** The spring flood came ordinary in height and went down. After it the deep water lay a little farther from the ford stones again, on the side away from the weirs: the eighth flood running. The ferry keepers said so at the water gate on the first day the stones showed and cut this year's mark on the gate stone, the ninth on it. The runner from the ford keepers came up the brook path with their spring low-water mark and it was cut at the ford stone and the gate stone as the council had set. Salt was out; the thaw took the last. The council did not argue it.

**The Ferry Elder reads the marks with her (month one, the day after the stones showed).** At her asking the Ferry Elder stood with her and the Reckoner at the gate stone and read the flood marks and the low-water cord. He said the deep water had moved again, a little, the same way, the eighth time, and that it was not the ford alone, since the weir hands say it left them whether or not they stood there and he had never disproved it. The Reckoner said the cord said the same. She asked the Elder to ask the head of the weir hands to measure the bed behind the opened oldest weir and behind the others, with their own pole, at the flood's fall, and the low-water cord at the two stones to be set beside last year's, and the Reckoner to keep it on the second cord. She said that two things going together are two things, that the pole was the weir hands' and the finding theirs, and that whether to leave the gap open, close it, or open another was the council's to say and, for his house's weir, the head of the oldest kin house's. She asked no weir to be unset on her word.

**The measure at the flood's fall (month one, the fifth day after the water fell enough to pole the footings).** The head of the weir hands said to the Elder that the council had set last autumn that the gap be read, that it was their gap and their pole, and that he would measure when the footings could be reached and not for any guest. The mill and weir hands measured the bed behind each weir with their own notched pole in hand-breadths from the footing logs. The Elder stood on the bank with his knee, the Reckoner's cord walker knotted what was called, and she stood on the bank at the Elder's side and did not wade. Behind the oldest weir, along the line of the gap, eight hands, a hand lower than last year's nine; to either side of the gap, nine, as it was. Behind the second weir seven and a half; the third five and a half; the newest of the four three and a half. Behind the first small fish weir a hand and two fingers; behind the second, two fingers. The Reckoner cut the figures on the second cord beside last year's, and wrote the season beside each, since last year's pole was at the low water and this year's at the flood's fall.
At the fire after, the ferry keepers said the gap had gone down a hand and the weirs were the cause. The head of the weir hands said a hole in a weir lets the brook through and a brook scours where it runs, that this was what a gap is, that nine to either side was what the brook had laid, and that the deep water was not back at the ford stones, so his pole stayed on its peg. The Elder said two things that go together are two things. The Reckoner said the cord said what and not why. The head of the weir hands said he would pole it again at the low water, since the flood lays silt thin and the low water shows what stays.

**The choosing (the sixth day after the flood went down).** The council chose its Speaker, by long talk until no one stood against it: the head of the second oldest kin house, a man of about sixty one, heavy, slow, who says less than any Speaker the town remembers and has the Warden say the cord back after each thing. He is not named in this record (see people/the-speaker.md). The Speaker of Year 4 went back to her seat.

**The sitting (about the tenth day after the flood went down).** The council sat. The guest, at the guest bench, said what she asked and that she asked and did not press. She answered the three questions that had stood.
- To the weirs' people and the head of the oldest kin house, who asked in the summer and the autumn whether the star dims for her and whether it is hers: she said she does not know what dims it, that she does not do it that she knows, and that she would say so if she did. She said she was away from the town about one night in fifteen of the two years, as the Reckoner counted, and that she agreed with him that it is not nothing and not something. The Reckoner said it was the first time a guest had agreed with a cord. A weir hand at the back said that "that she knows" was a small door. The head of the oldest kin house said a thing can be done and not known, and that he had heard her say what she knew and no more, which was plainer than he had been given by guests who knew more. Some of the young Wary said a guest who does not know is a guest who does not say. The Fond said she had said she did not know three winters running about three different things, and that a liar picks one.
- To the head of the oldest kin house, who has asked every year what she wants: she said she wants them fed, the children not drowned and the grandmothers not buried in a bad year. She wants a council that can say no to her, and it has. She wants to find others like her, slowly and by choice, and she has found none. The old women said the first part was said for them. The head of the oldest kin house said nothing for a while, and then that it was a plain answer, and that he would still ask it next year, since a thing wanted is not the same in each year. She said he might. She said nothing of any working through water, and the council did not ask.
- She asked the Seat Warden to keep the cord of it as she kept the rest, and the Warden said she would, and said back the question left with her last summer, so that the answer sat beside it in the cord.
The Ferry Elder then said what he had read at the stone and what the pole had found, and the head of the weir hands said what he had said at the fire. The council asked, as it asks, what the figures were worth.
- The Reckoner said the cord stood: along the gap, a hand lower; to either side, as it was; every other weir, a little more; the deep water, not back. The head of the weir hands said what he had said at the fire.
- The head of the oldest kin house said that his house's gap had cost it one part in six of the weir's year to learn that a hole in a weir is a hole, that a hand in a year was not nothing and was not a ford, and that one flood was one flood. He said his house's grandmothers had set the weir and his house had put the first hand to the gap, and that he said now, so that no young Wary said it of him later, that the gap stayed open another year because it was his house's and his house said so. The Fond said it was the second fair thing. The young Wary said he was being talked into it a second time.
- The guest said again what she had said at the stone: that she asked for no weir to be unset on her word, that the finding was the weir hands', and that the council might answer no.
- The new Speaker said the council had said last autumn that it would speak again after the flood and the low water, and that it would speak now as far as it could. The council ended where it ends: the gap stays open through the summer low water and the autumn; the weir hands pole it again at the low water; the ferry keepers cut the low-water marks with the gap open; no second gap is opened this year unless the autumn sitting says; the council will hear the low-water figures and the guest's telling together in the autumn. The head of the weir hands said his hands would not pull another third of anything on one flood, and that if the council wanted another weir opened it should say which and in what year.
- The head herd keeper, a hill pasture woman of about fifty two, said she had had the guest's word from the Warden. She said the pasture's offer stood as it had stood: one dusk to dawn at the dark of the moon, the guest counting at dusk and dawn beside the herd keepers, and any hand might say no. She said no one had. She said the guest would come to the pasture at the dark of the moon in the third month, after the kidding, and that the guest should bring nothing and be on time.

**Planting (months two and three).** Planting went as it does, terraces by the springs and the drained flats. A handful of salt carriers crossed. The council took the salt offered at the price asked, which was higher, and rationed it by house cord.

**The dimmed star, twice (month two).** A clear night in the first half of the month: the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and then it was as before. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut the first knot of the year and a mark: in the town. A second night a little after the middle of the same month, as the first. She was in the town. The second knot and the second mark: in the town. The midwives said the star's pains were still coming, and that two in a month was what they called a hard labor. The Wary said it stood three in the town to none away this year, for what it was worth.

**The first night on the pasture (month three, the dark of the moon).** She went up to the hill pasture at dusk and was met by the head herd keeper, the three hands who keep it, and the dogs. The herd keepers counted the herd at dusk, goats, cattle and geese, with her beside them, and she counted aloud after them and the counts agreed to the head. They kept the fold and the reed edge through the dark in turns, as they do, and she walked the pasture bounds from the fold to the reed's edge and back, not sitting, through the night. At dawn they counted again and it agreed with dusk. The head herd keeper said, to the cord and not to her, that the count was the same by two counts and that the guest had not tired. A hand said she was one more pair of feet and that it was a good deal more than they had had. Nothing strayed and nothing was seen to come to the fold. She did not eat, and said she had eaten. The Seat Warden, who had come up at dawn, said the cord back, so it was in it.

## Summer

**The council's leave (month four, the first days after the planting).** She asked the council, in a sitting, for leave for a going. She said what she asked: that she go with the salt carriers on their road toward the south town of the stone towns, ten days' walk out at most, and turn, and that she be home before the autumn low water. She asked that the Ferry Elder name one ferry keeper and the Reckoner his cord walker to go with her, and that the ford keepers be asked which carrier family would let them walk with them, to be paid in the town's goods and in work and in nothing of hers. She said what she meant to ask on the road and of whoever she met, by hearth, after they had eaten: where the salt road runs, what the carriers pay at each stage, and whether the town could be a place the road comes to. She said she would show nothing and not shift in anyone's sight, would stand in any running water she crossed to the knee at dusk and dawn, and would not listen on this walk. She said that while she was away no one was to act for her, and that a question put to her would wait. She said the reason: that the council could not weigh a ford it did not know the worth of against a salt road it had not seen. She said the council might answer no.
- The head of the oldest kin house asked her what she wanted of a road. She said what she had said, the three things. He said that was three things, and she said it was. He did not stand against it. The young Wary did, saying a road is not a river, that the town has no coin on it, that two of its own would walk where no one had been with no horn and no barge, and that a guest who took keepers where no one came home was owed a hard question. They said it for three evenings. The head of the oldest kin house said, on the second, that he had asked that question himself two years and had come to think the keepers were the town's to send or keep.
- The Ferry Elder said that he could count a river and could not count a road, that a barge goes down thirty miles a day and up twelve and a woman on a road goes as the road lets her, and that he would not set a day for a place he had not seen. He said ten out was twenty back and forth and ten was not a number he would set; the turn was to be made at dusk of the eighth day out, wherever they lay, or sooner if the road left the road to the south town, or the water rose, or a storm showed, or the family asked what the party could not pay; never later, and not decided by anyone tired and curious. Eight back was sixteen days from the ford. The older ferry keeper of the ford landing, asked, said she knew the carriers from the ford and not from the road; they leave at dawn laden and are not seen again; she would not go, since the ford landing was her work, and the younger ferry keeper who cuts the marks was the better walker.
- The Reckoner said his cord walker would knot every stage and hearth and everything given, and bring the knots to him before the town, and that no debt was to be taken in the town's name for any reason.
- The council gave its go on the Elder's terms: eight out and the turn at dusk of the eighth, or sooner, never later; no debt in the town's name; the knots to the Reckoner first; home before the harvest was well opened. If the party was not at the ford by the third day of month six the Elder would send the second barge down the brook with three poles and a runner ahead by the path.
- The Ferry Elder named the younger ferry keeper who cuts the marks, about 28, and the Reckoner named his cord walker, about 25. The Elder stayed home with his knee. The council sent by the party a bundle of the town's linen, six pots, two bronze knives and dried eel: as the fee for the carrier family, and for what the road asks of those who walk it, and no more. The ferry child, thirteen, asked the Elder to let her come on the road. He said it was a road and not a ferry. She said it was a ferry that goes on land. He said she would pole the ford ferry under the ford keepers again while they were away and come home by the Brook Path with them, and she said she would.

**The setting out (about the tenth day of month five).** The party went down to the brook landing at dawn, the three, the ferry child with her pole, with the low-water cord, the gifts and bread for the days counted and some over. The Elder counted four aboard and said it was the number he had counted. The barge went down the brook one day and made the ford at dusk.

**At the ford (the tenth to the thirteenth day of month five).** The ford keepers gave the town the stones and the ferry's first post, and the summer low-water mark was cut at the ford stone with the cord walker standing by, so that the low-water cord had its next knot from both ends. The ferry child was taken on again at the landing by the oldest ford keeper, who said she was quick and still bad at keeping still. The older ferry keeper of the ford landing, who had gone to the ford ahead of the party by the path to keep the landing, said the family the ford keepers know best was due: the carrier family that has crossed at the ford every dry season in the ford keepers' memory and their mothers', about a dozen persons with their laden beasts, as the ford keepers count them. They came on the twelfth day. The head of that family said its name aloud at the ford fire to the ford keepers, as carriers do at a hearth, and I set none down, my record giving none. She was fed, as the code requires, before she was asked. The guest asked her what she asked, by hearth, after the family had eaten: whether the guest and two of the town might walk with the family on its road toward the south town and turn, and what the family would have for it. The family said it would have them, for the town's goods and for work at the loads, and said what it would have; the ford keepers said it was a fair thing and the cord walker knotted it. She stood in the larger river to the knee at dusk on the twelfth day and at dawn on the thirteenth, facing upstream, her hands loose, as she had stood before. The ford keepers called her Knee Deep again.

**The going with the carriers (the thirteenth day of month five, eight days out, the turn, eight back).** The family left the ford at dawn of the thirteenth day by the road they take, and the three of the town went with it and the ferry child stood on the landing with her pole. They were to turn at dusk of the eighth day out wherever they lay and come up by the same road, to be at the ford at dusk of the twenty eighth, as the Elder had counted at the town landing. The guest and the two walked with the family as the family walked. They stopped where the family stopped and asked, as the code gives and asks, news, a count, what the family paid at each stage, where the road runs and whether the town could be a place it comes to. She stood in any running water the road crossed, to the knee, at dusk and dawn, and nowhere else, and did not shift in anyone's sight.
This is an attempt. Who goes: three, of whom two are mortal, with a carrier family of about a dozen that has agreed to have them. What they ask: where the salt road runs toward the south town of the stone towns, what the carriers pay at each stage, and whether the town could be a place the road comes to. Where the road runs beyond the ford, what is paid at each stage, who is met, what is said there and whether anything comes against the party are the Keeper's. I give no distance beyond the ford. I write nothing of what was found. See the report.
Written both ways, briefly:
- If they walked out eight days with the family, turned at dusk of the eighth, and came back by the same road as counted, they were at the ford at dusk of the twenty eighth and home on the Brook Path on the first day of month six, the ferry child with them, having poled the ford ferry sixteen days and been told by the ford keepers she was quick. The cord walker's knots went to the Reckoner before the town. The Elder counted them aloud from the landing post and twice. The ferry keepers said, as they say of any river and said now of a road, that you learn what a way is by going along it. The Wary said, as every year, that a guest takes the town's keepers where no one comes home, and were told the keepers came home.
- If they did not, because the family would not have them, or the road held them, or a stage asked what they could not pay, or they were late or hurt or held, the town waited without word past the days counted. If the family would not have them at the ford, they waited at the ford up to the fifth day for another and came home by the path, and the Wary said it was a thing the town should have known. If they were late, the town forgave it with a grumble. If they were held or hurt, on the third day of month six the Elder sent the second barge down the brook with three poles and a runner ahead by the path to the ford keepers, and the harvest opened without those hands. If the ferry keeper or the cord walker was lost, that is a death, population one less, two if both, and the council would not hear of the road again and the talk was of the ford alone, and the Reckoner kept a cord of it. If the family took a toll the party paid in what the council sent and no more, no debt was taken in the town's name.
Population differs by two at most.

**A question left with the Warden (month five, while she was away).** The morning after the party went down, a pasture hand left a question with the Seat Warden for the guest, from the head herd keeper: whether the offer of one more pair of feet stood for the dark of the moon in the first month of the harvest, since the herd had strayed in the first month of an autumn with every hand at the reaping. The Warden said she would put it in the cord for when the guest was home, and that it was not hers to answer. It met silence, since the guest was away, and stayed pending.

**The return (the first day of month six, if the party was back).** They walked home by the Brook Path, two days from the ford, the barge left at the ford for the ford keepers' use. She held no one's hands on the way. The party came to the gate house in the evening. On the second evening she asked the Warden for the question left with her, and said yes, that she would come to the pasture at the dark of the moon in the first month of the harvest, and the Warden sent word up the hill.

**The counts (month six).** The Reckoner's cord and hers agreed: a little over two thousand, about 355 hearths, births to midsummer as in other years.

**The measure at the low water and the autumn mark (month six).** The low water came when it does. The mill and weir hands poled the bed behind each weir again, with the Elder on the bank and the cord walker knotting, and the figures stood within a finger of the flood's fall figures at every weir, as the head of the weir hands had said they would: the oldest along the gap, eight hands; to either side nine; the second seven and a half; the third five and a half; the newest three and a half; the small weirs as before. The Reckoner wrote them beside the season. The ferry keepers cut the autumn low-water mark at the gate stone with the gap open, and she stood with them at it and read it, as the council had set, and said she would bring it to the sitting. The ferry keepers said the gap had held its hand through the summer and the deep water had not come back. The head of the weir hands said his pole stayed on its peg and that the ferry keepers might remind him next year.

**Harvest (months six and seven).** Ordinary weather, ordinary harvest. Every hand went to the barley and millet, the beans and flax. She reaped with the hamlet women for some days.

## Autumn

**No alarms (month seven).** After the first autumn rains the wells ran clear and the channel water ran as it runs. Nothing was told the council. No stock strayed beyond the ordinary.

**The dimmed night (month seven).** One night. She was in the town, if the party was home as counted. The Reckoner cut the third knot of the year and the third mark: in the town. The star had not dimmed in the summer, and the Wary said so, that it did not dim while she was away. The ferry keepers said she was away about twenty four nights of the year and the star came three times in all, which is no sort of count, and that the Reckoner had said as much. The Reckoner said he did not think it was nothing and did not think it was something.

**The second night on the pasture (month seven, the dark of the moon).** She went up at dusk, as in spring. The head herd keeper and the three hands counted the herd beside her, and she counted aloud after them, and the counts agreed to the head at dusk and at dawn. She walked the bounds through the night. Nothing strayed and nothing was seen to come. The head herd keeper said the harvest was the month the herd had been lost and that it had been counted by four pairs of eyes and one pair that did not shut, and said it to the cord and not to her. The Warden said the cord back.

**The one sitting (month eight, after the harvest).** The council sat for her in one sitting, as she had asked, and she asked nothing but that they hear it. She told what the keepers had measured at the gate stone and at the ford stone across the summer; what the weir hands had measured, at the flood's fall and at the low water, with the gap open; what the carriers and those on the salt road had said; and what she had seen. She gave her counsel as counsel: whether the town wants the carriers' road to come back to it, whether a mark to read by, a second ferry or a letting go of the ford is worth what it costs; and said the council might answer no. She named no one to do any of it, and said she would ask the Ferry Elder, the Reckoner or the keepers the council named, and not before. She gave the council and the Reckoner the count of the dimmed nights on the notched cord with where she stood each time: three this year, all in the town if the party was home, and sixteen across the three years of marks. The Reckoner said he kept the same: fourteen in the town and two away across the three years, and twenty four knots across the five. What she said of the road and what the water told her is the god's words and the Keeper's, not mine. The Seat Warden said back the cord of her sayings at the close. The council heard to the end, as the code requires, and talked three evenings, as it does.
- The weirs. The council took the figures as its own. It kept the gap open through the autumn dark moon's run and through the winter, so that the town would have a whole year of the gap's catch beside the three before, and said it would speak again after the next flood. It opened no second gap. The head of the oldest kin house said his house would carry the autumn run's loss and that he would not be thanked for it. The weir hands counted the run at all four great weirs as in other years: the oldest weir's autumn take was less than in other years by about a fifth by the hands' reckoning, the second weir's a little more, and the hands did not say it proved anything either way. The loss to the oldest weir's households for the year, with the gap open through all of it, was about one part in four of that weir's year, about one part in twenty of the town's eel, which the other houses made good by cord through the winter, a small share of dried eel and barley, a little larger than last year's.
- The road. Written both ways, briefly, for what the council took of her telling. If the party came home with the road's stages, what the carriers pay and a way for the road to come to the town, the council talks of a market at the ford or the town's own salt party in spring, the ferry keepers are for it, the Wary say the town is spending its keepers on a stranger's errand, and talk of the ford grows small. If the party came home with little, or the road would not come to the town, the council holds to the marks, the small weirs, the gap and the thaw, and the ferry keepers say a ford crossed less is a ford that costs less, and the Elder says he will not be told that by anyone with sound knees. If the party was lost in part or whole, the council will not hear of the road, and the talk is of the ford alone. The Keeper's file settles which.

**Salt and the store (month eight).** The council set aside again about one part in twenty of the barley and millet for the flood store at the water gate, turning the older grain to the table, and smoked fish and eel with little salt. The carriers came less often again and asked more. The council took the salt offered at the price asked and rationed it by house cord. It said again it would not set salt aside, since salt is what the town buys.

## Winter

**The ice (months ten to twelve).** The ice came as it does and the channels slept. She sat at the council through the winter, told stories at the fire, learned the new children's names, about 75 born in the year, and sat by the eldest midwife when she was asked, and was not asked for a child or a mother in a fever. She counted again with the Reckoner at the turn of the year, with the second cord, and the low-water cord's fourth mark of the year was cut through the ice at the gate stone by the ferry keepers, the ford keepers sending theirs up the brook path by a runner after the ice held; sixteen marks each at the two stones now. She did not listen in the ice and did not go to the water. The cup stood at the low seat each morning, filled by the Warden, and she did not drink it. The star did not dim in the winter, and the midwives said it was the first winter in three it had not, and that a labor that slows ends one way or the other.

**The run of fish (month eleven, in the last of the ice).** In the eleventh month a great run of fish came up the river and stood at the weirs for four days, more than the oldest weir hands have seen. The water at the weirs runs too fast to freeze and the fish stood in it, thickest at the opened gap in the oldest weir, so thick that children took them by hand. Nobody did anything to bring them. The town came down to the weirs with baskets, the ferry child and the small boy among the first, and the oldest hands stood at the gap with their mouths open and said so. She came down with the town and stood on the bank with the Ferry Elder and did not go to the water. The town ate fresh for the four days, froze what it could in baskets in the cold of the yards, smoked what the smokehouses would take and salted a little, since there was no salt to speak of. About two parts in three of the take kept, by the Reckoner's count of baskets; the rest went to the dogs, the pigs and the geese. It was the largest fish store any weir hand remembered, and a part of it will not last the thaw without salt.
A quarrel followed, kindly. The oldest weir's households said the run was theirs, since it stood at their gap; the houses who had made good the gap's loss by cord said it was theirs to be repaid by. The Reckoner sat it. He said a fish taken by hand is the hand's and the hand's house's, that the cord of the gap's loss was a cord of eel and the run was fish, and that the cord did not count one against the other. The head of the oldest kin house said that was a clean ruling and that he would not stand on it: the oldest kin house's gap had cost the town by cord and the run had come to the gap, and he would send back shares of the fish by cord to the houses that had made good. He did, the next day. The Fond said it was the third fair thing. The weir hands said the ferry keepers would be saying the fish had come because the weirs were wrong, and the ferry keepers said they were not saying it, they were only waiting for it to be said. A weir hand's child asked the guest, at the weir, whether she had sent the fish. Her answer is not in my files.

**The weavers and the pots.** Spinning, weaving, pottery and long stories as in other winters. No raids, no strangers, no quarrel beyond the usual at the fire. The weir hands and the ferry keepers quarrelled kindly through the dark months about the fish, the gap and the pole, and the head of the weir hands said that his pole was still on its peg.

**The turn (end of month twelve).** In the early thaw she went up the stream path before first light to the spring head, alone, as she had gone the four years before. The Warden noticed the guest bench empty and filled the cup anyway. The people made ready for the spring flood, which will say what the gap does a second time, and the choosing of the next Speaker, which falls on the first days of Year 6.

## What the people did, in short

They kept the cords: the flood marks on the gate stone, nine now, the low-water cord at the gate stone and at the ford, sixteen marks each by the turn, and the cord of the weir measures, now at the flood's fall and the low water. They heard a guest answer three questions that had stood a year, and a head of a kin house say a thing was plain. They found the bed behind the opened weir a hand lower along the gap and every other weir fuller, kept the gap open another year on the head of the oldest kin house's word, and opened no second. They gave a guest leave for a going on the carriers' road, eight days out and the turn at dusk of the eighth, never later, and sent two of their own with her and a girl of thirteen as far as the ford. They took a guest's one more pair of feet on the pasture twice and lost nothing. They saw the star dim three nights, as few as in the first year, and cut a mark by every knot for where she stood. They took fish by hand at the gap in the last of the ice, more than anyone remembers, and did not know why. No one took her for a god. No office holder acts for her in her absence.
