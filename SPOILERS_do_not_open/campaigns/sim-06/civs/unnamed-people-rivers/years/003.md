# Year 3: unnamed people of god-rivers

Months are counted from the opening of the year. Spring is months one to three, summer four to six, autumn seven to nine, winter ten to twelve. Everything below is what the people did and met. What a power did is marked and left for the Keeper (see 003-report.md). Where the year turns on an uncertain outcome, it is written both ways, briefly. The Keeper's line on Year 2 is taken as what happened: about 35 goats and 3 cattle came home of about 60 and 5, so about 25 goats and 2 cattle were lost; nothing came against the short listening, and she slept about two hours; both whom she warmed recovered; the council's course on the ford was a mark to read by.

## Spring

**The thaw and the guest's return (first days after the thaw).** The ice went out of the channels as it does. Before the flood peaked a weir hand at dawn work saw the woman come down the stream path from the hills, barefoot, stooped, in the grey wrap and hemp cord, as she had gone up at the turn of the year. He called the gate house. The Seat Warden had filled the cup at the low seat that morning, as every morning of the winter, and left it where it stood, and showed the guest to the guest bench. She was fed barley porridge and dried eel at the Warden's hearth, unsalted, and asked nothing until she had eaten. The town knew where she had been, as the year before. The weir hand said only that she came from the stream side.

**The children's fire (first evening after).** She sat with the children at the fire in the yard of the gate house, about thirty of them, the ferry child, eleven now and taller than the pole, at the front. The ferry child asked her again what she had asked in the summer: whether she could go with her to the ford. The guest said that the Ferry Elder had said no, that the Elder was right to keep a child out of the larger water, that she would tell the child at the fire what she saw and what the keepers measured, and that she would ask again when the Elder said. The child sulked, and said that was what a grown person says. Then the child asked, as she had asked it before and the town had repeated it all winter, whether she was a spring's. The guest said she had woken at a spring, that she did not know whether she was any spring's, and that this was all she knew. A small boy said a spring cannot walk. The others laughed, and the ferry child did not. It pleased no one and was kept. The guest asked the Seat Warden, who had come to the gate of the yard, to keep the cord of it as she kept the rest. The Warden said she would, as she kept the rest, and went in to fill the lamp.

**The flood and the marks (month one).** The spring flood came ordinary in height and went down. After it the deep water lay a little farther from the ford stones again, on the side away from the weirs: the sixth flood running. The ferry keepers said so at the water gate on the first day the stones showed, and cut this year's mark on the gate stone, the seventh on it. The runner from the ford keepers came up the brook path with their spring low-water mark, and it was cut at the ford stone and at the gate stone as the council had set. Salt was out; the thaw took the last. The council did not argue it.

**The Ferry Elder reads the marks with her (month one, the day after the stones showed).** At her asking the Ferry Elder stood with her and the Reckoner at the gate stone and read the flood marks and the low-water cord, and said what he had seen. He said the deep water had moved again, by the stones and by the cord, a little, in the same direction as before, and that it was not the ford alone: the weir hands say it left them whether or not they stood there, and he had never been able to disprove it. The Reckoner said the cord said the same as the Elder, and said it in fewer words.

**The star, once, in the flood month (month one).** On a clear night the blue deepened for about an hour and the star stood dim, and then it was as before. The weir hands on night watch and the ferry keepers saw it, and anyone who woke. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut a knot on his cord of it, and cut beside it a mark he had not cut before, for the month, and said aloud that his saying that it had never fallen in a flood month was now a thing he had said. It had fallen in one. He said that he would rather be shown wrong by a cord than right by a habit, and that this was not a thing he said often.

**The choosing (sixth day after the flood went down).** The council chose its Speaker, by long talk until no one stood against it: the head of a younger kin house, a man of about 46, loud, quick to laugh and slow to decide, who had sat the council six years and was said to hear a thing three times before he spoke on it. The Speaker of Year 2 went back to her seat. He is not named in this record (see people/the-speaker.md).

**The sitting (about the tenth day after the flood went down).** The council sat. The guest, at the guest bench, said what she asked, and that she asked and did not press. She asked that the flood marks and the low-water cord be kept as they were kept. She asked the Speaker whether the council had settled what to do about the ford since the thaw. She asked the herd keepers, through the Seat Warden and the Ferry Elder who carried it, that when stock strayed into the reed they send to the water gate, and she would search if she were sent for and not otherwise.
- The Speaker said the council had not settled it, and that he would say so plainly rather than have it said for him. The weirs' talk, a year old, was now sat for two evenings. The council ended where it ends: it would set a second small fish weir this spring, in a side channel on the far side where the deep water now lay, twenty worker-days of work, to try that position as it had tried the first; it would not move the great weirs before the autumn sitting and the keepers' word. The head of the oldest kin house said nothing against it. A weir hand said a second small weir was a small answer to a large water, and was told that a small answer was the only kind the council had in its hand this season.
- The head of the herd keepers, a hill pasture woman of about fifty, said the loss had been about twenty-five goats and two cattle, not thirty and two. She said it without heat and the Reckoner's cord said the same. She said the pasture hands would now count at dawn as well as at dusk through the harvest, would keep three hands on the pasture and not two and a boy, and would send to the water gate for the ferry keepers' boats after looking an hour, since the water gate was for the boats and the dogs, and the guest was welcome to come if she liked. The Seat Warden said it back to her, so it was in the cord.
- The Ferry Elder asked what he was to say to the keepers about the river. She said nothing yet; she would bring it to the council in summer, with a going she wished to ask leave for.

**Planting and the second small weir (months two and three).** Planting went as it does, terraces by the springs and the drained flats. Few carriers came; a handful of salt carriers crossed. The council took the salt offered at the price asked and rationed it by house cord. The second small fish weir was set in the third month by six weir hands in about four days, in the side channel the council had named. The ferry child carried messages and sulked, and was not asked whether she minded.

## Summer

**The council's leave (month four, after the planting).** She asked the council, in a sitting, for leave for a going. She said what she asked: that two ferry keepers and the Reckoner's cord walker walk with her, by barge down the brook to the ford and then down the larger river some days and back, to see where the carriers cross now, and to look for why the deep water moves. She said how long: some days down, three or four, and the days back, and that she would be home before the autumn low water. She said that she asked, and that the council might answer no.
- The head of the oldest kin house stood against it. He said the larger river was not the town's, that no one of the town had been more than a day below the ford, and that a guest who took the town's keepers where no one came home was owed a hard question. He asked what she sought below the ford. She said why the deep water moves, and where the carriers cross now. He said that was two things, and she said it was.
- The Ferry Elder said what every ferry keeper knows of any river: a barge goes down about thirty miles in a day and comes up about twelve, so every day run down costs two and a half days coming home. Three days down was eight back. Four was ten. He said three, and not a day more, and that the turn should be made at dusk of the third day wherever they lay, and not decided on the water by anyone who was tired and curious. The older ferry keeper, asked, said three. The Reckoner said his cord walker would knot a count of every landing and hearth they passed, and bring it, and the town would have a count of its own and not rest on hers.
- The council talked three evenings. The head of the oldest kin house did not stand against it by the third. The council gave its go on the condition that was the Elder's: three days down and the turn at dusk of the third, whatever was met; home before the harvest was well opened; and that the cord walker brought the knots home before anything else was told.
- The Ferry Elder named the two ferry keepers: the older woman of the ford landing, about 41, who had gone last year, and the younger man of about 26 who cuts the marks cleanest, who had gone last year. The Reckoner named his cord walker, about 23. The Elder stayed home with his knee. The ferry keepers at the ford landing, asked by a runner, said the town might have the barge left with them last summer, and would keep the ferry meanwhile.

**The pair of dimmed nights, first (month five, before they left).** A clear night. The blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, then as before. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut a knot, and beside it a mark for where she stood: in the town.

**The setting out (about the middle of month five).** The party of four went down to the brook landing at dawn with the low-water cord, a bag of the town's cords for the ford keepers, and food for the days counted and a few over. The ferry child was found asleep under the reed mat at the stern of the barge before it cast off, with a day's bread in her shirt. The Ferry Elder, leaning on the landing post, took her by the ear and walked her up the bank. The guest said nothing that is in this record. The child carried the Reckoner's cord messages every day of the summer and was spoken to by her mother in a way the whole landing heard. The barge went down the brook one day and made the ford at dusk.

**At the ford (the first days).** The party stayed two days. The ford keepers showed the stones, the ferry's first post and the marks, and the summer low-water mark was cut at the ford stone with the cord walker standing by, so that the low-water cord had its next knot from both ends. The ford keepers gave the town's barge back for the going, and kept the ferry. A ford keeper, the one who had asked last year what she was looking for in the river, asked it again at the ford fire. She said that she was looking for why the deep water moves, and that she was also looking, if there were any, for another like herself, and that she had found none. She said that she did not know what she was looking for in the sense of why she came. She said nothing of any working. The ford keepers were quiet, and then one of them said that that was a plain answer and a strange one. A party of carriers crossed on the second day, and ordinary travelers. She asked them what they had seen of the larger river and where they go when they cross less. They said what the town had been told: that others go a southern way. They gave no place, no distance and no reason, and she was told none. She stood in the larger river to the knee at dusk on the second day, and at dawn on the third, facing upstream, her hands loose, in the woman's shape, as she had stood last year. The ford keepers have called her Knee Deep since, and said so again.

**The going down the larger river (about month five, three days down, the turn, eight back).** The barge cast off from the ford in the dawn of the fourth day of the going. The two ferry keepers poled and rowed, the cord walker kept the knots, and the guest took the sweep as any would, and stopped at each landing and hearth the river gave, as she had asked, and asked news and a count and where the carriers cross now. She stood in the larger river to the knee each evening and each dawn, and nowhere else, and did not shift in anyone's sight. They were to turn at dusk of the third day wherever they lay, and come up the river eight days, and be at the ford about the twenty eighth of the month, as the Elder had counted at the town landing.
This is an attempt: who goes is four, what they ask is where the carriers cross and why the deep water moves, and what lies below the ford, who is met there, what the river tells her and whether anything comes against her listening are the Keeper's. See the report. I write nothing of what was found.
Written both ways, briefly:
- If they went down three days, turned, and came up as the keepers counted, they were at the ford about the twenty eighth of month five and home in the first days of month six, the Elder having counted the days aloud at the gate: three down, eight up, the ford, two on the path. The town asked what they had seen, and the ferry keepers said, as they say of any river, that you learn what a river is by going up it. What lay below and who was met and what knots the cord walker cut is told as the Keeper gives it, and the cord walker's knots are the Reckoner's before the town's. The ferry keepers' pride grows, and the Elder grumbles that the larger river is not the town's and asks to hear it twice.
- If they did not, because the river held them, or the barge was lost or harmed, or someone came against them, or they were late, the town waited without word past the days counted. The Elder sent runners down the Brook Path to the ford and the town's own barge after them with three poles, and the harvest opened without those hands. The Wary said a guest takes keepers where no one comes home. If a ferry keeper or the cord walker was lost, that is a death, population one less, and the council shut the larger river below the ford to its own keepers and the Reckoner kept a cord of it. If all came home late, the town forgave it with a grumble.
Population differs by one at most.

**The pair of dimmed nights, second (month five, while she was on the water).** A clear night. The town saw it from the gate and the weirs, the blue deepening for about an hour and the star standing dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, then as before. She was away from the town. The Reckoner cut a knot, and beside it a mark: away. The Wary had said, the year before, that it dimmed oftener in the years she stayed. Two ferry keepers on the first barge said at dawn that it had dimmed in her absence, and that this proved nothing, as the Wary had said of theirs. If the party was on the water, they saw it from the river and the cord walker cut a knot of his own, and told it when he came.

**The listening at the ford (the last days of month five, if the party was back).** At the ford, at the low water, she asked the two ferry keepers and the cord walker to sit by her while she knelt in the running water of the larger river from the ford stones and listened about an hour and no more, and to keep a fire, a mat and the dogs and the cold off through the sleep that followed, and to wake the council's runner if anything came against her, though no one could wake her. The ford keepers kept the ferry and sent word to the crossing that the ferry was closed for the evening. A few dozen of the ford's people watched from the bank, and some travelers. What was seen: she knelt with her hands in the current, went still, did not answer when the older ferry keeper spoke to her, and after about an hour lay down at the edge and slept. The three kept the fire and the mat. She slept about two hours, as the Keeper had ruled of the last. When she woke she asked for water, and the older ferry keeper had it from the ford's own jug. What she heard is not told to the people.
This is an attempt too. The watch over the sleeper is the Keeper's to roll, and the circumstances are in the report.
Written both ways, briefly: if nothing came against her, nothing was seen but the watchers and the fire, and the ford keepers said the larger river's listening was as easy as the gate's; if a dog or a stranger or worse came, the older ferry keeper sounded the ferry horn and the ford drove it off, or the ford learned more than she meant.

**The return (the first days of month six, if they were back).** They walked home by the Brook Path, two days, the barge left at the ford for the ford keepers' use. She held no one's hands on the way. The party came to the gate house in the evening, and the Ferry Elder counted them aloud from the landing post and was seen to count them twice.

**The counts (month six).** The Reckoner's cord and hers agreed: a little over two thousand, about 345 hearths. Births to midsummer as in other years.

**Harvest (months six and seven).** Ordinary weather, ordinary harvest. Every hand went to the barley and millet, the beans and flax. The ferry keepers cut the autumn low-water mark with the others at the gate stone. The pasture hands counted at dawn and at dusk and lost nothing. The woman reaped for some days with the hamlet women.

## Autumn

**The pair of dimmed nights (month seven).** Two nights, as the others, in the town. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut two knots and two marks: in the town, in the town. The Wary said again that it dimmed oftener in the years she stayed. The ferry keepers said that one of the six had fallen when she was on the river, and that if she were the cause it should not have been the town that saw it. The Wary said that a thing could be in two places. The ferry keepers said that was exactly what they had said.

**Two small alarms, and none (month seven).** After the first autumn rains the upper well ran cloudy again; the keepers drew it and found the rain wash, and it cleared in three days. The weir hands said the channel water ran muddy behind the great weirs after the same rains, and told the council at once, as asked. Nothing came of either. No stock strayed this year beyond the ordinary: no herd was missed.

**The one sitting (month eight, after the harvest).** The council sat for her in one sitting, as she had asked, and she asked nothing but that they hear it. At the autumn low water she had stood with the ferry keepers at the mark the council set and read it with them. She told the council what the keepers measured at the gate stone and at the ford stone across the summer, what the carriers said at the ford, what she saw, and what the water told her at the ford, and she gave her counsel as counsel and said the council might answer no. She named no one to do any of it, and she asked the Ferry Elder, the Reckoner or the keepers the council named, and not before. She told the council and the Reckoner that a notched cord is kept for the dimmed nights of the star, with where she stood each time. The Reckoner said he kept one already, and that the knots were his own, and that he had added the marks for where she stood since the flood month, because the Wary said she brought it and the cord should settle it or not, whichever it was. She said that was the cord's business. The Seat Warden said back the cord of her sayings at the close. The council heard to the end, as the code requires, and talked three evenings, as it does. What the counsel was and what the water told her is in the god's file and for the Keeper to set beside this; I write only the council's way of meeting it. The council did not decide the large things in the one season. The winter marks and the next flood will say more; it will answer at the thaw before the flood; and the smallest thing counselled it would do now.
Written both ways, briefly: if the party went and came back with news of where the carriers cross and a way to it, the council talks of sending its own salt party the new way in spring, and the Wary say the town is spending its keepers on a stranger's errand; if the party brought back little, the council holds to the marks, the small weirs and the thaw, and the talk of the great weirs waits for the flood; if the party was lost in part or whole, the council will not hear of the larger river below the ford and the talk is of the ford alone. If what the water told her was that the deep water would not return, the talk is harder and longer; if it was that the bed was quiet and the water would come back, the council holds easier. The Keeper's file settles which.

**The dimmed night (month eight).** One night, as the others, in the town. She was in the town. The Reckoner cut the sixth knot and the fifth mark in the town.

**Salt and the store (month eight).** The council set aside again about one part in twenty of the barley and millet for the flood store at the water gate, turning the older grain to the table, and smoked fish and eel with little salt. Salt was thin again. It said again that it would not set salt aside, since salt is what the town buys.

## Winter

**The ice (months ten to twelve).** The ice came as it does and the channels slept. She sat at the council through the winter, told stories at the fire, and learned the new children's names, about 75 born in the year. She counted again with the Reckoner at the turn of the year, with the second cord, and the low-water cord's fourth mark of the year was cut through the ice at the gate stone by the ferry keepers, the ford keepers sending theirs up the brook path by a runner after the ice held; eight marks each at the two stones now. She did not listen in the ice, and she did not go to the water.
At the children's fire in the deep of the winter she told them what she had seen and what the keepers measured, as she had said she would. What she told of the river below the ford is as the Keeper gives it. The ferry child asked again to go with her to the ford, and was told to ask the Ferry Elder, and asked him in front of the gate house. The Elder said, when she was twelve and had a pole of her own. The child said that was a year, and he said it was.

**The weavers and the pots.** Spinning, weaving, pottery and long stories as in other winters. No raids, no strangers, no quarrel beyond the usual at the fire. One quarrel over a boat between two house heads was sat by the Reckoner.

**The turn (end of month twelve).** In the early thaw she went up the stream path before first light to the spring head, alone, as she had gone the two years before. The Warden noticed the guest bench empty, and filled the cup anyway. The people made ready for the spring flood and the choosing of the next Speaker, which falls on the first days of Year 4.

## What the people did, in short

They kept the cords: the flood marks on the gate stone, seven now, and the low-water cord at the gate stone and at the ford, eight marks each by the turn. They read the marks with her in the flood month and heard that the deep water moved again. They chose a Speaker who hears a thing three times. They set a second small weir where the deep water now lies, and kept the great weirs where they were. They set a count on the pasture at dawn and dusk. They gave a guest leave for a going down the larger river, three days down and the turn at dusk of the third, and sent three of their own with her. They saw the star dim six nights, the first in a flood month, the second of the pair in the fifth while she was on the water, and cut a mark by every knot for where she stood. No one took her for a god. No office holder acts for her in her absence.
