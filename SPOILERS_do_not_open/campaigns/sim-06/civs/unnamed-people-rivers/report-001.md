# Report to the Keeper: unnamed people of god-rivers, Year 1

## How the god was met
Spring, first morning. The ferry child (a girl of nine) saw her first at the water gate landing and called the Seat Warden. The Seat Warden, whose gate house hearth was the first hearth, offered her the cup from the low seat as a courtesy to one who came in from the water's side, fed her and asked nothing until she had eaten. She drank. The Warden took her word as a guest's.
Spring, about the ninth day. The Speaker (chosen on the sixth day after the flood) gave her the council's hearing. The council heard her to the end, as the code requires. The head of the oldest kin house stood against her counting. The Reckoner was uneasy. The council ruled that she may stay as a guest at the guest bench beside the low seat (not the low seat), may count, and that the Reckoner walks with her with a second cord. The old house head stood down on that condition. No oath either way. None took her for a god.
Spring, month two. The Seat Warden agreed to keep the cord of what she says and does and to say it back, as she would for any guest, and said she would not say whether the cup waited for her before the council did.
Spring and summer. The ferry keepers took to her first, for her questions about the water. The eldest midwife saw her cold hands and told the council she has a gift like her own with a worse cost. The Ferry Elder sent two ferry keepers with her to the hamlets. He took her word on the marks and the store as counsel and took her for a woman with a gift. The Reckoner kept the second cord and by winter said their counts never differed. The well keepers took her word on the well rule as the code and told her and the council at once when the lower well turned.
Autumn, month nine. The Seat Warden, a well keeper and a ferry keeper sat the watch at the gate. Winter. She sat at the council. The Wary grew quiet, the Fond fond.
Whether each took her word as the god's helper: none did. No office holder has taken her for the one awaited or for a god. The Seat Warden holds the nearest to it, saying only that the cup has not been empty of her. The Keeper rules whether that makes the Warden a helper under the record's rule. I have written her as not yet one.

## What was done in the god's name
Nothing was done in her name. Done at her asking, by choice and as the people's own: the ferry keepers cut flood marks on the gate stone (this year's and the earlier four, by the old ferrymen's recital) and the council kept them as its own. The council set aside about one part in twenty of the harvest and some smoked fish and eel as a flood store. The well keepers closed and relined the lower well and told her. The Warden kept the cord of her sayings and said it back at two sittings, and sat the watch at the gate. A small fish weir was set in a side channel by the council on its own year-long talk; not at her asking.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper
1. The watch over the sleeping woman, autumn month nine. Who tries: the Seat Warden, a well keeper and a ferry keeper with a horn. What: to keep her from the cold and the dogs, wake the council if anything came against her. What threatens: dogs, the cold, a hostile house head, anything that hunts a sleeping god. Circumstances: she lay at the water's edge below the gate, in a reed mat shelter with a fire, and the town gathered and watched. I wrote it as a stray dog driven off and nothing else. If something came against her, the keepers sound the horn, the town sees something it cannot name, and the council learns more than she meant it to. If the watch had failed, she was helpless for about a day and a night.
2. Warm hands (see below). It is a power and not a contest, but the course of the three sick ones turns on it.
Nothing else is contested: no fight, no search, no persuasion of another people, no craft beyond what my people know.

## What turns on a power
1. The listening at the spring head before first light, day one. Unseen. What she learned, if anything, and whether the pull was felt, is for the Keeper.
2. The listening at the water gate, autumn. Seen: she went still, then lay down and slept for about a day and a night, and was not woken by the Warden's calling or the ferry keeper's shaking. Seen: she did not answer when spoken to. I wrote that the calling and shaking failed, as the god's file says no one can wake her; if the Keeper rules otherwise, the town saw something different. What she heard is for the Keeper.
3. Warm hands: a child of the second hamlet with a fever after a channel pull, a fevered woman in another hamlet, a man in the town with a reed knife cut gone bad. Seen: her hands went cold and white while she held each, and she warmed them after and said little. What it did to each of the three is for the Keeper. I wrote the year both ways in the year file: if each recovered, the hamlets say she has the midwives' touch in a larger measure; if not, they say she gave what she could and are kind anyway. Population differs by at most two or three either way.
4. The unsigned reading: the town reads her works as the water's or the midwives' craft and she does not claim them. Nothing in my record contradicts it.
5. The three nights the star dimmed are the Keeper's, not mine. The people connected the first two to her coming. They are wrong in that and I know no cause.
6. The god's cost on its hands after warm hands: seen only as cold hands. The Keeper rules if there is anything more.

## What touches anyone else
Salt carriers: crossed fewer times than last year, as the release says. I wrote no trade with them beyond the usual, and nothing sent. Word of a barefoot counting woman may travel with them upriver by ordinary gossip; I wrote none of that, and the Keeper may if it wishes.
Boat bands: traded at dusk as before. The barge trip to the hamlets in summer may have been seen by a band; I wrote no meeting. Twice they went for some days at the lowest tides.
Namers: none came.
Parties sent out by my people: none. Ferry keepers went to the hamlets by barge and on foot along the banks, in summer; I give no distance or time, the Keeper has the map. The ford is two days on foot by the path from the town, by my record; she did not go to it this year.
One conflict for the Keeper: her decisions file says the town lies two miles down the stream from the spring head where she woke. My record says the town's spring is inside or hard by the wall and gives no distance to any spring up the stream. I wrote only that she came down the stream path from the hills above the town. The Keeper settles the distance.

## Asked of the god
The ferry child, first day: "What are you called?" Answered in front of her: the water has called her the Lady of the Ford, or only the river, and they may call her what they like.
The Seat Warden, first day, offering the cup: "Will you drink?" Answered: she drank.
The Speaker at the hearing: "What do you want among us?" Answered, as her decisions give: to stay a while, a seat at the edge of the talk, no rite, no shrine, no obedience.
The head of the oldest kin house: "Who do you count for?" Answered: she said she counts aloud, so no one thinks it done in secret, and that the Reckoner may keep the second cord.
The Ferry Elder: "Where does the water go?" Answered in part: she said she was asking him the same thing and would learn it with the keepers.
The hamlet mother: "Will you hold her?" Answered: yes, she held the child's hands.
The Seat Warden, autumn, on waking: no question put.
Winter, the children: "Where do you come from?" Answered: the water's side. They asked again and she told them a story of water instead.
Any question I wrote beyond what her decisions file gives is not written here, the god's words being the DM's.

## Names said aloud
The Lady of the Ford, or only the river: said by her, first day to the ferry child and the Seat Warden, and in the council hearing to the Speaker.
The Counting Woman: said by the ferry child in winter in front of the other children, and spread. The child named her for her counts.
Nessa Ferry: said by the Seat Warden to the guest on the first day when asked about the cup, in the old telling (already in the record). The three tellings of why she set it were repeated, and the Warden said she did not choose among them.
Names of the four reed hamlets: each hamlet gave its name aloud when asked. I have not set them down because my record gives none.
No name for the people or their home was said. No office holder was named.
