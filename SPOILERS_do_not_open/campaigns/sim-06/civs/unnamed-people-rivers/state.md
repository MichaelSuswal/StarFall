# Unnamed people of god-rivers: state at the end of Year 4

Written by the civ agent at the close of Year 4, pass one. Where a number turns on an uncertain outcome or on what a power did, a range is given.

population: about 2,060 (about 1,900 in the walled town, about 160 in four reed hamlets), in about 352 hearths. Why: about 75 births and about 60 deaths in an ordinary year, a net rise of about 15 on the Year 3 figure of about 2,045. Deaths were the old and infants as in other winters, and the usual one or two to the channels. On the journey down the larger river the number may be lower by one (a ferry keeper or the cord walker lost to the river) and by up to three if someone came against the party; the girl of about four who was held in winter, if she did not recover, is one fewer; range about 2,055 to 2,060.

stores: barley, millet, beans, flax, onions and dried eel and fish, enough to the next harvest in an ordinary flood. The flood store at the water gate stands at about one part in twenty of the barley and millet, the older grain turned to the table and new put by, with smoked fish and eel. Salt: none in store; the thaw took the last; what the carriers brought was little and dearer and is rationed by house cord. Why: ordinary harvest; the council's habit; the carriers crossed fewer times again. The oldest weir's households carry a loss of about one part in six of that weir's year, about one part in thirty of the town's eel, made good through the winter by the other houses by cord, a small share of dried eel and barley. Livestock: the herd is nearly back to its count before the Year 2 loss, made good by two springs of kids and calves; the winter's milk and fat were less short. The pasture keeps three hands, counts at dawn and dusk through the harvest; nothing strayed.

treasure: little: cords, boats, a few bronze tools. New: the eighth flood mark on the gate stone; four more low-water marks at the gate stone and at the ford stone, twelve each in all; the Reckoner's cord of the weir measures beside the marks (the bed behind the four great weirs by age, nine, seven, five and three hands, and behind the two small fish weirs, one hand and a finger); the Reckoner's cord of the dimmed nights, seven knots this year with a mark for where she stood at each, thirteen across the two years of marks; the cord walker's knots of the landings and hearths below the ford, if the party was home; a pole cut for the ferry child; a gap, a third of the width, in the oldest weir, about thirty worker-days, which is a loss of work and not a gain. The two small fish weirs stand. A bundle of linen, six pots, two bronze knives and dried eel went out as gifts for hearths below. Why: the council's own keeping of the god's asking; one small unsetting.

trade: reed work, eels, linen and pots out; salt in at the ford, less than last year and dearer. The boat bands traded at dusk as before. The carriers who came said little. The town's own going down the larger river was to look for where the salt joins it; what it found is the Keeper's. Why: the carriers come less each year and the town cannot make them come.

strength: a standing watch of about 30 and a call-up of about 300, unchanged. Earth wall, palisade and ditch repaired after the flood. Why: no fighting, no new arms.

mood: curious, steady, a little worried. The ford worry is the same, now with eight flood marks, twelve low-water marks at each stone and a cord of the weirs; the salt worry is the same and a little sharper; the star dimmed seven nights, three of them in the winter, which worries the weirs and the midwives more than the ford. The weir hands are sore at the gap and say so kindly, the ferry keepers are glad of it, the head of the oldest kin house is spoken of more fairly than he has been. If the party came home as counted, the ferry keepers are proud, the ferry child has been to the ford, and the Elder grumbles that the larger river is not the town's; if not, the town is quiet and sore and the Wary say they were right. They are easy about the guest, who is a guest, and many say she is the town's own.

factions: the Wary (the head of the oldest kin house, some of the weir hands, and the younger of his house) are split: the head of the oldest kin house gave his house's weir to the cord and says nothing against it, the young Wary say he gave it to a guest's guess; they say the star dims oftener in the years she stays. The Fond (the midwives, the ferry keepers, the ford keepers, the children, the head herd keeper in her way) are fonder, the ferry keepers more for the going and the gap. The Counters (the Reckoner and his cord walkers, with the ferry keepers who cut the marks) say the cords are the town's, the going was the town's and the weir figures are the town's, and keep the star cord with where she stood. The weir hands are a body of their own this year, sore, proud of the pole, fair to the cord. The rest keep their own counsel.

## Keeper, Year 1, pass two
Nothing came against the sleeping woman at the water gate; no roll. Each of the three she laid hands on recovered, as her card gives: the numbers take the year file's branch for that.

## Keeper, Year 2, pass two
- The strayed herd [r-0295]: part home, part lost: about thirty-five goats and three cattle home, no searcher hurt.
- Nothing came against the short listening. Both whom the god warmed recovered. The council had a mark set to read by at the autumn low water.

## Keeper, Year 3, pass two
- The journey: three days down the larger river, about ninety miles, into the reed country of many channels below the hills, and about eight days back. They were back at the ford by the counted day. Met below: reed cutters at their staithes, and one dusk a band of the boat people, who traded reed work for a knife. The reed cutters say the salt for the stone towns now comes up from the sea in the south and does not need the ford.
- She stood in the larger river each dusk and dawn and felt nothing pull.
- Nothing came against the listening at the ford. She slept about two hours.
- What she told the council in autumn: what the keepers measured; what the reed cutters said of the salt; that the deep water lies farther again from the ford stones on the side away from the weirs and the bed behind each weir is shoaled; that fewer laden beasts crossed; and that she found none like herself in that water. The council's course is the one you wrote for that telling.
