# Year 5 of the Longwalk

Months are counted from the opening of spring: month 1 is the first month of spring. Where a number is mine it is rough, a people's own cord count and not a census. Where the year turns on something the Keeper holds, I write the plain case here and the other branch briefly at the end. Where the Keeper's line on Year 4 differs from my Year 4 file, the Keeper's line is what happened: the calving lost about thirty calves, and the people know it.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The first storm of the year broke over the winter camp in the high wood at the end of the afternoon, and the council sat at dusk in the ring at the main camp before the band went down the Herd Road. The Speaker, the Marchwarden, the Houndwarden, the Saltkeeper and the clan heads sat wet in the ring, and the eldest head of the three clans that camp apart sat among them with two of his men behind him. Tessaly had sent a runner to the apart clans' fires the day before, as she did last year for the herder of the two clans, to say that the keeper had an answer for the young herder who had asked it twice. He came, and stood at the back of the eldest head's men, with his own clan's people round him. The herder of the two clans stood at the edge of the ring among his own. The keeper stood at the edge of the firelight.

Tessaly came into the ring from the margin and said the keeper's answer slowly and whole, as she says everything it gives her: "I did not say yes to the question whether the star dims because the band is too many. I said I know nothing of it, and that is all I said. I do not say it is not any god's, because I cannot say what I do not know. If the band parts, it parts by the council's word and the clans' own, and not for my sake or the star's. I want this band many and unhurried, as I told the council in the second year, and I ask no more than that. A body keeps one margin and not two. I say it now so that no one thinks a part is left unkept by my choosing."

The ring heard it without a sound for a time. The young herder said, to the ring and to Tessaly, that he had asked twice and been given an answer the second time that was not a no, and that his clan had taken it for a yes, and that now the keeper said it had not said yes. He said that if it was not a yes then his clan had counted heads against the nights on the strength of a thing the keeper never said, and he did not say it plainly whose fault that was. Some of his clan's people, behind him, were angry, and said the keeper was taking back a thing it had let them hold for a year. Others said it was the first plain thing it had said of the star and that it had said it was not a yes, and a clan does not part for a thing that was not said. The eldest head said the keeper had now said twice that it wanted the band many, once in the second year and once tonight, and that a keeper that says a thing twice is saying it. He said that "a body keeps one margin and not two" was said to him, since he had refused it a second month at his own, and that the apart clans were told by it that if they parted they would be the part left unkept. Tessaly said the keeper had said it was not by its choosing, and that she had the words as given. The eldest head said she had said them, and that he had heard what she said and what it meant. The old of the main band said it meant only that it keeps the main camp's margin, as it has always said. The Speaker held it in his hand and did not close it. He said the council had heard the keeper, that the parting of a band was the council's and the clans' and no keeper's, as the keeper itself said, and that the council would not take its word on the star nor hold it to one. He took no vote.

Tessaly then said the second thing, in her own voice and not the keeper's. She said she would ask the council's leave for the calving once more, to send hunters and dogs the same dusk on a good reading of wolves, and that she must say plainly what the leave had cost in the last calving, since she had stood in the ring at the river camp and given the cord as about six, and that six had been the first night's count. The second and third nights had been reckoned after, when the herds were counted at the river, and the loss in the whole calving had been about thirty. She said she had sent at the dusk of each reading and the calves had been drawn to the pens by the fires at that dusk, and the wolves had got in on the second and third nights all the same, and that she did not know whether the leave was worth the calves it had cost or whether the wolves were more than the leave could meet. The heads of the clans that had lost the most said the leave was worth no more than waiting, and that they had said the six was too good. Others said the first night had been turned, and that thirty was a bad calving and not the leave's doing, and that it had cost the council nothing. The eldest head said that a thing given twice was a custom and that the custom was thirty calves, and that he had said so and wished it said again. The Speaker said the leave had been given for the calving only, that the calving came again, that the Houndwarden would hold the leave as before and send her hunters on a reading she judged good, and that he would hear at the river camp the whole count of the calving, taken after the third night and not before. He took no vote. No clan head stood against it.

Tessaly went out and gave the ring's words back to the keeper at the margin as they were said, and told it besides that the council had taken none of its word and had given it none, that she held the leave and acted on the leave, and that the loss of the last calving was thirty calves and not six. She said the six was the first night's count, that the herders had penned at the dusk it spoke as it had asked, that they had said afterward they should have done it sooner, and that the second and third nights had been the same dusk and the same pens. She said she did not wish it to go about believing six. It heard her, and said nothing more that night. The herder of the two clans came to the margin after the ring and asked it, through her, to read his herd at dusk each few nights as it had done through the winter, and it said it would.

The band went down the Herd Road behind the storms, and the calving came behind them. The keeper walked at the rear with Tessaly's dogs and kept the margin of each night's camp, standing still at dusk and before light for a long time and then going to Tessaly. On three nights of the calving it told her wolves lay near the herds, how many gatherings, where and which way: the first night the rear herds, the second the east flank, the third the head of the line near the edge of the Farrow Grass. On each of those nights she sent hunters and dogs the same dusk to the place it named, by the council's leave, and told the herders it was her word and the leave and not the keeper's order. This year she did one thing she had not done: she did not send every dog and hunter to the place. After thirty calves she kept back a third of the dogs and four hunters at the pens by the fires with the herders, and sent the rest, since she said she had sent all last year and the wolves had gone by the end of the line. The herders drew the calves from the outer edge of each herd to the thorn pens at the dusk of the reading, at her word, in every herd along that stretch of the line and not only the herd the keeper named, and set the dogs at the pen edges for the night. Some of the herders said as they did it that they had done as much last year. In the plain case the wolves were turned each night before they reached the calves and about six calves were lost in the whole calving. The herders said the herds slept better, and some said it quietly, having said last year that it was not the keeper's reading but the pens. The old herders said they would have the pens by the fires every night of the calving if they had the thorn for it.

In the third month the keeper told Tessaly that a gathering at the far end of the two clans' herds stood hurt in part. The herders found four beasts coughing in it and put them apart in a brush pen the same day. The cough did not go through the herds in spring.

In the second month the star dimmed twice, some nights apart, the blue deepening for about an hour and the star standing dim in the middle of the night. Both nights the keeper was at the margin of the camp on the Farrow Grass, and Tessaly said it stood at dusk before and again after midnight, a long time each, and that it stood no differently otherwise. The herders at the pens saw the herds as they are on any night: the cattle standing and chewing, the calves at the cows, the herds neither fretting nor listening. The dogs lay down both nights and would not be called, as they have on every dimmed night since the first. The Still One did not sit by a herd on either night. The Marchwarden notched them. The keeper went to his fire in the morning after the second and told him what it had read of the herds under the star, as it had said it would, and said that it read the beasts and not the sky and did not take it as an answer to why the star dims. He took it to his notches and said it was a mark that cost no one and claimed nothing, and that he would put it with the rest. The camps on the Farrow Grass said the star had dimmed twice in the second month and that it was as many as last spring and not yet midsummer. The apart clans, whose eldest head was in the camp, counted again against the band's heads, and said only that they would count at the going down. The band said it was because the keeper had told the ring at the first storm what it had not said and what it had, and that the star had waited to be answered. This is not known.

In the third month the salt ran to the bottom of the cord, as it does every spring. The Saltkeeper told the Speaker the party must go in the fifth month and take no cattle into the towns' yards while the towns' oxen coughed. At the council on the Farrow Grass the council spoke of whether to send the stone towns a word. The Speaker, who had said last year that it had been a claim and would not be said twice, said the party would carry goods and the Saltkeeper's cord and no more. A clan head who lost most of the calves asked whether three sound bulls of the main herds might go to the yards for salt, as cattle used to. The Saltkeeper said no beast of the main herds would go into the yards where the oxen coughed, and that the band would smoke the lean meat and salt the fat. The Speaker took no vote.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The band came down to the river under its line of trees, as every summer. There was rain in the summer as in most years. The pools stood and the river ran, and no month was dry. The three clans that camp apart came down the Herd Road behind the main band at their own pace, as they do, and camped on the river's upper bank apart as in other years, no nearer and no farther. The keeper stood at the stopped camp at dusk and at each pool and stream the Marchwarden named, and read the herds that came down to drink. No pool went bad. No flux came. No camp was moved on its word or any word. If the keeper read a herd hurt at the first dusk at any pool, Tessaly carried it to the Marchwarden as last year, with how little one dusk is and that the water was its guess, and the Marchwarden would keep the herd off the pool one night, have the camps draw and boil their drinking water above, ask for the next dusk's reading and not move the camp unless the second dusk read hurt too. A small belly sickness took three infants in the sixth month in a clan whose camp lay on the lower bank, and that was the ordinary of summer.

In the fifth month the trade party set out in the first days for the stone towns of the Hask Ridge by the way the band has always gone, with sound goats and sheep, hides and felt and pack goats, and no cattle. It carried no word from the council. It carried the Saltkeeper's cord, salt first and iron only for the edges the band could not do without.

In the sixth month the council sat at dusk at the river camp. The keeper stood at the edge of the firelight, and Tessaly gave the ledger of her cord and the whole count of the calving, taken after the third night. The cord held one knot this year, a boar at the river. She gave the count of the calving, which in the plain case was about six. The eldest head said the leave had been given three times and that a custom given three times is a law. The council spoke of the calving, and of the leave, and of the star, which had dimmed twice and not three times by midsummer. No one asked the keeper for anything. No vote was taken.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

In the seventh month the star dimmed once. The keeper had told Tessaly it would be along the timber some nights of that month, toward the winter woods, and it was not at the margin on those nights. Tessaly marked it and said nothing, as in other years. The night the star dimmed was one of those, or not: Tessaly could say of the month that it was gone on some nights and could not say of that night whether it was at the margin. The herders saw the herds as on any night, and the dogs lay down. The Marchwarden notched it and said that was three in the year, to seven last year, to six, to five and three before, and twenty four since the keeper came, and that the numbers had fallen and he could no more find a rule in the fall than in the climb. The apart clans said the star dimmed when the keeper was gone from the margin. The main band said the keeper had stood at the margin on two of the three. The apart clans did not say which was right. The band's own talk was that the star had eased, and some said it was because the apart clans had spoken of parting and some because the keeper had said what it said.

On a night in the seventh month the keeper was away, the herder of the two clans came to the margin at dusk for his reading of his herd. Tessaly said it was along the timber and she did not know when it would return. He waited the night and the next dusk at the edge of the Houndwarden's fire, and the next dusk the keeper was at the margin and read his herd, and told him which gatherings stood hurt in part. He had found two coughing beasts in the meanwhile by walking his gatherings at dusk, which he said was what a clan did that had a dog that points at a flock.

In the eighth month the keeper came to the Marchwarden's fire at dusk and told him what it read of the stone towns' cutters in the winter woods, the gatherings with draught beasts, working and unafraid, and said again that past a mile it read gatherings and their state and not the number of people, and that it kept more than a mile from them. The Marchwarden asked whether they had come in since the last count. It answered with what it read, and no more. He took what it read to the Speaker and to the Saltkeeper's fire. The keeper said once more that what the council wanted of the cutters was the council's to say and that it took no trail on a people who had done no wrong. The Marchwarden said he had thought it would say so.

The hunt in the timber came with the first cold. The council asked the keeper in few words to stand at the hunt's edge and say where the game lay, and it said it would, and asked the hunt to keep its season: no doe with young, and no beast for its hide alone. The hunt kept it, as it always does. Game was found as in most years. The larders were filled as well as last year, not full. No one was gravely hurt. No share was set on the flat stone for the keeper, and the keeper set none. The share of the first kill was left on the stone and was gone in the morning.

The cough came back in the cattle of the two clans from the seventh month, as in other years. About ten of their beasts died by the ninth month. They had the coughing beasts apart in a brush pen within two days of the first, as they have learned to, and the herder used the keeper's readings and his own walking at dusk, and said it was a day sooner each time.

The trade party came home in the ninth month. It brought salt, about half of what the band wanted for the winter, dear, at no better price, since no cattle had paid for it. It brought iron in about as few pieces as last year, and grey and soft. The party reported the towns' draught oxen coughing still, and the cutters in the winter woods again, about as far in as last winter and no farther. It had been in the towns' yards and seen the oxen from the fence and had not gone in among them. It bought no beast. It carried no word either way and was not asked after any.

Tessaly carried the keeper's asking to the Saltkeeper in the ninth month at the margin of the camp: that the party's pack goats and the sheep it brought back be kept apart on the downwind end until a dusk's reading showed no hurt in part, as six weeks served last year, and that the two clans' cattle winter apart on their own ground. The Saltkeeper said they had meant six weeks, since the towns' oxen coughed still, and that the two clans had meant to winter apart. The band said once more that a keeper told what it meant is not contradicted. The Saltkeeper asked Tessaly to have the keeper's reading of the party's beasts at dusk at the end of the six weeks, as one asks a dog, since it cost nothing. At the end of the six weeks Tessaly gave the keeper's reading. In the plain case it read no hurt in part, and the pack goats and sheep went in with the herds. Nobody took the keeper's word as an order in this.

In the ninth month, when Tessaly's asking about the winter came to the two clans, part of them said again that they should winter among the herds and be done with being the clans that coughed. The old woman of the two clans who had said last year that a keeper that says it does not know is better than one that says what it does not know came to the margin with the herder and put it to the keeper through Tessaly: whether its words that the cough came hard to those that wintered apart meant that the two clans were blamed. It answered in front of her that the cough came hard to them and no other herd took it, that it did not say it was their fault, and that it did not know why it had chosen them. She said that was the same thing it had said before and that she would take it as that, and went back to her clan. The two clans, after some quarrel among themselves, said they would winter apart on their own ground, because the cough had come to one in fifteen of theirs and not one in six when they had kept apart and put the sick apart, and because the herder had said he could find the sick sooner than the others could.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

The band went up to the high wood for the lean of winter. The two clans wintered on their own ground at the downwind end of the camp, fed from their own cut browse, with the coughing beasts in a brush pen at the far end, as they did the last three winters. The Speaker named browse stands to them by cord, which cost the others little. The three clans that want to winter apart wintered apart again, a little nearer than last year, within sight of the main camp's smoke at dusk, the second time they had come nearer and not farther. The young herders of the three clans said the star had dimmed three times and not seven, that the count against the band's heads was broken, and that the keeper had said it was not a yes. The eldest head said nothing was settled, that the band would be counted again at the next going down, and that if the star dimmed as it had in the years before he would speak of parting in the ring and the keeper would not be asked for its leave. The fuel wood was scarcer again, and in the tenth month the Speaker named the stands for each clan by cord before there could be a quarrel, and the three clans took their cord. No blow was struck this winter.

The cough was in the two clans' cattle through the winter. It took about one beast in fifteen of theirs, some forty, and no more. They burned the dead at the far end and did not eat them. It stayed in their beasts and no other herd took it. The herder of the two clans came to the margin each few nights at dusk and the keeper read his herd and told him which gatherings stood hurt in part, and he found beasts coughing in the gatherings it named, and he and the young of his clan began walking the gatherings at dusk themselves, so that the clan found the sick sooner than last year by a day, and said so. The herder said the saying that a clan with a dog that points at a flock does well to go and look was now the clan's rule.

The star did not dim in the winter. The Marchwarden said it was the third winter of the five that it had not, and that the keeper had stood at the margin in all of them, the winters it dimmed and the winters it did not. The band said the star dimmed less and the keeper did the same, and that none could say which was the cause of which. In the twelfth month, as the band was making ready to go down, the Marchwarden came to the margin at dusk and asked the keeper, through Tessaly, whether the star dimming three times and not seven was its doing. It answered in front of them that it knew nothing of it, that it had done nothing at those hours that it knew of, and that it did not say it was not any god's, because it could not say what it did not know. He said he had thought it would say so, and put it with the notches.

In the eleventh month the Still One sat by the herds of the two clans for one night at their asking, and did not eat the day after, and was slow among her own for some days. She said the herd was tired in the chest still, and about as last year. The young are wary of her again. She has still not spoken with the keeper.

The Speaker had no chest fever this winter. He is slower, and slept through the closing of a long ring in the eleventh month, and the Marchwarden spoke the closing words in his place, as an old man does for an old man, and the ring let him. The clans' talk of who follows the Speaker is a little louder, and some said that the Marchwarden had spoken the closing, and no name was said aloud.

The year ended with the band at the edge of spring on the way down from the winter woods, with the keeper at the margin of the camp, the horn at its side and a way out behind it, as it has stood the four winters before. It has been asked for a watch, a count, a standing at the hunt's edge, a leave for the wolves that went to the Houndwarden, and the reading of a pool and a cough.

## If the Keeper rules the other way

The wolves of the calving, if the hunters sent the same dusk with a third of the force kept at the pens and the calves drawn in to every herd along that stretch did not turn them on all three nights: about thirty calves lost again. The herders say the leave given three times has cost thirty calves twice and that waiting would have cost no more. The eldest head says a custom given three times has been shown. Tessaly brings her whole count to the river camp short, and does not ask the leave a fourth time unless the council gives it. Cattle in the state numbers fall by about twenty four from the plain case.

The party's beasts at the end of the six weeks, if the keeper's reading showed hurt in part: the Saltkeeper keeps them apart a second month, and if the hurt stays the beast it reads in is killed and burned at the far end by the old rule, and the band says that the keeper was right and that the stone towns' cough is in the iron. Nothing in the state numbers moves by more than a goat or two.

The pool: no pool went bad in either branch; the branches differ only in whether the Marchwarden kept a herd off a pool a night and what the band says.

## Causes, in short

- The council kept the leave for the calving because it cost nothing and the first night had been turned, and the Speaker would not close it or take a vote, and the eldest head said what he had said.
- Tessaly kept a third of her force at the pens because thirty calves were lost with all of it sent out, and she thought the wolves went round the force to the pens.
- The apart clans' count broke because the star dimmed three times with the band larger, and because the keeper said it had not said yes. They came a little nearer in winter because the count had weakened and the Speaker's cord for the fuel wood had cost them nothing.
- The salt was about half the wanting because no cattle went to pay for it and the price did not fall, and the band would take no beast into the yards where the oxen coughed.
- The iron was fewer pieces because the Saltkeeper bought only the edges the band could not do without; the band believes the iron is sick.
- The cough took one in fifteen again because the two clans wintered apart on their own ground, and because they found the sick sooner, or because the cough was fewer. Nothing in my record says which.
- The star dimmed three times because the star dimmed three times. Nothing in my record says why.
- The band says the star eased because the keeper spoke at the first storm. It is wrong about the order of it: the star dimmed twice in the month after.
