# Year 2 of the Longwalk

Months are counted from the opening of spring: month 1 is the first month of spring. Where a number is mine it is rough, a people's own cord count and not a census. Where the year turns on something the Keeper holds, I write the plain case here and the other branch briefly after it.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The first storm of the year broke over the winter camp in the high wood on the evening the band was making ready to go down. The council sat at dusk in the ring at the main camp, wet and short-tempered: the Speaker, the Marchwarden, the Houndwarden, the Saltkeeper and the clan heads, and among them the eldest head of the three clans that camp apart, with two of his own men standing behind him. The keeper stood at the edge of the firelight where it stands. Before the ring had settled it asked Tessaly to carry a word in, and she came into the ring and said it slowly and whole, since she is slow to speak and wanted it kept exactly as given: "It is true, and I will say what I want once. I want this band many and unhurried, and I want to be told when what I do costs it. I ask no share, no seat and no rite. The council may keep me at the edge or send me from it." She said it was in answer to what the eldest head had said before the keeper at the river camp, and the eldest head heard it said to him by the Houndwarden, which was not how he had expected to hear it.

Nobody spoke for a time. The eldest head said it was a small thing to want, and that small things are the first things. The Speaker held it in his hand and did not close it. He said the council had heard the keeper, that a thing which asks no share, no seat and no rite is a margin keeper, and that the margin is the Houndwarden's, as before. He said the council would not send it from the edge and would not take its word. As to being told what its doing costs the band, he said the band has no habit of telling a thing its costs, but that Tessaly might carry to it what she saw of them, as she carries the cord. No vote was taken. Tessaly went out to the margin and gave this back. The keeper stood where it was and said nothing more.

The band went down the Herd Road behind the storms. The calving came behind them, as it does, and the herds were thin from winter, the dried meat low and the salt short, so the first weeks were lean and quick. The keeper walked at the rear with Tessaly's dogs and kept the margin of each night's camp. Each dusk and again before light it stood still at the edge of the camp for a long time and then let out a breath as if after effort and walked back to the fire. Then it went to Tessaly and told her where the herds were hurt or afraid or resting, and she told the herders, and on three nights it told her where wolves were and which way they were going, and she and the hunters turned them as the year before. In the plain case the wolves were turned each of the three nights and no more than six calves were lost to them in the whole calving. The herders at the rear say, quietly, that the herds sleep better when the keeper is at the margin, and the old herders say a herd that sleeps does not drop its calves early. Through Tessaly the keeper had asked the herders to put apart any beast that coughs. The two clans whose cattle cough did that without being asked twice. Eight beasts coughed in the spring and were kept apart. The cough did not go through the herds.

In the second month the star dimmed, one night, the blue deepening for about an hour and the star standing dim in the middle of the night. It was the fourth since the keeper came, counting last year's three, and the band had a saying about the fourth: that when the star dimmed a fourth time the keeper would ask a thing of them. People lay awake for it. The old said the asking had come already, a month before, at the first storms, in the keeper's own words to the ring, and that the star had only waited to be sure it was heard. The younger herders said the asking was to be told when the keeper's doing costs, and that this was not a thing worth a star. The eldest head's people said the saying was an old wives' saying and that the star dims as the star dims.

In the second month at the council on the Farrow Grass, the Speaker put the keeper's other request to the eldest head, as the keeper had asked it of him through Tessaly: leave for the keeper to keep the margin of the three clans' camp for a month, as it keeps the main camp's, and the telling to their head that a keeper would stand at the edge of their fire and ask nothing. The eldest head said he would answer when the summer water was known. The Speaker did not press him.

By the third month the salt had run to the bottom of the cord. The Saltkeeper told the Speaker the trade party would have to go in the fifth month as it did last year, and that the cutters would be farther in.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The band came down to the river under its line of trees, to the summer grazing, as every summer. In the fourth month no rain fell on the grazing for the whole of the month. The grass by the river browned at the edges of the range, the small streams ran thin and some stopped, and the river itself ran on, lower, with its banks standing out of the water as they do not stand in summer.

How the band met it. The Marchwarden and the Houndwarden together, with the Speaker's word on it, laid the watering out by cord, clan by clan, so that each clan's herds had a stretch of bank and an hour at dawn and at dusk, and no herd drank outside its turn. The herds were rested in the shade of the trees through the heat and moved by night onto the browned edge, where the grass is dry but standing, in short moves, never more than a day at a time, to keep the herds out of the press at the water. The women drew drinking water above the camps, at the top of each clan's stretch, and the children were kept off the standing pools in the dry stream beds. No fire was lit outside the hearth stones on the dry grass. Where a stream had stopped, the herders dug scrapes in the bed to see if water stood under, and in some it did. The keeper stood at the margin at dusk and before light as in spring. It told Tessaly each dusk what it read of each herd at the water: hurt, afraid or resting. Tessaly told the herders at the water that the keeper read it so, and that she took it as one takes a dog's word, and the herders shifted a herd or let it stay as they judged. The keeper spoke with no beast that anyone saw. In the plain case the herds were watered by turns, and the band lost about eighty cattle in the month, mostly calves and the old cows, and some thirty goats and twenty sheep.

The Still One, the old woman who can sit by a herd and tell its mood, sat by the herds at the thinnest water on three nights, at the Houndwarden's asking, and did not eat the day after each, and was slow among her own for a week after. She said of the first herd she sat by that it was afraid of the others and not of the thirst. Tessaly said she would not ask her again unless the herd was near to breaking, and did not.

A flux took the children at the fouled pools in the middle of the month, in the clans whose camps lay on the streams that stopped. About sixteen died, twelve of them children and four old, before the rain. The clans burned the fouled bedding and moved the camps.

A woman of the herders of the east flank, whose calf-herd was on the dry edge, said in front of Tessaly at the water that the keeper was the storms' temper, as the old said, and that someone should ask it for rain. Tessaly said the keeper asks nothing and the band does not ask a keeper for what it would not ask of the council, and that she would not carry it. The woman said that was a dog handler's pride. Tessaly did not carry it. This was not put to the keeper.

The three clans that camp apart had their own streams stop, early. They came down to the river's upper bank in the second week with their herds and found the turns laid out without them. A young herder of theirs and a boy of the main band's herd came near to blows at the bank over the dawn turn, with their dogs between them, and the Speaker named the Three at the Fire for the matter, three who had not sat on last winter's knife. He did not say who was right. He gave the dawn watering at the upper bank to the three clans' herds for ten days, as the ones who had come last and had farthest to come, and left the dusk watering to the main band's herds, and had the turns cut by cord so no one could say another had been cheated. The eldest head said it was a fair thing and a thing the Speaker had done to hold them. It was observed that he did not thank him.

In the fifth month the rain came back, steady, for three days. The flux stopped within a week. The browned edge greened a little at the roots. The herds were thin and the cows gave little milk, and the fat they should have laid on in the fattening was not laid, and the band went lean into the autumn.

At the beginning of the fifth month, before the rain, the eldest head answered the Speaker. The keeper might keep the margin of his camp for one month, as it kept the main camp's, since it kept the margin and not the band, which was what his people had said of it. It would not come into the ring or the camp, it would bring no word from the main council and carry none back, and at the month's end it would go. The Speaker carried it to Tessaly and Tessaly to the keeper. The keeper went by the wood's edge at dusk and came to the three clans' dogs first. Their dogs fell silent and turned to it as the main camp's do, and the herders saw it. It kept their night watch on their flock for the month. It said to their head what it had said to the council, that it asked nothing. The head came to the edge of the light on the twentieth night and stood a while and asked it two things, and went away. Nothing else happened that the three clans could tell afterward, and they told that: that nothing had happened. At the month's end it went back to the main camp by the wood's edge.

The trade party set out in the fifth month for the stone towns of the Hask Ridge by the way the band has always gone, with sound sheep and goats, hides and felt and fewer cattle than last year: the Saltkeeper and the Houndwarden kept back every beast of the two clans that had coughed, and every beast thin from the dry month. The party came back before the going up with salt and iron at no better price than last year. It reported the cutters of the stone towns in the winter woods again, farther in than last year, and that in the stone towns' yards some of the towns' own draught oxen were coughing.

In the first days of the sixth month the council sat at dusk at the river camp, after the keeper's return from the three clans. The keeper gave the council its ledger as Tessaly's cord had it. The cord for this year held four knots: a fox at the camp by day, a boar at the last standing pool, three wolves seen at the river by day and not touched, and one for the pack that came near the herds on the three nights of the calving. The keeper told the council it had spoken with neither the fox nor the boar of last summer, and with no beast this year. The Marchwarden, who notches the nights the star dims, asked whether the dimming was the keeper's. The keeper said that it knew nothing of it, that it had done nothing at those hours that it knew of, and that it did not say it was not its own because it could not say what it did not know. The council spoke at length. No vote was taken. A dozen nights after, the star dimmed the fifth time since the keeper came, the sixth month's night, and the keeper was at the margin of the camp, and did nothing that the Houndwarden saw.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

In the long fattening season the keeper went on some nights by the timber along the band's range toward the winter woods, and was not at the margin, and Tessaly took note and said nothing. In the eighth month it came to the Marchwarden's fire at dusk and told him what it had counted of the cutters of the stone towns in the winter woods: how many, how far in, what they take. The Marchwarden asked it nothing and heard it. It asked him whether the songs said the cutters had come so far in before. He said the songs of the winter woods name the stands of timber one after another, and that the verses have the axe at the wood's edge in his grandfather's grandfather's day and never past the first stands, and that the verse for the third stand says only that the axe has not been there. He took what the keeper had counted to the Speaker, and the two of them took it, with the trade party's word when it came, to the Saltkeeper's fire.

The hunt in the timber came with the first cold. The Speaker did not put the keeper to the council this year as a thing to be quarrelled over. He said that last year the hunt asked it and that the hunt might ask it again, and the council asked it, in few words, without a quarrel: would it stand with the hunters at the hunt's edge and say where the game lay. It said it would, and asked the hunt to keep its season. The hunt kept it, as it always does, and found game as in most years, and the larders were filled enough, as last year, not well. No share was set on the flat stone for the keeper, and the keeper set none. The share of the first kill was left on the stone as every year, for whom no one says, and was gone in the morning. In the ninth month, the night after the last of the big kills, the star dimmed, the sixth night since the keeper came, and the hunters said the keeper had stood at their edge the day before and that the star had dimmed on it.

The coughing sickness came back in a small way in the ninth month, in the cattle of the two clans, as last year. Five beasts died. The clans had them apart before the second day, and the keeper had told Tessaly of the herd that was hurt at the edge of the camp. The cough did not go through the herds.

The trade party, home in the ninth month, told what it saw in the stone towns' yards and the Houndwarden heard it, and the Saltkeeper heard it. The band said the cough was the stone towns', that it had come to the clans' cattle with the iron, or with the beasts the holds sold, or with the cutters. The Houndwarden and the Saltkeeper said that next year's party would take no cattle into the towns' yards, and that sheep, goats, hides and felt must pay for what cattle paid before.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

The band went up to the high wood for the lean of winter. The two clans' herds wintered at the downwind end of the camp on their own ground, fed from their own cut browse, with the coughing beasts in a brush pen at the far end. The three clans that want to winter apart wintered apart again, a little nearer than last year, within sight of the main camp's smoke at dusk, the first time that they had come nearer and not farther since the woods began to thin. The fuel wood was scarcer: the nearest stands burned down quickly, and in the tenth month the Speaker named the stands for each clan by cord, before there could be a quarrel. There was no blow struck this winter. The dog handlers brought Tessaly small costs of the keeper's doing, as the keeper had asked to be told, and she carried them to it at the margin, and it heard them.

In the eleventh month the star dimmed the seventh night since the keeper came. That night the keeper stood at the margin of the winter camp. The dogs lay down and would not be called, as on the dimmed nights before, and the keeper, the Houndwarden said, stood no differently than other nights.

In the eleventh month a chest fever took Orrin Haddock for two weeks. He lived. He was slower on the going down, and the clans, quietly, began to talk of who would follow him, and no one has said a name aloud.

In the twelfth month, at the end of winter, the star dimmed the eighth night since the keeper came, as the band was making ready to go down, and the old counted it with the Marchwarden's notches and said it was five this year to three last year, and that they were coming oftener.

The year ended with the band at the edge of spring on the way down from the winter woods, with the keeper at the margin of the camp, the horn at its side, and a way out behind it. It has been asked for nothing but a watch, a count and a standing at the hunt's edge, and it has asked nothing but one thing, said once.

## If the Keeper rules the other way

The spring wolves, if the watch did not turn them: about thirty calves lost over the three nights, the rear camp saying the keeper was no better than a dog, and the knot for the spring wolves left open. The dry month, if the watering did not hold: crowding at the banks and the herds breaking their turns by night, calves trampled in the press, about three hundred and fifty cattle and about a hundred and fifty goats and sheep lost for the month, the quarrel at the upper bank coming to blows, and the band going into the autumn much leaner and the clans sourer, the three clans likelier to winter far apart than near.

## Causes, in short

- The salt came in short again because the herds sent to the towns were fewer and sounder, since the cattle that cough or are thin were kept back, and because the price did not fall.
- The flux took the children because the streams stopped and the camps on them drank from the pools the herds had fouled.
- The three clans came nearer this winter because the Speaker gave their herds the dawn water in the dry month, and because they had had the keeper at their own margin for a month and nothing had come of it.
- The cord for bold beasts grew because the dry month brought the beasts to the water.
- The dimming nights were five because the star dimmed five times. Nothing in my record says why.
