# Year 4 of the Longwalk

Months are counted from the opening of spring: month 1 is the first month of spring. Where a number is mine it is rough, a people's own cord count and not a census. Where the year turns on something the Keeper holds, I write the plain case here and the other branch briefly at the end.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The first storm of the year broke over the winter camp in the high wood in the evening, and the council sat at dusk in the ring at the main camp before the band went down the Herd Road. The Speaker, the Marchwarden, the Houndwarden, the Saltkeeper and the clan heads sat wet in the ring, and the eldest head of the three clans that camp apart sat among them with two of his men behind him. At the edge of the ring, among his own clan's people, stood the herder of the two clans who had put his question at the margin in the tenth month of last year. Tessaly had sent word to him by a runner that he should be there. The keeper stood at the edge of the firelight.

Tessaly came into the ring from the margin and said the keeper's answer slowly and whole, as she says everything it gives her, since she wanted it kept as given: "You asked whether I can say what the cough is. I cannot. When I listen over a herd I know that it is hurt in part, and I do not know with what, or which beasts. I know that it came back hard in the two clans' cattle that wintered apart, and that no other herd took it. I can tell you at dusk which gatherings read hurt in part, and I will, and the choice of what to do with it is yours."

The ring heard it without a sound for a time. The herder said, to the ring and to Tessaly and to no one, that he had asked for a name for it and been given a smaller thing than a name. An old woman of the two clans said that a keeper that says it does not know is better than one that says what it does not know, and that she had expected a guess. The herder said that a keeper that listens over a herd and cannot say which beast is hurt is a dog that points at the whole flock. Tessaly said that was so, and that a dog that points at the flock has still told you to look. He did not answer her. His clan said afterward that the keeper had said the cough came hard to them because they had wintered apart, and some were angry that it said so. Others said that it had said only what had happened, that the apart wintering was their own rule and had kept the cough in their beasts and out of everyone else's, and that the angry ones had heard a blame that was not in the words. The angry ones were not persuaded.

Tessaly then said the second thing, in her own voice and not the keeper's. She said she meant to do as she had done at the last calving, if the council did not say she might not: to send the hunters and dogs the same dusk on a good reading of wolves, and to bring her cord of it to the river camp. She said the keeper had asked nothing more of the council and that she asked for the leave only as the council had given it. 
The Speaker, slower in his speech again, said the leave had been given for the calving and for the calving only, that the calving came again as it comes, and that the Houndwarden would send her hunters on a reading she judged good, as before, and he would hear her cord at the river camp. He took no vote. The eldest head said he did not stand against it, that a thing given once is a leave and a thing given twice is a custom, and that he wished it said in the ring that he had said so. No clan head stood against it. Tessaly went out and gave the ring's words back to the keeper at the margin as they were said, and told it besides, plainly, that the council had not taken its word for the wolves, that it had given her leave to send her own hunters, and that she had acted on the leave and not on a word, and she did not wish it to go about believing otherwise. It answered the herder's asking through her at the margin that night, that it would read his herd at dusk and tell her which gatherings stood hurt in part, as it had said. That was all.

The band went down the Herd Road behind the storms, and the calving came behind them. The keeper walked at the rear with Tessaly's dogs and kept the margin of each night's camp, standing still at dusk and before light for a long time and then going to Tessaly. On three nights of the calving it told her wolves lay near the herds, how many gatherings, where and which way: the first night the east flank, the second the head of the line near the edge of the Farrow Grass, the third the rear herds. On each night she sent the hunters and dogs the same dusk to the place, and the herders did what they do only after a wolf is seen and had not done before the dusk of a reading: they drew the calves in from the outer edge of each herd to the thorn pens by the fires and set the dogs at the pen edges for the night. Tessaly told the herders each time that it was her word and the council's leave. In the plain case the wolves were turned each night before they reached the calves, and no more than six calves were lost in the whole calving. The herders said the herds slept better for the third spring, and the old herders said the pens by the fires were what a wolf night wanted whether the keeper spoke or no, and that they would have made them sooner had they known which night.

In the third month the keeper told Tessaly that a gathering at the far end of the two clans' herds stood hurt in part. The herders found three beasts coughing in it and put them apart in a brush pen the same day, as they do. The cough did not go through the herds in spring.

In the second month the star dimmed, one night, the blue deepening for about an hour and the star standing dim in the middle of the night. It dimmed again in the third month, and the camps on the Farrow Grass said that the star had dimmed in two months running and that it was not yet midsummer. The dogs lay down and would not be called, both nights. The Marchwarden notched them. The apart clans, whose eldest head was not at that council, sent a young man to say they had counted the band's heads at the river before the going down and that the band was five thousand and a hundred and more, and that when the band had stood at four thousand and a few hundred the star had not dimmed. The Speaker said he would hear the count and said no more of it.

In the third month the salt ran to the bottom of the cord, as it does every spring. The Saltkeeper told the Speaker the party must go in the fifth month and take no cattle into the towns' yards. At the council on the Farrow Grass the council spoke at length of whether to send the stone towns a word again. Some of the council said last year's word had been heard and nothing promised, and that a second word was a begging twice. The Speaker, who had had the party carry it last year as the band's claim and not as a plea, said it had been a claim, and that the band said "begging" because it had come home with the iron soft. He said the council would send no word this year, that the party would carry goods and the Saltkeeper's cord and no more, and that the Marchwarden would sing the third stand the way he had said it needed to be sung until the towns' cutters came to hear it. He took no vote.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The band came down to the river under its line of trees, as every summer. There was rain in the summer as in most years. The pools stood and the river ran. In the fourth month the star dimmed once more, the third time since the first storm, and the band said it was a year of three before midsummer. The apart clans counted again. The keeper stood at the stopped camp at dusk and at each pool and stream the Marchwarden named, and read the herds that came down to drink.

In the first dusk of the fifth month at the pool below the third camp, the keeper told Tessaly that a herd that watered there stood hurt in part, and that this was one dusk and was little and might be nothing. It said that whether the herd had drunk or only grazed, and anything about the water, was its own guess and not what the Listening gave. This is the earliest it had said such a thing, and the mothers of the three children who died last year at the pool below the second ford were told of it by the old women the same night. Tessaly took it to the Marchwarden. He heard it once and said that the verse of that pool is sweet in rain and sweet when it is stood, and that he would not move a camp on one dusk and a guess, and that he would not sit on his hands either. He had the herd that had watered there kept from that pool for the night and watered at the stream above the camp. The women drew the camps' drinking water at the top of the stretch and boiled it, as the old women's custom is for a flux, though nothing had yet sickened. He asked Tessaly to ask the keeper to read the herd again at the next dusk. The next dusk the keeper told her the herd read resting. The camp stayed its three days and went on up the stream in the round. No flux came to that pool. A small belly sickness took two infants in the sixth month in a clan whose camp lay on the lower bank, and that was the ordinary of summer.

The band says the keeper's word saved the camp again, or that the keeper cried at a herd that had a cough in it and the Marchwarden lost a night's herd off the water for nothing. The mothers of the three, who wished it said, said the keeper said it the first dusk and that a mother would sooner have a keeper that cries early. The Marchwarden said nothing of either and notched the nights.

In the fifth month the trade party set out in the first days for the stone towns of the Hask Ridge by the way the band has always gone, with sound goats and sheep, hides and felt and pack goats, and no cattle, as the Saltkeeper and the Houndwarden had said. It carried no word from the council. It carried the Saltkeeper's cord of what the band wanted, which was salt first, and iron only for the edges the band could not do without, since the band's talk was that the iron was the sickness's work and the smiths of the towns could not be trusted to make it sound.

In the sixth month the council sat at dusk at the river camp. The keeper stood at the edge of the firelight, and Tessaly gave the ledger of her cord. The cord held two knots this year: a fox at the camp by day and an old boar at the river, and no knot was tied for a thing the keeper did. She said the cord of the calving had two lines in it, the leave and the number of calves lost, and gave it: about six. The eldest head said the leave had been given twice. The council spoke of the calving, and of the pool, and of the leave. No one asked the keeper for anything. No vote was taken.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

In the seventh month the keeper went on some nights through the timber along the band's ground toward the winter woods and was not at the margin. Tessaly marked it and said nothing, as in other years. In the eighth month the star dimmed, once, on a night the keeper was not at the margin, and the Marchwarden notched it and said the numbers were three, five, six and now seven by the end of the year, if the count went on as it had, and that he could find no rule in them and wished he could.

On the second of the nights the keeper was away, a young herder of the three clans that camp apart, sent by his own clan, not by his eldest head, came to the margin of the main camp at dusk and said to Tessaly that he would ask the keeper whether the star dimmed because the band was too many to walk as one. The keeper was not at the margin. Tessaly said it was along the timber and she did not know when it would return. He waited two nights, ate at the edge of the Houndwarden's fire as a guest, and went home on the third morning without an answer. Tessaly said the question would be put to it when it came back. Among his clan it was said that the keeper had not answered because it knew the answer was yes.

In the eighth month the keeper came to the Marchwarden's fire at dusk and told him what it read of the stone towns' cutters in the winter woods, the gatherings with draught beasts, working and unafraid, and a little farther in than when it had last counted, and said again that past a mile it read gatherings and their state and not the number of people, and that it kept more than a mile from them. The Marchwarden asked it whether the axe had passed the third stand. It answered with what it read, a little deeper than last year, and no more. He took what it read to the Speaker and to the Saltkeeper's fire. The keeper said once more, to him, that what the council wanted of the cutters was the council's to say and that it took no trail on a people who had done no wrong. He said he had thought it would say so.

The hunt in the timber came with the first cold. The council asked the keeper in few words to stand at the hunt's edge and say where the game lay, and it said it would, and asked the hunt to keep its season: no doe with young, and no beast for its hide alone. The hunt kept it, as it always does. Game was found as in most years. The larders were filled as well as last year, not full. One hunter was hurt in a fall, not gravely. No share was set on the flat stone for the keeper, and the keeper set none. The share of the first kill was left on the stone and was gone in the morning.

The cough came back in the cattle of the two clans from the seventh month, as in other years. About ten of their beasts died by the ninth month. They had them apart in a brush pen within two days of the first, as they have learned to, and Tessaly told the herders what the keeper said of the far end of their gathering, and they said they had known and that it was a day sooner.

The trade party came home in the ninth month. It brought salt, about half of what the band wanted for the winter, at no better price, since no cattle had paid for it. It brought iron, fewer pieces than last year, since the Saltkeeper's cord had said to buy no more than the edges could not do without, and it was greyer and softer again; the smiths of the stone towns were complaining of their ore, and said so, as they had last year. The party reported the towns' draught oxen coughing still, and the cutters in the winter woods again, farther in. It had been in the towns' yards and seen the oxen from the fence and had not gone in among them. It bought no beast. It was not asked about the word, since none had been sent, and the band noted that the towns had asked after it: a smith had said to a man of the party, at the salt scale, that the band had been quiet this year, and the man had said the band had said what it had to say.

Tessaly carried the keeper's asking to the Saltkeeper at the margin of the camp in the ninth month: that the party's pack goats and the sheep it brought back be kept apart on the downwind end for longer than last year's month, until a dusk's reading of them showed no hurt in part, and that the two clans' cattle stay on their own ground apart through the winter. The Saltkeeper said they had meant six weeks already, since the towns' oxen coughed still and this year's party had been in the yards, and the two clans had meant to winter apart as they had before, and said so. The band said, as it said last year, that a keeper told what it meant is not contradicted. The Saltkeeper asked Tessaly to have the keeper's reading of the party's beasts at dusk at the end of the six weeks, as one asks a dog, since it cost nothing. At the end of the six weeks Tessaly gave the keeper's reading. In the plain case it read no hurt in part, and the pack goats and sheep went in with the herds. Nobody took the keeper's word as an order in this.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

The band went up to the high wood for the lean of winter. The two clans wintered on their own ground at the downwind end of the camp, fed from their own cut browse, with the coughing beasts in a brush pen at the far end, as they did the last two winters. The Speaker named browse stands to them by cord, which cost the others little. The three clans that want to winter apart wintered apart again, no nearer and no farther than last year, within sight of the main camp's smoke at dusk. The fuel wood was scarcer again, and in the tenth month the Speaker named the stands for each clan by cord, before there could be a quarrel, and the three clans took their cord. No blow was struck this winter.

The cough was in the two clans' cattle through the winter. It took far fewer than last year, about one beast in fifteen of theirs, some forty, and no more. They burned the dead at the far end and did not eat them. It stayed in their beasts and no other herd took it. The herder of the two clans came to the margin on the first week of the tenth month and asked the keeper through Tessaly to read his herd at dusk each few nights and say which gathering of it stood hurt in part, since he could not know which beast. It said it would, and did. He found beasts coughing in the gatherings it named and put them apart, and said in his clan that it was a dog that points at a flock, and that a clan with a dog that points at a flock does well to go and look.

In the tenth month the young herder of the apart clans came again to the margin, this time with a man of the main band beside him, and the keeper stood at the edge of the light. He asked it again whether the star dimmed because the band was too many to walk as one. The keeper answered in front of him that it knew nothing of the dimming, that it had done nothing at those hours that it knew of, and that it did not say it was not any god's, because it could not say what it did not know. He went back to his clan and said the keeper had not said no. His clan said that meant yes.

The star dimmed in the tenth month, in the eleventh and in the twelfth, three nights in the winter, and the band remarked that last winter it had not dimmed and the keeper had stood at the margin, and that this winter it dimmed and the keeper had stood at the margin the same. The old said it was seven this year, to six last year, to five and three before, and that they had stopped saying it would come right. The apart clans said the band should part at the next going down.

In the eleventh month the Still One sat by the herds of the two clans for one night at their asking, and did not eat the day after, and was slow among her own for some days. She said the herd was tired in the chest still, and less than last year. The young are wary of her again. She has still not spoken with the keeper.

The Speaker had no chest fever this winter. He is slower, and sleeps through part of the ring when the council is long and wakes to the end of it. The clans talk of who follows him a little louder, and no name has been said aloud.

The year ended with the band at the edge of spring on the way down from the winter woods, with the keeper at the margin of the camp, the horn at its side and a way out behind it, as it has stood the three winters before. It has been asked for a watch, a count, a standing at the hunt's edge, a leave for the wolves, and the reading of a pool and a cough.

## If the Keeper rules the other way

The wolves of the calving, if the hunters sent the same dusk with the calves drawn in to the pens did not turn them on all three nights: about thirty calves lost, the herders saying the leave is worth no more than waiting, the eldest head saying he had said so and that a custom had been given twice for thirty calves, and Tessaly bringing her cord to the river camp short. Cattle in the state numbers fall by about twenty four from the plain case.

The party's beasts at the end of the six weeks, if the keeper's reading showed hurt in part: the Saltkeeper keeps them apart a second month, and if the hurt stays, the beast it reads in is killed and burned at the far end by the old rule, and the band says that the keeper was right again and that the stone towns' cough is in the iron. Nothing in the state numbers moves by more than a goat or two.

The pool: if the next dusk's reading had shown the herd still hurt, the Marchwarden moves the camps up the stream as last year, and the band says the keeper's word moved the camp; no flux comes to the pool in either branch, since the Keeper has said none went bad beyond the ordinary.

## Causes, in short

- The council gave the Houndwarden leave again because the leave had cost it nothing and saved its calves last year, and the eldest head did not stand against it.
- The herders drew the calves in to the pens at the dusk of the reading, which they had not done before, because Tessaly told them it was her word and the council's leave and the dusk was the time.
- The Marchwarden kept the herd off the pool for one night and did not move the camp because the keeper told him itself that one dusk was little, and he weighs a night's herd against a child, as he did last year.
- The salt was about half the wanting because no cattle went to pay for it and the price did not fall.
- The iron was fewer pieces because the Saltkeeper bought only the edges the band could not do without; the band believes the iron is sick.
- The cough took far fewer in the winter because the two clans kept their herds on their own ground, and because they had the far end of the gathering apart before it spread, or because the cough was fewer this year. Nothing in my record says which.
- The council sent no word to the stone towns because the band said the last was a begging, and the Speaker would not have the claim said twice and called the same.
- The star dimmed seven times because the star dimmed seven times. Nothing in my record says why.
