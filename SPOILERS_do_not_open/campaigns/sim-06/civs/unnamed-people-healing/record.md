# People: unnamed

The agreed record of god-healing's people, written by the Keeper on creation Night 3a, 2026-11-24, from the god's own description as confirmed in two rounds. Quoted blocks are the god's words. This record holds what the people are, know and believe; what they believe of their past may be wrong.

Unnamed by their god, and their homes unnamed (the larger seat is unnamed court steading [f55]), until a mortal names them in play.

## Who they are
> Mortal folk of the common kind, river people of the lowland: stocky and weathered, used to wet feet, damp wool and the smell of the marsh. They hold the land in scattered steadings along the slow river and the creeks that feed it, not in one town. They are a patient, practical, dry-humored people who have learned to live beside rot and flood, and who are not fond of strangers or of fuss. They hold the mounds in their fields as their own old work and keep them, without any clear tale of who first raised them.

## How many
About 5,000, in scattered steadings of a dozen to a few score households along the lower River Morrow [f02] and its creeks, with one larger seat (unnamed court steading [f55]). Within the god's size lean. Nothing was argued about size.

## How they live
> They farm the silt of the river banks with barley, beans and flax, set eel and fish traps in the creeks, cut reed and rush from the marsh, keep pigs, geese and a few cattle, and cut timber and fuel from the forest behind. The river floods each thaw and leaves silt, and they plan the year around it. Days are filled with plowing the silt and clearing channels, mending traps and thatch, retting flax, drying fish and reed, and burying and tending their dead. They live in reed-thatched, clay-daubed houses raised on low earth platforms against the flood. Winters are long and hard and it is the season of illness; they lose children and the old most often in the wet autumn and the late winter.

## Their code
> They honor the laying out of the dead: no body is left unburied, no one is turned from a deathbed, and the dying are never refused water. They honor the mounds and the ground of the dead and do not plow them. They honor the midwife, the bonesetter and the one who sits with the dying, even if they find them unclean, and they feed them. The worst of them would still not strike a healer at work, would still not foul a shared well, and would still not leave a sick neighbor outside the door to die. The worst of them is capable of a great deal of greed and cruelty, but not of those.

## Skills and culture
> Free and ordinary: the flood calendar, kept by knotted cords; clay and reed building on raised platforms; eel and fish traps and weirs; flax retting and weaving; brewing of barley beer; the digging of drainage cuts and wells; the cleaning of wells each thaw; a strong custom of boiling water for the sick, whose reason is held as old habit; herb lore of marsh plants, moss and roots kept by the women who attend births and deaths; burial in mounds with grave goods of simple pottery and cloth; funeral songs and a shared meal at each burial. Customs, not edges: nothing here would tell in a contest with another people of the same number, and I ask no pay and offer no weakness for it.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. The god proposed no edge and none was priced; no weakness is owed. The habits and prides above are tempers, able to fail. The force under Helpers is ordinary by the measure in the ruling ledger (L-0030).

## Their magic
Sitters: here and there a midwife or laying-out woman can ease a fever or a hard labor by sitting through a night with a hand on the sufferer, taking a little of the harm into herself; she has the chill and a shake in her hands for days after, and one who does it much grows grey and short-lived. Perhaps one in several steadings, none in many. It cures nothing past an ordinary fever. Ordinary in this world; no edge. No one can make another by telling.

## What they believe about gods
> On the morning before I come, they say that the dead are tended by a quiet one who walks the marsh edge and the mounds, whom they do not name and do not pray to, and whom they leave a bowl of milk or beer at the shrine of the hearth and the wellhead for. They say there are other great powers in the river, the weather and the forest, and that they are not to be trusted or bargained with. They hold the throne in the name of a Tender who has not yet come: an old custom, kept by the line, that a god of mending and rot will one day come and sit, and that until then the seat is kept and not claimed. Most do not think about it; few expect it soon.

## Rule and worship
> Rule: as the leans give it. A sacred monarch of a mortal line, called the Hearth-Holder, holds the throne in the Tender's name and is held sacred for it. A court stands between the throne and the people, and the god is reached through the court. Worship: local shrines only, at the hearth, the well and the grave-ground, kept by whoever keeps them, with no order of priests. I do not depart from either lean.

## Hierarchy
- The Hearth-Holder, of the line: holds the throne for the Tender who has not come; speaks through the court.
- The Reckoner of Bodies: counts the sick and the dead each spring and autumn.
- The Warden of Waters: wells, drains and the flood.
- The Keeper of the Mounds: the grave-ground and its records.
- The Spearward: the levy of about four hundred.
- The Judge-Mother: disputes, with the elder of each steading in the first instance.

## Helpers
Designed, not yet serving, 2026-11-24 (creation Night 3a), by the god's own choice with the cost stated to it. The offices below stand and are filled, each under the people's own custom. None takes the god's word at landing. Each becomes the god's helper when its holder hears the god and takes its word, in play: for an office held for one awaited, when its holder takes the god for that one. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away, its unplayed time runs on its own conduct alone, and the people go their own way under their own court. A bend, not a refusal of a default; not priced; it bought nothing.

The offices are those under Hierarchy. Orders travel by runner and boat along the river and creeks, steading to steading; a word reaches the far end of the people in some days.

## How the god meets them
They are already there. The line keeps the seat for a Tender who has not come; most do not think about it and few expect it soon. They do not know the god. Designed, not yet serving.

## Neighbors and their trouble
Neighbors: few; forest between them and everyone. Salt comes up the river from the gulf in other people's boats. Their trouble: a wasting fever emptied two steadings last winter and the Reckoner's count was worse than any in memory; the thaw flood has been higher three years running; and the court is short of sitters.

## Named mortals
None named. The Hearth-Holder is a person of the line, left to be found in play. Office holders are filed by office.

## What the old tell
Added on creation Night 3b, 2026-11-24. The people's own telling, which may be wrong; nothing agreed on Night 3a is changed.
- That the Morrow Mounds [f56] are all their own people's work. The Keeper of the Mounds knows the oldest are of a different make and says nothing.
- That a sitter named Edda Marsh first boiled water for the sick, because the Tender told her to in a dream.
- That the mound moss was the Tender's gift against the wasting, and that it has grown weak because the seat stands empty.
- That walkers come out of the forest once in a lifetime, sing names at the mounds through a night, and go back into the trees. The fen calls them the night-counters and thinks them a kind of mourner.
