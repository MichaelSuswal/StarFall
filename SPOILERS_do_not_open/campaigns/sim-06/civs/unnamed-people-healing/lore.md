# What the people now say, end of Year 4

Their own telling, which may be wrong. Each saying names who tells it.

## About the stranger
- Hedda's house and the court steading: that the stranger is a great sitter who has stayed, who reads ground, wells and graves as it reads a sufferer, and who walked down the river alone a second summer to read the rest of it. They are wrong that it is only a sitter, and wrong that its reading is a sitter's gift.
- The old sitter of the mounds, and some of the old women: that the stranger said to her face that it does not know, and that a thing which says so is either honest or very careful. They keep both. They are wrong in nothing that is checked, but they have taken its not knowing for a thing it is hiding.
- The three nearest steadings' sitters, split again. The younger sitter says the stranger judges a brew by the body and that this is better than anything the court gave her. The other two say it is a poor help to be told what a body did and not what to give. Both sides are wrong in one way: it said it reads the body and never what is in a cup, and told them to judge the brew themselves.
- The steading of the weir, and the steading of the heap: that the stranger lays hands on the dead's ground and will wake what lies there. They fear it. Wrong: it laid a hand on the soil of the bank graves, as it did on the mounds, and moved and rotted nothing.
- The bank plot's kin: that the stranger came with the Keeper to find which of their dead fouled the wells, and that the rise was made sound to shame them. Wrong: it said it reads what is wrong now and never what brought it, and the Keeper's rows were read to find where the foulness lies.
- The old elder and the kept-well steadings: that old water is good water, and that the rota on the rise is the court's way of drawing the low steadings into its pocket. Wrong: the Reckoner's cords show the kept-well steadings sickened as many as before and the closed ones fewer, a third year running.
- The court's yard, about the walk: that the stranger went to the gulf, or to the sea, or to find who fouled the wells. Wrong: it told the Reckoner what it looked for, and she has told no one what it found.
- The yard of the court, about the knife: that it is for the Spearward's work. Wrong: Hedda has seen it cut bread and cord.

## About the dim star
- Seven nights this year, more than any year, and three in a row at the end. The old women say the nights come oftener each year because the seat is half full and the lamp gutters, and that with three in a row the lamp is going out. Wrong: they were not tied to any sitting, and none fell while the stranger was away on its walk.
- Hedda, who counts, counted the seven against the sendings: four fell in months when the stranger was sent for and three did not. She has said nothing but to the Judge-Mother, when asked. The Judge-Mother said the stars are not hers to judge.
- Children say that the star winks when the quiet one passes, and some say it winks when the stranger walks out under it. Not known.

## About their rulers
- The Hearth-Holder keeps the seat in the Tender's name, as ever. He has sat the stranger at the hearth a third time and asked it nothing. He sent three old women home with beer again when they asked that the seat be filled. The old women say he is stubborn or afraid. Wrong: he has not taken the stranger for the one, and he is not afraid.
- The Reckoner is said to be the stranger's carrier, and to have carried its three askings to the Warden and the Keeper. True in the carrying, wrong in the saying: she carried them and did not say she agreed, and keeps her office to the people.
- The Judge-Mother is said to be soft on the stranger. Wrong: she wants it watched and has found nothing to hold against it.
- The Spearward is said to have been satisfied. Wrong: his two men stand at the grain house still.
- The Warden of Waters says that dry ground makes sound wells, that no well will be dug below the bank graves, and that he would see the three carry the rota before he dug a fourth. He does not say the rise is better than the rest.
- The Keeper of the Mounds says nothing. The court says he said his records aloud on the bank ground for the first time in anyone's life, that he did not speak the dead by name, and that he pegged the rise back from the wells. All true. He knows what the old tellings of the mounds leave out and says nothing of that.

## About the moss and the dead
- The old women of the three nearest steadings say the moss was the Tender's gift against the wasting and has failed with the seat not filled. They have the saying from their grandmothers. The sitters say the moss was only moss. Not known to the people.
- The old sitter says the moss was going before the stranger came, and holds it so because the stranger said it could not say.
- The court says the dead of the bank are fouling the wells. The Keeper's rows were said aloud; the court does not yet agree on what they showed.

## Of neighbors
- Nothing new. Salt came up the river and the boatmen are the only people the steadings saw. No walker came out of the forest this year, and no stranger. The yard says the stranger walked far down the river and met no one, or met everyone. No one knows which.
