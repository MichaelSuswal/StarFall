# What the people now say, end of Year 5

Their own telling, which may be wrong. Each saying names who tells it.

## About the stranger
- Hedda's house and the court steading: that the stranger is a great sitter who has stayed, who reads ground, wells and graves as it reads a sufferer, and who this year walked into the trees alone for five days. They are wrong that it is only a sitter, and wrong that its reading is a sitter's gift. They add this year that it is a sitter who says where it can be found and where it cannot, and who sent a word to the steadings about its going. They are wrong that this is a sitter's manners: it is the first thing it asked of the steadings, and it asked it for itself.
- The elder of the steading below the court, and the steadings that heard the words: that the stranger said a woman in labor must not wait for it and that she should send to her own sitter first, which a decent guest says. They hold it kind and a little late. They are wrong that it was late: it said it as soon as it saw it had been wrong to be silent, and it said it was its own to say.
- The yard of the court, about the words: that the stranger has now given an order. Wrong: the Reckoner carried it as a word, and the Judge-Mother ruled that it bound no steading.
- The old sitter of the mounds, and some of the old women: that the stranger says the same thing twice, that it does not know, once to her face and once to Hedda, and that this is either honest or a habit. They keep both. They are wrong in nothing that is checked, but they have taken its not knowing for a thing it is hiding.
- The old women of the three nearest steadings: that the stranger's sight is closed on the fen's sick because the seat is empty, and that filled, it would see. Not known. The stranger said only that its reading is closed on their own people and that it would ask.
- The three nearest steadings' sitters. The younger says the stranger asks what she saw and says what it can from her words, and that this is the only help that has asked her anything. One of the other two asked Hedda what the younger made of it. The third says nothing. They are wrong in one thing: they take its asking for a way of teaching them, when it said it knows no brewing.
- The steading of the weir, and the steading of the heap: that the stranger lays hands on the dead's ground and will wake what lies there, and that a lamp that does not know whether it is going out is going out. They fear it. Wrong: it laid a hand on the soil of the bank graves, as it did on the mounds, and moved and rotted nothing, and the star stood fewer nights this year than last.
- The bank plot's kin: that the stranger came with the Keeper again to find which of their dead fouled the wells. Wrong: it said it reads what is wrong now and never what brought it.
- The old elder and the kept-well steadings: that old water is good water, and that the rota on the rise is the court's way of drawing the low steadings into its pocket. Wrong: the Reckoner's cords show the kept-well steadings sickened as many as before and the closed ones fewer, a fourth year running.
- The court's yard, about the walk: that the stranger went into the trees to speak with the walkers who sing the names at the mounds, or with the quiet one who walks the marsh edge, or to find out where the forest keeps its own Tender. Wrong: it told the Reckoner what it looked for, and she has told no one what it found.
- The yard of the court, about the knife: that it is for the Spearward's work. Wrong: Hedda has seen it cut bread and cord.
- The yard, about the salt: that the stranger asked the Warden of Waters about salt in the river and was told there was none, and that this is the first thing it has got wrong that everyone could check. Some say it is a good sign, since a thing that can be wrong can be trusted to say so; the Warden says only that salt comes in sacks.

## About the dim star
- Three nights this year, the fewest since the first year, two in the second month and one in the seventh. The old women say the lamp has steadied, some because the seat was spoken for in their asking, some because the stranger went into the trees and not downstream. Wrong: the nights were not tied to any asking, and none fell while it was away.
- Hedda, who counts, counted the three against the sendings: two fell in the moon in which it mended a man, both before he was brought to her, and one did not, and none while it was away. She has told the old women only that it gave her its count and that it agreed with hers. The Judge-Mother has not asked again.
- Children say that the star winks when the quiet one passes, and some say it winks when the stranger walks out under it. Not known.

## About their rulers
- The Hearth-Holder keeps the seat in the Tender's name, as ever. He sat the stranger at the hearth a fourth time and asked it nothing. He sent two old women home with beer. The old women say he is stubborn or afraid. Wrong: he has not taken the stranger for the one, and he is not afraid.
- The Reckoner is said to be the stranger's carrier, and to have carried its words about the sitters and its asking about the salt. True in the carrying, wrong in the saying: she carried them and did not say she agreed, and corrected it on the days it had told her, and keeps her office to the people.
- The Judge-Mother is said to be soft on the stranger. Wrong: she wants it watched and has found nothing to hold against it. She says it is no longer a thing to judge that it goes to the count first.
- The Spearward is said to have been satisfied. Wrong: his two men stand at the grain house still.
- The Warden of Waters says that dry ground makes sound wells, that no well will be dug below the bank graves, that salt comes in sacks and not in the river, and that he would see the three on the rise through another wet season before he dug a fourth. He does not say the rise is better than the rest.
- The Keeper of the Mounds says nothing. The court says he said his rows aloud on the bank ground a second time, this time by month for the rows where his cords hold it, and that he did not speak the dead by name. All true. He knows what the old tellings of the mounds leave out and says nothing of that.

## About the moss and the dead
- The old women of the three nearest steadings say the moss was the Tender's gift against the wasting and has failed with the seat not filled. They have the saying from their grandmothers. The sitters say the moss was only moss. Not known to the people.
- The old sitter says the moss was going before the stranger came, and holds it so because the stranger said it could not say.
- The court says the dead of the bank are fouling the wells, and now that the same ground is foul at the two emptied steadings. The Keeper's rows were said aloud twice; the court does not yet agree on what they showed.
- The families that lost kin in the two emptied steadings say the Warden would first have to dig on the rise, and no longer speak of going back.

## Of neighbors
- Nothing new. Salt came up the river in sacks and the boatmen are the only people the steadings saw. No walker came out of the forest this year, and no stranger. The yard says the stranger went into the trees and met no one, or met everyone. No one knows which.
