# Unnamed people of god-healing: state at the end of Year 4

Set by the civ agent. The numbers take the branch in which the mendings held (about four cases) and in which the Readings found what the Reckoner's winter cords show. If the Keeper rules that the mendings failed in whole or part, deaths rise by up to about 4 and mood falls among the sitters and the near steadings.

population: about 5,175 (births about 210, deaths about 150, in scattered steadings along the lower River Morrow [f02], one larger seat [f55]). Why it moved: no fever winter fell; in the low steadings whose wells were drawn off or closed fewer sickened again, in those that kept their wells as many as before, and about a third of the steadings that still kept wells at the thaw closed or drew them off that spring; the god was sent for about six times against about seven last year, four of them for mendings and two to read a sick one beside a sitter's brew.
stores: barley, beans, dried fish and smoked eel, adequate to the thaw. No steading short of seed. Why: an ordinary thaw, ordinary harvest, ordinary eel and fish runs. The fields of the two emptied steadings still lie unplanted, and talk of going back rose again and came to nothing.
treasure: little: cloth, pots, a few bronze things laid by for burials. Unchanged but for the usual grave goods, some laid on the rise.
trade: flax, linen and smoked eel out; salt in, by river. About five salt boats, as in most years. No new trade. Why: nothing changed the river traffic; no stranger came.
strength: a levy of about 400, little trained; no walls. Unchanged. The Spearward's two men still stand at the court steading's grain house by night. Why: he holds that a promise is not a lock, and has not asked the stranger again.
mood: the Reckoner stands as she stood, and carried the stranger's three askings in the first days of the thaw. The Warden of Waters keeps a rota of about thirty households from the low steadings on the three test wells on the rise, reads his own tries beside the stranger's Readings, and has dug no fourth well. The Hearth-Holder keeps the seat, sat the stranger at the hearth a third time and asked it nothing, and has neither taken its word that it is the one nor refused it; he sent the old women home with beer again. The Judge-Mother watches and has found nothing to hold against it. The Keeper of the Mounds came to the bank ground at the Reckoner's word, said his records aloud, and had the pegs moved back from the wells; he says nothing more. The Spearward asked nothing. The old sitter of the mounds had her answer, that it does not know, and calls it the first that did not lean. The sitters of the three nearest steadings are divided over its offer to read a sick one before and after a brew: one thanks it, two call it a poor help. The weir steading's old women are cold to it still. The elders who kept their wells are sore, and the old elder keeps his. The bank plot's kin are sore at the rota on the rise and at the stranger's hand on their graves. Seven dim nights, three of them in a row at the end of the year, have frightened the old women and some of the steadings; they say the lamp is going out and press the seat to be filled. The far steadings have heard of it and little more. Why: the old sitter's question answered; the rota; the bank days; the walk downstream unseen about twelve days; the nights of the star; a third winter with the low steadings sicker less often.

## Keeper, Year 1, pass two (kept)
Each mending did what the card gives, the Hearth-Holder's granddaughter among them: the numbers take the year file's branch in which the mendings held. The court steading's well and store read sound; the branch for that stands.

## Keeper, Year 2, pass two
- All nine mendings held; the numbers take that branch.
- The moss read as sound living moss. The rise read sound; the first burials stand there. The kept wells below the bank read foul as before; the closed wells fainter, still foul; the emptied steadings' wells foul.

## Keeper, Year 3, pass two
- All seven mendings held, each with its days of shaking after. Your numbers take that branch.
- The three test wells on the rise read sound before any drank and sound at the season's end.
- The old elder's well read foul, as before.
- The summer walk: the river and every creek and spring above the bank burials read clean; the ground under the bank burials and the wells below them read foul; nothing foul comes down from upstream. Your year takes the branch where the foulness lies under the bank graves and the low ground alone.
- The moss read living and sound, with nothing in it that common moss lacks. It found only what the old women knew.
- The old sitter's question had no answer.
