# Unnamed people of god-healing: state at the end of Year 5

Set by the civ agent. The numbers take the branch in which the mendings held (about three cases), in which the Readings beside a brew gave what the Keeper ruled last year, and in which the Readings found what the Reckoner's winter cords show. If the Keeper rules that the mendings failed in whole or part, deaths rise by up to about 3 and mood falls among the sitters and the near steadings.

population: about 5,245 (births about 210, deaths about 140, in scattered steadings along the lower River Morrow [f02], one larger seat [f55]). Why it moved: no fever winter fell; in the low steadings whose wells were drawn off or closed fewer sickened again, in those that kept their wells as many as before, and about a third of the steadings that still kept wells at the thaw closed or drew them off that spring; the god was sent for four times against six last year, three of them for mendings and one to read a sick one beside a sitter's brew.
stores: barley, beans, dried fish and smoked eel, adequate to the thaw. No steading short of seed. Why: an ordinary thaw, ordinary harvest, ordinary eel and fish runs. The fields of the two emptied steadings still lie unplanted, and talk of going back did not rise this year, since the stranger's finding below was told to the Warden, the Judge-Mother and the Keeper.
treasure: little: cloth, pots, a few bronze things laid by for burials. Unchanged but for the usual grave goods, some laid on the rise.
trade: flax, linen and smoked eel out; salt in, by river, in sacks. About five salt boats, as in most years. No new trade. Why: nothing changed the river traffic; no stranger came.
strength: a levy of about 400, little trained; no walls. Unchanged. The Spearward's two men still stand at the court steading's grain house by night. Why: he holds that a promise is not a lock, and asked the stranger nothing at the hearth.
mood: the Reckoner stands as she stood. She carried the stranger's words about the sitters to the steadings as a word and not an order, told it it had said about twelve days to her last summer, and showed the cords to the Hearth-Holder in her own words at the thaw; she is teaching her runner, a girl of the court steading, the cords. The Warden of Waters keeps the rota of about thirty households on the three test wells, dug no fourth well, answered the stranger's word on salt that salt comes in sacks and not in the water, and read his own tries beside the stranger's Readings. The Hearth-Holder keeps the seat, sat the stranger at the hearth a fourth time and asked it nothing, sent two old women home with beer, and asked again to see the cords; he has neither taken its word that it is the one nor refused it. The Judge-Mother says it is no longer a thing to judge that the stranger goes to the count first, ruled that the stranger's words bind no steading, and has found nothing to hold against it. The Keeper of the Mounds came to the bank ground again and said his rows aloud, by month where his cords hold it; he says nothing more. The Spearward asked nothing. The old sitter of the mounds asked nothing and says it said the same thing twice, which is honest or a habit. The sitters of the three nearest steadings: the younger sent once more and says it is the only one who asked what she saw; of the other two, one asked Hedda what the younger made of it and has not sent, and one says nothing. The weir steading's old women are cold to it still, and fewer came to the hall. The elders who kept their wells are sore, and the old elder keeps his. The bank plot's kin are sore at the rota on the rise and at the stranger's hand on their graves, as in Year 4. Three dim nights, two in the second month and one in the seventh, have eased the old women and the steadings; they say the lamp has steadied, and the asking that the seat be filled came once and not three times. The far steadings have heard of the stranger's words about the sitters and little more. Why: the Reckoner's carrying; the stranger's walk west into the trees for five days; the Warden's answer on salt; the bank day; a fourth winter with the low steadings sicker less often; fewer nights of the star.

## Keeper, Year 1, pass two (kept)
Each mending did what the card gives, the Hearth-Holder's granddaughter among them: the numbers take the year file's branch in which the mendings held. The court steading's well and store read sound; the branch for that stands.

## Keeper, Year 2, pass two
- All nine mendings held; the numbers take that branch.
- The moss read as sound living moss. The rise read sound; the first burials stand there. The kept wells below the bank read foul as before; the closed wells fainter, still foul; the emptied steadings' wells foul.

## Keeper, Year 3, pass two
- All seven mendings held, each with its days of shaking after. Your numbers take that branch.
- The three test wells on the rise read sound before any drank and sound at the season's end.
- The old elder's well read foul, as before.
- The summer walk: the river and every creek and spring above the bank burials read clean; the ground under the bank burials and the wells below them read foul; nothing foul comes down from upstream. Your year takes the branch where the foulness lies under the bank graves and the low ground alone.
- The moss read living and sound, with nothing in it that common moss lacks. It found only what the old women knew.
- The old sitter's question had no answer.

## Keeper, Year 4, pass two
- The four mendings held, each with its days of shaking after.
- The Tender's reading is closed on your people's sick; beside a brew it asked the sitter what ailed the sick one and said what it could from her words.
- The bank ground: foul under the rows laid in the wasting winter and since, and in the wells below them; sound under the older rows and on the rise. Your numbers take the branch where the Keeper of the Mounds pegs a limit on rows laid in one place.
- The summer walk: the same foulness under the bank burials of the two emptied steadings and in their wells; the river clean; no other steading or people below within its reach.
- The test wells read sound.

## Keeper, Year 5, pass two
- The three mendings held: the man with the fouled shin, the child, the old man. Each lived; the Tender took each fever and the shake after.
- The reading beside the brew was closed, as last year; it asked the sitter and spoke from her words.
- The bank ground: foul under the wasting winter's rows and those laid on the low bank since, as last year and no less, and the wells below the same; sound under the older rows, under the rows laid on the rise since the peg, and on the rise. The peg's limit stands.
- The walk west: it made about fifteen miles of fen in two and a half days, saw no treeline, read every spring clean, met no one, and was back on the fifth day.
- The test wells read sound.
