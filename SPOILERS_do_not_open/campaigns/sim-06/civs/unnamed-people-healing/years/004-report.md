# Report to the Keeper: Year 4, unnamed people of god-healing

## How the god was met
Spring. Month 1, first days: the Reckoner of Bodies, at her door before any other office, heard what it read last summer and its three askings (two to the Warden of Waters, one to the Keeper of the Mounds). She said she carried what she was asked to carry and not her opinion of it, carried them that week, and did not take back her word of Year 1. The Judge-Mother judged the going to her first for manners a third time. The Warden heard the Reckoner with his arms folded, said the rise was the court steading's ground and the elders' to give, said the rule on wells below the bank graves was his own since Year 2 and needed no carrying, and set a notch for it on his cords. The Keeper of the Mounds was silent a long time and said he would come to the bank after the sowing. Month 2: at the mounds the old sitter was answered to her face; she said it was an answer and the first that did not lean. The sitters of the three nearest steadings heard its offer to read a sick one before and after a brew: the younger took it as more than the court had given, the other two called it a poor help. The Keeper walked beside it, saying little. The elders met (the old elder and the bank plot's kin against) and let about thirty households of the low steadings with closed wells draw from the three wells on the rise by the Warden's rota. Month 3: the Keeper came to the bank, said his records aloud, and stood with it at each grave it read. Neither the Warden nor the Keeper took it for the one awaited.
Summer. Month 5: the Reckoner was told, not asked, that it would walk downstream about twelve days; she asked what it looked for, was answered, and said to be back before the reaping. While it was away no one acted for it. Month 6: the Reckoner heard it first on its return.
Autumn. Month 7: the old women of the weir steading asked that the seat be filled and the Hearth-Holder sent them home with beer. Month 8: the younger sitter sent for it for a brewed child and heard its Readings; the Hearth-Holder sent a runner through the Reckoner and sat it at the hearth a third time, with the Judge-Mother across the fire and the Spearward by the door. No one asked it anything. The Hearth-Holder did not take its word that it was the one and did not refuse it. Neither the Judge-Mother nor the Spearward took its word. Month 9: it read the three wells on the rise before any elder asked, and told the Reckoner and the Warden.
Winter. The sitters sent for it six times in all. Month 12: three old women asked the Hearth-Holder that the seat be filled; he gave no answer; the Judge-Mother asked Hedda what she counted. At the thaw the Hearth-Holder asked again to see the cords. The Reckoner is the only office that has taken its word. The Warden acts on its Readings only. The Keeper acts on them and on his own records.

## What was done in the god's name
The Reckoner of Bodies carried the god's three askings in month 1, as her own errand: two to the Warden, one to the Keeper. The Warden's rota, his notch on his cords, the elders' agreement, the Keeper's coming to the bank and his moved pegs, the Judge-Mother's saying about the stars and the Spearward's two men at the grain house are theirs, in their own offices. In month 5, while it was away, Hedda took no act in its name; she sent two sitters to a labor on her own word, and that is theirs. Nothing else.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper
1. Mendings, four cases across the year, one at a time with rests: a woman of a low steading with a fever past the ninth day (month 2); a small child with the chest rattle (month 8); an old man (month 9); an old woman (month 12). Who tries: the stranger, at Hedda's or an elder's or a sitter's asking, after the sitters have done what they can, by sitting a night with a hand on the sufferer. Success: the fever breaks by morning, the stranger takes the chill and the shake. Failure: the sufferer dies or the stranger is hurt worse than seen. Bearing on it: the sitters sit in the same way and cannot mend past an ordinary fever; the mendings of Years 1 to 3 held, each with days of shaking after; no child of the line fell ill; it mended none while it was away. Written both ways: if mendings fail in whole or part, deaths rise by up to about 4, the Reckoner's winter count is higher by as many, and the sitters send for it less.
2. Readings of a sick one before and after a sitter's brew, two cases: a child with the rattle (month 8, the younger sitter of the three nearest steadings); an old man with the wet chest (month 11, a sitter of one of the three). The god asks you to rule whether the sitters, being my people, close the Reading to it. Written both ways in the year file: the Reading holds and it tells the sitter in plain words what changed in the body, and she judges the brew by that; or it closes and it asks her what ails the sick one and says what it can from her words. What it reads in the body and what changes between is yours. Bearing on it: it said it reads the body and never the brew; the sitters sit and brew common moss and marsh herbs.
3. The bank ground in month 3, with the Keeper's records said aloud (the rows run along the bank from the court steading's side downstream, laid by year, with three hard years: one before the Keeper's time, a flood year, and the wasting winter before Year 1 when two steadings were emptied). It read the soil at the graves he pointed to, oldest, newest and between, and at the wells below each, and the ground at the far end of the rise where he means to lay the next. What it reads is yours. Written both ways: the foulness follows the age of the graves or the number laid in one place, and the Keeper wrote it in his cords, told the Reckoner and pegged a limit on rows laid in one place; or it lies under the low ground alone, whatever the age or number, and the Warden's rule stood and the Keeper went on laying the court steading's dead on the rise. In either branch no grave was moved and none rotted.
4. The summer walk, month 5 to month 6, alone, downstream. Who tries: the stranger searches for the same foulness under other bank burials, and for any other people or steading and what sickness lies among them. Success: it finds and tells the Reckoner plainly. Failure: it finds nothing, which it tells her too. Written both ways: the same foulness lies under other bank burials below, or other people with their own sickness, and the Reckoner told the Warden, the Judge-Mother and the Keeper, and the talk was of what lay below; or it found only clean ground and empty country, and the court said it walked for nothing. If it met people or ground not in my record, that is yours.
5. The three test wells on the rise at the end of the wet season (month 9), read before any elder asked. The release says the water gave as last year, and the Warden's own tries found them as before; I wrote the rota standing. What it read is yours. Written both ways: sound, and the Warden would dig a fourth well in the thaw if the households asked; foul, which the water did not suggest, and he covered that well.
Nothing else in my year is a fight, a search by my people or a persuasion of someone outside my people. The elders' leave, the old elder's refusal, the bank plot's kin, the weir women's asking, the Hearth-Holder's answers and the Judge-Mother's sayings are my people's and are written as they went.

## What turns on a power
The mendings and the shake after each (item 1). The Readings beside a brew and whether they close to it among my people (item 2). The Readings at the bank graves, the wells below them and the rise (item 3). The Readings on the walk (item 4) and of the test wells (item 5). The moss: I wrote nothing beyond the release, which says it cannot be told from common moss by taste or use, and that the old women found it so. That it called the rot out of nothing this year: no carcass or spoiled store lay by a wellhead, none of Hasten the Rot was seen, and it told the old sitter at the mounds it used none on any grave or mound. The seven dim nights of the star (months 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, 11 and 12): not known to be anyone's doing; I wrote that the old women tie them to the half-filled seat and that with three in a row they say the lamp is going out, that none fell while it was away on its walk, and that Hedda counted four in months when it was sent for and three not, and told only the Judge-Mother, who asked.

## What touches anyone else
Salt boats came up the river about five times; flax, linen and smoked eel went down. I took "no stranger came" to leave the ordinary boatmen, and no walker out of the forest came. No party left my people's land but the stranger alone: it set out in month 5 at dawn from the court steading, on foot, with a pack, by the river bank downstream past the two emptied steadings toward the lower reaches and the edge of my people's land and, as it said, as far beyond as was sensible, and my people did not see it for about twelve days by their own count, returning at dusk by the river path early in month 6, before the reaping. I give no distance or time for the way; if you price it longer or shorter, the end of month 5 and the start of month 6 move with it and the reaping runs as written. Nothing was carried to another people. Talk of going back to the two emptied steadings rose and no one went. The runner who came to Hedda in month 5 from a steading below the court was from my own people.

## Asked of the god
The old sitter of the mounds, month 2, at the mounds: her Year 3 question, "Was it you that did this, or was it going already?", answered now in front of her: it does not know. She asked nothing new.
The sitters of the three nearest steadings, month 2, through Hedda: "What should we brew now the moss does nothing?" Answered in front of them: it knows no brewing and cannot teach what it does not know; it offered to read a sick one before and after a brew.
The Keeper of the Mounds, month 3, at the bank, through his own asking of it: "Read the ground at the far end of the rise, where I mean to lay the next." Answered by its going.
The Reckoner, month 5: "What do you look for?" Answered in front of her: whether the same foulness lies under other bank burials, and any other people or steading and what sickness among them.
Hedda, month 5, while the stranger was away: a runner from a steading below the court, for a woman in a hard labor: "Will it come?" Met silence, because it was away. Hedda said she had no answer to give and sent two sitters. By its return the labor was over.
Hedda, for each hard case, four times: whether it would sit with the sufferer. Answered by its going.
The younger sitter, month 8, and a sitter of one of the three, month 11: that it come and read a sick one, before and after her brew. Answered by its coming.
The Hearth-Holder, month 8, by runner through the Reckoner: that it sit at the seat's hearth on the night she named. Answered by its coming.
Three old women asked the Hearth-Holder, not the god, in months 7 and 12, that the seat be filled. Not asked of it.

## Names said aloud
None. The records the Keeper said aloud at the bank were by year, steading, number and row and not by name, and the god's name was not said aloud this year.

## What the people tell their god
The Reckoner, at the thaw, in her own words: "The cords are lower again. Where the wells are closed, fewer took ill twice. Where they were kept, as many as before. A third of those that kept have closed since the cords went round. The old elder keeps his and his people carry from the creek. I hang what I count, and I do not say what it means."
The Warden of Waters: "The three on the rise carry thirty households and I have dug no fourth. I tried the water myself at the end of the wet and it gave as it gave last year. The rule stands that no well is dug below the bank graves. That was mine before you carried it."
The Keeper of the Mounds, if he speaks: "I said my rows aloud once. I moved no body. The pegs are back from the wells. The rest is in my cords."
Hedda, who asked for four hard cases against seven last year and kept its bed after each: "No child of the line fell ill. The star dimmed seven times, three in a row at the end. I counted them against the sendings and I tell you the count: four in months you were sent for and three not, and none while you were gone. The old women say the lamp is going out. I say nothing to them."
The old sitter: "It was an answer."
The sitters of the three nearest steadings: that the younger found the reading of the body more than the court gave her, and the other two want to be told what to give.
The Hearth-Holder, through no one: that the seat is kept and not claimed, and that he asked again to see the cords.
The people at large: that the winter was ordinary, that no fever winter fell, that the barley stood and the eel ran, that salt came up the river about five times, and that no stranger came.
