# Year 2: the unnamed people of god-healing

Written by the civ agent. Month 1 is the first month of spring. What the god's power did is not settled here; where the year turns on it, the line says so, writes it both ways, and the report carries the question to the Keeper.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The thaw was ordinary in height, and the silt lay as it does. The storehouses were thin at the end of the winter and held. The Warden of Waters had the wells cleaned as at every thaw. In the first days of the thaw Hedda saw the shake go out of the stranger's hands, and said nothing of it.

In the first days the stranger, in undyed wool as before, went from Hedda's house across the court's yard to the small house of the Reckoner of Bodies, before any other door, and not to the seat. The yard saw it go. The Reckoner received it at her door. It told her plainly that it had her answer to what she asked in the autumn: it would take no step toward the court without coming to her first, and it said so to her there. It told her the Hearth-Holder had asked it to sit at the seat's hearth and that the sitters had asked it to look at the moss of the mounds. It asked her to carry word to the Hearth-Holder that it would come when she had judged the day, and that it would come with her or after her leave. The Reckoner heard it out, said she would carry the word and judge the day, and went to the Hearth-Holder's hall that week. She told him what the stranger had said and nothing of what it had told her in the summer about the seat. She said he should ask that himself, straight, and that it answers. The Hearth-Holder said the day was hers to name, that he had sent one runner and would send no second, and that the asking stood. The Judge-Mother, hearing that the stranger had gone to the count before the throne, was not sure whether it meant a slight or manners, and decided on manners. Some at the court said the Reckoner had put her office in the stranger's pocket. She did not answer them.

In month 2 the star dimmed, the first of five nights this year. The old women counted it as they count them, and the children were kept in.

Also in month 2 the stranger went to the mounds, 2.9 miles from the seat on foot, out and back in the one day, with Hedda and three sitters of the nearest steadings, two of them the two who had sat in Hedda's evenings the summer before. The old women set the pace. The Keeper of the Mounds, who had watched it from the field edge the spring before, came out to meet them and walked beside it, and said little. At three places on two mounds the stranger stood within a few paces of the moss and looked at it a long while, as it looks at a body. It did not touch the moss and knelt at no mound. It asked the sitters what the moss did before and what it does now, and took their words. The old women told it: that the moss grew thick and dark, that a pinch dried and steeped made the tongue bite and the lip go numb, that a cup of it eased a fevered person in the wasting and let them sleep; and that now it is pale, brews weak, and hardly bites. It mended no one on that walk. What it read in the moss is the Keeper's. Both ways: if the Reading reached the moss and found something, the stranger told the sitters in plain words what it found, and the old women carried it home and weighed it against the saying that the moss is weak because the seat is empty, and did not drop the saying; if the Reading found nothing it could read, it said so in plain words, and the sitters took the moss for something past reading and were glad and afraid of it by turns. Either way the Keeper of the Mounds heard it and said nothing, and the sitters of the three nearest steadings said they had been answered.

In month 3, late in the spring, the stranger went again to the Reckoner's door, and she sent for the Warden of Waters to her house. In her hearing the stranger asked him to bring the Keeper of the Mounds to the bank, and the Reckoner told him he should. The four went to the bank at the village, the Reckoner slowly on her bad hip. There the stranger showed the Warden and the Keeper where the newer graves lie on the low side and where the wet goes down at the end of the thaw. It told them plainly what it had read there the year before: that the ground of the bank graves and the wells of the low steadings are foul with the same foulness. It asked that the new burials be laid on higher ground and that no well be dug below the bank graves. It did not order either. It said it would not undo any laying out, and it did not.

The Warden said, with his arms folded, that no well would be dug below the bank graves while he held the office, and that this was his to say and he said it. The Keeper of the Mounds was silent for a long time. He said the dead lie by their kin, that the bank ground is the court steading's and the steadings below keep their own and he keeps only their records, and that he would not move one laid body. Then he said that for those whose kin would have it, the next burials of the court steading would be laid on the rise behind the court's yard, which the old women call dry ground, and that this was a trial. He asked the stranger to stand on the rise and say what the ground was. A few days later the stranger stood there with the Keeper and the Warden. What it read is the Keeper's. Both ways: if the rise read sound, the Keeper pegged the first rows that week, and the rest of the year runs as written below; if it read foul, the Keeper chose no rise this year, no one was laid on higher ground, the burial quarrel of month 7 never rose, and the bank ground went on as before, with the Warden's rule on wells alone standing.

In the same month a labor went wrong in a steading below the court and was brought to Hedda's door. The sitters sat and could not hold it. Hedda sent to the stranger, and it sat the night with its hand on the woman. This is the first mending of the year, and only the stranger's power can say how it came out (see the report). The people saw the woman come through, as they saw it last year, and the stranger kept to the corner of Hedda's house some days after, with the shake.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The summer was ordinary. The barley stood, the beans did well, the eel traps were full in the low creeks. A salt boat came up to the court steading's landing, then another, and flax, linen and smoked eel went down. The boatmen went on as they do. In month 4 a child of one of the near steadings took the early rattle and the sitters failed, and Hedda sent. The stranger sat with it once and rested. This is the second mending of the year.

The Reckoner named the day in month 5, when the flood was down and the harvest had not begun. She went with the stranger at dusk to the seat's hall. The Hearth-Holder had his household along the walls and sat the stranger where a guest sits, and fed it, and it ate what was set. The Judge-Mother sat across the fire and watched, as she had said the court would. The Spearward, whom the stranger had not met, sat by the door.

The Hearth-Holder is not a man who asks, and he asked once, straight, whether it was the one the seat is kept for. It answered him truly, in front of the hearth, and the Reckoner, the Judge-Mother and the Spearward heard the answer. Nobody spoke for a time. He asked its name and was told it, and the three who sat there heard that too. The Judge-Mother asked what it would do if the court bade it go from the fen. The Spearward asked whether it could do to a granary what it did to the heap behind the grain house. Each was answered in front of the hearth. The stranger asked nothing of the court, and no one asked it to take the seat. The Hearth-Holder said that it was welcome at the seat's hearth as long as it would sit there. He said he kept the seat as his line keeps it, and that a keeper does not hand over what he keeps on a word, however the word is answered, but that he would not hinder it, and that he would see what the year made of it. He did not take its word that it was the one. He did not refuse it. The Reckoner spoke once, to say that the court should watch it and not fear it. The Judge-Mother said afterward that the stranger had not hidden anything she asked about, and that she liked that less than if it had. The Spearward said nothing of what he thought of the answer, and the court did not hear him say it again.

In month 6 the star dimmed a second time this year. The court noted that it fell the month after the hearth sitting.

In month 6 an old man of the court steading died, and his sons, who had his word that he would lie on the rise, laid him there, the first. The rest of his line, who keep the line's plot at the bank, said that he was cut from his fathers. Hedda heard it said at her door and told the Keeper.

At the end of month 6 the stranger set out downstream, by the river bank, for the two steadings emptied by the wasting fever two winters back and for the steadings below the bank burials. A sitter of one of the nearer steadings, an old woman who had sat in Hedda's evenings, said she would go, and went. No distance or time is written here; the Keeper prices the way.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

In the low steadings the stranger read the wells and the soil about them, knelt at each wellhead as it had at the court steading's, and told the elder and the sitter what it found and what it asked: that what was foul be drawn off or closed and creek water carried. It did not ask the empty steadings to be resettled. It read their wells and the ground about them and told the sitter what it read. What it read in each is the Keeper's. Both ways: where a well read foul, it said so, and the elders who had kept their wells the autumn before were asked a second time; where a well read sound, it said so and the well stayed. Of the low steadings that had kept their wells the year before, about half closed or drew off this autumn, because the sitter beside it spoke for it and the Warden's rule at the court was known. The rest did not. One elder, an old man whose father dug his well, said in the open that his people had drunk it all their lives and would not be told by a stranger to carry water like children in the wet season, and asked why. The stranger answered him in front of his steading. He kept the well. Talk of going back to the two empty steadings rose among families who lost kin there, and the Warden said it was not his to say and that the stranger had said nothing of it. Nobody went. The stranger and the sitter came back to the court steading before the wet-season illnesses began in month 8, so far as my year runs; if the Keeper prices the way longer, the autumn tending below moves later.

In month 7 the burial quarrel came up. The old man's brother's line would not have his name struck from the bank plot, and the sons would not have him moved. The elder of the court steading sent it to the Judge-Mother. In month 8 she ruled that a man lies where his sons lay him, that the line's plot at the bank is for those who choose it, and that the Keeper of the Mounds shall write each name in his records at both places, so that none is cut from his fathers by being laid elsewhere. The Keeper said it was what he would have asked, and did it. The Judge-Mother said it was not to be laid to the stranger either to blame or to praise. Others were laid on the rise after that, by the winter's end about eight of the court steading's and the nearest three, and the bank ground was still used by those who would.

In month 8 the wet-season illnesses began, the chest rattle in the small and the old. The stranger went among the steadings one at a time and tended the children and the old whom the sitters could not. In that month a drowned ox, swept down in a spate, lodged in a creek weir above the wellhead of a steading, and the men could not drag it free in the flood. The stranger, called, put its hands to it once, that afternoon, and the people saw it go down to black slurry and the smell rise and sink, as with the heap the year before, and it did the same to nothing else all year. It mended no one that day or the next night and sat with its back to a wall. Whether the carcass went as the people saw is the stranger's power and is carried to the Keeper. The steading said it was done as the heap was done. The Warden heard it and said the man had done well to call, and that the thing was not to be done for what could be dragged out.

Also in month 8 two were mended at Hedda's asking after the sitters failed: a small child with the chest rattle, and an old woman with a fever that would not break. In month 9 a third, an old man. The stranger rested between each.

In month 9 the star dimmed a third time this year. The Reckoner took her autumn count at the end of the month with the Keeper of the Mounds, as for twenty years. He told her the number laid on the rise. She said nothing to the stranger of what the count showed, only that she would wait for the winter.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

An ordinary sickness winter: coughs, agues, the old and small taken ill. The wasting fever did not return. The stranger stayed among the steadings of the long sickness, and the sitters sent for it fewer times than the winter before. In month 10 it sat with one, in month 11 with one, in month 12 with two, each when the sitters had done what they could, each with long rests between. It mended no child of the line, for none of the line fell ill. The Hearth-Holder's household, the girl of five among them, kept well. The star dimmed in month 11 and in month 12, the fourth and fifth nights of the year, and the old women, who say the nights come oftener now, talked of little else for a month.

At the thaw, at the year's end, the Reckoner took her winter count. In the low steadings whose wells had been drawn off or closed, fewer sickened than the winter before. In the low steadings that kept their wells, as many sickened as before. She showed her cords to the Warden in the court's yard, the two side by side, and he said nothing for a long time and then said he would send to the steadings that kept. The Judge-Mother asked to see the cords too, and was shown. The Reckoner kept her office to the people.

At the thaw meal the Hearth-Holder said in the open, for the first time, that he had sat the stranger at his hearth and that it was welcome there, and said nothing of what it was. The old women whispered afterward that he had taken it for the Tender. He had not.

The year closed on the thaw, the flood not yet up.

## What the year cost and bought

Nothing was done in the god's name beyond the errands it gave the Reckoner, which she carried, and the Warden's coming to the bank at her asking, which he did as her asking and not its own. The Warden's rule on wells and the Keeper's rise are theirs, set in their own offices. The stranger gave no order. The court is nearer the stranger than it was and no nearer to calling it what the line keeps the seat for. Eight of the dead lie on the rise, and a quarrel was settled by writing each name twice. Fewer were sent for the stranger than last year, about nine against about thirty.
