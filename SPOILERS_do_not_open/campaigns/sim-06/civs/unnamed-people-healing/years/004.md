# Year 4: the unnamed people of god-healing

Written by the civ agent. Month 1 is the first month of spring. What the god's power did is not settled here; where the year turns on it, the line says so, writes it both ways, briefly, and the report carries the question to the Keeper. The numbers take the branch in which the mendings held and in which the Readings found what the Reckoner's cords show. Where the Keeper's line on Year 3 differs from my Year 3 file, the Keeper's line stood: all seven mendings held, the test wells read sound, the old elder's well read foul, the foulness lies under the bank graves and the low ground alone, and the moss was common.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The thaw was ordinary in height. The storehouses were thin at the end of the winter and held, and no steading was short of seed. The Warden of Waters had the wells cleaned as at every thaw. The shake was out of the stranger's hands from the thaw, as it had gone out the three springs before. It came out of the winter in undyed wool, with the bone-handled knife at its belt, which Hedda saw it use on bread and cord only. No stranger came to the fen this year but the boatmen.

In the first days the stranger went from Hedda's house to the small house of the Reckoner of Bodies by the court's yard, before any other door, and the yard saw it go. It told her plainly what it had read on its walk the summer before: that the river and every creek and every spring above the bank burials read clean, that the old mounds on higher ground read sound, and that the foulness begins at the newest bank graves and stops where the low ground's wells stop. It said it did not know what brought it, only where it lies. It asked her to carry two askings in its words to the Warden of Waters: that the households nearest the rise who now draw from the three test wells be joined by the low steadings' households, as the elders agree; and that no well be dug on ground below the bank graves. It asked her to carry one more to the Keeper of the Mounds: that he let it stand with him on the bank ground and the mounds and read it, grave by grave, while he told it from his records when each was laid. The Reckoner said, dry as ever, that she carried what she was asked to carry and did not carry her own opinion of it, and that the Warden would not like the first. She did not take back her word of Year 1. The Judge-Mother, hearing it had gone to the count before the throne a third time, judged it manners, as before. In the yard two or three said the Reckoner had her office in the stranger's pocket. She did not answer them.

She went to the Warden that week. He heard her with his arms folded. He said the rise was the court steading's ground and the elders' to give, that a well is not a ladle to be passed round, and that he had meant to speak at the elders' meeting in any case, since his own account of the three wells matched what the stranger read. On the second asking he said he had set that rule himself in Year 2 and it needed no carrying. He had it spoken again to the elders anyway, and set a notch for it on his own cords at the Reckoner's house. She carried the third asking to the Keeper of the Mounds the same week. He was silent a long time. He said he would think on it and that she should say he would come to the bank after the sowing.

In month 2 the old women of the three nearest steadings went to the mounds at the first green, as they do each year, and took a pinch of the moss, dried and steeped it, and then a pinch of common moss from the marsh edge. They were the same, as the year before. They went the second time before the end of the month, to be sure, and came away saying little. The stranger went to the mounds in that month, 2.9 miles from the seat on foot, out and back in the one day, with Hedda and the sitters, and the old women set the pace. The Keeper of the Mounds came out and walked beside it, as he had the years before. At the moss the old sitter who had asked the stranger the year before whether it was it that had done this, or whether the moss was going already, asked nobody anything. The stranger spoke to her, to her face, in front of the others. It said it did not know. It said it read what is wrong now and never what brought it, that it had laid no hand on the moss and had called the rot out of no grave and no mound, that it had found nothing in the moss that common moss lacks, and that it could not say whether the moss was already going. The old sitter was quiet a while. She said that was an answer, and that it was the first she had had that did not lean. She said no more and walked back with the others. The sitters of the three nearest steadings asked Hedda, who asked for them, what they should brew now the moss did nothing. The stranger said to them through Hedda that it knew no brewing and could not teach what it did not know. It said that if they sent for it when they had brewed for a sick one, it would read that sick one before the brew and after, and tell them in plain words what changed in the body, and that they might judge the brew from that. Two of the three sitters said it was a poor kind of help. The third, a younger woman, said it was more than the court's yard had given them in years. The old women of the weir steading, who keep from the mounds since the year before, heard of it and did not change.

Also in month 2 the elders of the court steading met, with the elders of three of the low steadings who came by boat and on foot for it, on the Warden's asking. The Reckoner had hung her winter cords in the hall again, the closed-well steadings beside the kept-well ones. The Warden put the first asking as the elders' to settle and not his. A low steading's elder said his people carried creek water in the wet season in baskets and pots on their backs and would drink from a well on dry ground if the court allowed. The old elder of the kept-well steading, whose father Tolm dug his well, said he wanted nothing from the rise, and said before the hall that old water is good water. The kin who keep the line's plot at the bank said again that wells were dug for the court while theirs were called foul. The elders agreed, with the old elder and the bank plot's kin against, that households of the low steadings that had closed or drawn off their wells might draw from the three wells on the rise, in turn, under a rota the Warden kept, about thirty households. No new well was dug this year, for the Warden said three wells could carry that many and he would see them carry it before he dug a fourth. The Keeper of the Mounds said he would have the pegs moved back a little from the wells, for the rota would put more feet on the ground, and no one disputed it.

In month 2 a woman of a low steading, with a fever that would not break on the ninth day, was brought to Hedda's door after the sitters failed. Hedda sent to the stranger, and it sat the night with its hand on her. This is the first mending of the year, and only the stranger's power can say how it came out (see the report). The people saw the woman come through, and the stranger kept to the corner of Hedda's house some days after, with the shake. In the same month the star dimmed, the first of seven nights this year. The old women counted it as they count them and the children were kept in. Hedda noted that a mending fell in the same month.

In month 3, after the sowing, the star dimmed a second time. In that month the Keeper of the Mounds came to the bank ground. The Reckoner's word had reached him, and he said at the bank that he had thought on it and that he, too, would sooner know. He brought his records. They are knotted cords hung by year and by row, with the dead's steading marked and the rows pegged on the ground, and the oldest set he had from the Keeper before him. The stranger cannot read cords, so Hedda stood at his elbow with the Reckoner's runner, a girl of the court steading, and the two of them set his words in order as he said them aloud: the year, the steading, the number laid, the row. He did not say the dead by name on the bank ground, for it is not the custom there. The rows run along the bank from the court steading's side downstream, laid in the order of the years, a few in an ordinary year and many in a hard one. There were three hard years in the cords: one before his own time, a flood year, and the wasting winter before Year 1, when two steadings were emptied and the bank took more than it had taken in any year before. He pointed to graves, oldest and newest and some between, and the stranger went to each, knelt, and laid a bare hand on the soil. The kin of the line's plot stood by, and an old woman among them spat. She said nothing else. The stranger told them it laid no hand on the dead and moved nothing, and that it would say to them what it read. The Warden stood at the wells below each row. He had the wells below each grave pointed out in turn, and the stranger stood at each wellhead after the grave and said in plain words what it read. At the end the Keeper asked it to read the ground at the far end of the rise, where he means to lay the court steading's next dead, and it did. What it read at every grave, well and the rise is the Keeper's. Both ways: if the foulness follows the age of the graves or the number laid in a place, the stranger said so, the Keeper wrote it in his cords that week, the Reckoner was told, and the Keeper pegged a limit on the rows laid in one place; if it lies under the low ground alone, whatever the age or number, the stranger said so, the Warden's rule on wells stood and the Keeper went on laying the court steading's dead on the rise, for those whose kin would have it. In either branch no grave was moved and none was rotted, and the bank plot's kin kept to their ground.

Also in month 3 the sowing went in. The storehouses had held.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The summer was ordinary. The barley stood, the beans did well, the eel traps were full in the low creeks. Salt boats came up to the court steading's landing about five times and flax, linen and smoked eel went down. The boatmen went on as they do. In month 4 the star dimmed the third time. No one was mended within that month. The old women said the nights come oftener each year, and that the seat is half full.

In month 5, at midsummer, the stranger came to the Reckoner's door and told her, not asking, that it would go on a walk alone, downstream along the river past the two emptied steadings, to the lower reaches and to the edge of the people's land and as far beyond as was sensible, for about twelve days. She asked what it looked for. It said it looked for whether the same foulness lies under other bank burials, and whether there was any other people or steading down there, and what sickness was among them. She said it should be back before the reaping and that she would hold her tongue until it had told her what it found. It set out at dawn from the court steading, on foot, alone, with a pack, by the river bank downstream. No one went with it and no one followed. My people did not see it for about twelve days by their own count, as far as my year goes. The Keeper prices the way and the days; if he prices it longer, the end of month 5 and the start of month 6 move with it, and the reaping runs as written. If its walk met ground or people not in my record, that is not mine to write. The court said it had gone to ground again, or to the sea, or to find who had fouled the wells. Hedda kept its bed ready.

While it was away, in the second week of month 5, a runner came to Hedda's door from a steading below the court. A woman was in a hard labor and the sitters could not move it, and he asked for the stranger. Hedda said it was away and could not say when it would be back. She said she had no answer to give. She sent two of the sitters of the nearest steading, the old sitter among them, who sat the night in the way they sit, with the chill and the shake after. The woman bore a living child on the second day and was weak after. The child lived. It is not the stranger's act and it was not its mending. The runner took Hedda's word and went home. In the yard some said the stranger's being away had cost the woman her strength, which is a thing one can say of any hard labor.

It came in at dusk by the river path, early in month 6, and went to the Reckoner's door before any other. What it told her, how far it went and what ground it saw are the Keeper's. Both ways: if the same foulness lies under other bank burials below, or if it found other people with their own sickness, the Reckoner told the Warden and the Judge-Mother and the Keeper what it said, the people's own wells stood as ruled, and the talk was of what lay below; if it found only clean ground and empty country, she told them that, which changed nothing in the cords, and the court said it had walked for nothing. If it met any people in my record's silence or beyond it, that is not mine to write. The star dimmed none of the nights it was gone.

The reaping took every hand in month 6, the stranger's among them for some days.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

In month 7, after the first frost, the old women of the three nearest steadings went to the mounds and found the moss as before. They did not go to the stranger and did not ask the Hearth-Holder anything. Some of the weir steading's women came to the court's hall to say that the seat should be filled, and the Judge-Mother sent them to the Hearth-Holder, and he sent them home with beer.

The wet season began in month 8: the chest rattle in the small and the old. In that month the star dimmed, the fourth night of the year, and the stranger went among the steadings one at a time. It tended with broth and its hands the children and the old that the sitters could not, and sat the night with one of them: a small child with the chest rattle. This is the second mending of the year. In the same month the younger sitter of the three nearest steadings brewed for a child with the rattle and sent for the stranger as it had said. The stranger came to the child before the brew was given, put its hand to the child's chest and was still, and said to the sitter in plain words what it read in the body. It came again after the cup had been taken and said again what it read, and told her what had changed. The sitter said afterward to Hedda that it spoke as a reader of water speaks of a well. What it read, and what changed, are the Keeper's. Both ways: if the Reading held for the sick of the sitters' own steading, the stranger told the sitter in plain words what changed in the body and the sitter judged her brew by that and told the other two; if the Reading closed to it because the sitter is one of the people's, it asked her what ailed the child, took her words, and said what it could from those. The sitters of the three nearest steadings said to each other in either branch that the stranger judged the brew by the body, which is not what it said, for it said it reads the body and never what is in a cup.

The Hearth-Holder sent a runner to the Reckoner in month 8, as he had the year before, to say that the stranger might sit at the seat's hearth again, on the night she named. She named it near the end of the month, and went with the stranger at dusk to the seat's hall. The household sat along the walls and the Judge-Mother sat across the fire. The Spearward sat by the door. No one asked the stranger anything. The Hearth-Holder said it was welcome as long as it would sit there and that the seat was kept and not claimed. He did not take its word that it was the one the seat is kept for, because it did not give it to him and he did not ask. The Judge-Mother said afterward that it had said nothing and she liked that better than less. The Spearward said nothing in the hall. His two men stood at the court steading's grain house that night as on every night since the year before.

At the end of the wet season, in month 9, the stranger went on its own to each of the three wells on the rise, before any elder asked, and read each and told the Reckoner and the Warden what it read. The Warden had tried the water by his own ways beside it, as he does, and found the wells as he had found them the year before, and the thirty households of the rota drew on without a sickness among them that the Reckoner could trace to the water. What the stranger read is the Keeper's; the water gave as last year. Both ways: if it read sound, the Warden let the rota stand and said he would dig a fourth well in the thaw if the households asked; if it read foul, which the water did not suggest, he covered that well and the rota drew from the two. Also in month 9, the stranger sat with an old man whom the sitters had failed. This is the third mending of the year. In that month the Reckoner took her autumn count with the Keeper of the Mounds. She said nothing to the stranger of what it showed, only that the wet season's rattle had been fewer again in the low steadings that had closed their wells, and as many as before in those that kept them.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

An ordinary sickness winter: coughs, agues, the old and small taken ill. No fever winter fell. The stranger stayed among the steadings through the long sickness, going where the need was greatest, mending only what the sitters had failed at, one at a time with long rests, and no child of the line fell ill. The Hearth-Holder's household kept well, the girl of seven among them. The star dimmed in month 10, in month 11 and in month 12, the fifth, sixth and seventh nights of the year, and the old women said the nights had never come three in a row, and talked of little else. Some said the lamp was going out and that the seat must be filled before the thaw. Three old women of the nearest steadings went to the seat's hall in month 12 and said so to the Hearth-Holder. He heard them and sent them home with beer, and gave no answer. Hedda told the Judge-Mother what she had counted, when the Judge-Mother asked her, and the Judge-Mother said that the stars were not hers to judge.

In month 11 a sitter of one of the three nearest steadings sent for the stranger for a sick one she had brewed for, an old man with the wet chest. It read him before the brew and after, and told the sitter what changed. What it read and what changed are the Keeper's, and the line above stands (see month 8). In month 12 it sat with one, an old woman, after the sitters had failed. This is the fourth mending of the year, four against seven last year and nine the year before, and two Readings of the sick beside a brew. The sitters of the three nearest steadings sent for it six times in all, against seven the year before, which was the count the stranger said it wished.

At the thaw, at the year's end, the Reckoner took her winter count. In the low steadings that had closed or drawn off their wells, fewer sickened again than the winter before. In the low steadings that kept their wells, as many sickened as before. The total was lower. She showed the cords to the Warden and to the Judge-Mother, and to the Hearth-Holder, who had asked again. A third of the steadings that still kept wells closed or drew them off before the sowing, because the cords went round with the runner and the elders counted. The old elder kept his well, and a number of his households carried creek water, as they had.

The year closed on the thaw, the flood not yet up.

## What the year cost and bought

The people followed no order of the god this year, for it gave none. The Reckoner carried its three askings in month 1, as her own errand. The Warden's rota on the rise, his notch on his cords, the elders' agreement, the Keeper's coming to the bank and his moved pegs, the Judge-Mother's saying on the stars and the Spearward's men at the grain house are theirs, set in their own offices. The old sitter had her answer. The sitters of the three nearest steadings have a way to judge their brews, if the Reading holds for them. The low steadings are sicker less often than any winter the cords hold. The court is as near the stranger as it was, and no nearer to calling it what the line keeps the seat for.
