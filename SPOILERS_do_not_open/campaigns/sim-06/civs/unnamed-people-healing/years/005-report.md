# Report to the Keeper: Year 5, unnamed people of god-healing

## How the god was met
Spring. Month 1, first days: the Reckoner of Bodies, at her door before any other office, heard it tell her four things and ask three. She told it the woman and the child of last summer lived. She said it had told her about twelve days at her own door and that Hedda had been given none, which is where its words rested on something else, and she said she was saying what was and not that it was wrong. She said she had never needed its leave to show the cords. She carried its words about the sitters to the elder of the steading below the court and to two other steadings that asked, as a word and not an order, and carried its word on the salt to the Warden of Waters. She did not take back her word of Year 1. The Judge-Mother said its going to her first was no longer a thing to judge, and ruled when the elders asked that its words bound no steading. The elder of the steading below the court said a woman in labor never waited for a stranger and that it was a decent thing for a guest to say. The Warden heard the salt word with his arms folded and said salt does not come up the river but in sacks, that none is poured in the water, and that he would not send a runner for a sack; the landing was there if it wished to read it. Month 2: at the mounds the stranger told Hedda its star count and asked her to keep counting by the month and tell the old women. Hedda said that of the Year 3 pairs only one night fell while it was away, and told the old women what it said. The old sitter said it said the same thing twice, which is honest or a habit. The sitters of the three nearest steadings heard its offer and its word that its reading is closed on their own people: the younger said she would send as before, one of the other two asked Hedda why it should be so, the third said nothing. The Keeper walked beside it, saying little. Month 3: the Keeper came to the bank at his own thinking, after the Reckoner told him the rows since his peg were unread, said his rows aloud again by year, steading, number and row and by month where his cords hold it, and stood with it at each grave it read. Neither the Warden nor the Keeper took it for the one awaited.
Summer. Month 5, first day: the Reckoner was told the days of a walk west into the trees, five at the longest, and what it looked for; she sent runners to the steadings and said it should be back before the reaping. While it was away no one acted for it. Month 5, fifth day: the Reckoner heard it first on its return.
Autumn. Month 7: two old women asked the Hearth-Holder that the seat be filled and he sent them home with beer. Month 8: the Hearth-Holder sent a runner to the Reckoner and sat it at the hearth a fourth time, with the Judge-Mother across the fire and the Spearward by the door. No one asked it anything. He did not take its word that it was the one and did not refuse it. Neither the Judge-Mother nor the Spearward took its word. Month 9: the younger sitter sent for it for a brewed old woman and heard it ask what ailed her; it read the three wells on the rise before any elder asked and told the Reckoner and the Warden. The Warden told it at the third well that no salt had come and never would.
Winter. The Hearth-Holder, the Judge-Mother and the Warden did not meet it beyond the thaw. At the thaw it gave Hedda its count and she gave it hers. The Hearth-Holder asked again to see the cords and the Reckoner told him in her own words what she counts, and he did not ask for it. The Reckoner is the only office that has taken its word. The Warden acts on its Readings only. The Keeper acts on them and on his own records.

## What was done in the god's name
The Reckoner of Bodies carried the god's words about the sitters to the elder of the steading below the court and to two steadings that asked, and its word on the salt to the Warden, as her own errand; she told the Hearth-Holder what she counted in her own words, as it asked and as she would have done. She sent runners in month 5 to tell the steadings the days of its walk and that its words stood. Hedda, in month 5, gave a runner from a low steading its words, "send to your own sitter first", and sent her apprentice with him to carry broth and sit up with the household; that was her own act. The Judge-Mother's ruling that its words bind no steading, the Warden's answer on salt, the Keeper's coming to the bank, the Spearward's two men at the grain house and the Hearth-Holder's asking to see the cords are theirs, in their own offices. The Reckoner's word to the Keeper at the thaw, that the rows since his peg were unread and that it would stand with him, was her own and was not its asking. Nothing else.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper
1. Mendings, three cases across the year, one at a time with rests: a man of a low steading whose adze cut of the shin had gone red and hot to the knee by the ninth day, after the sitters sat two nights (late month 2); a small child of a steading by the river with the chest rattle (month 8); an old man of a steading near the weir with the wet chest (month 11). Who tries: the stranger, at Hedda's asking, after the sitters have done what they can, by sitting a night with a hand on the sufferer. Success: the fever breaks by morning, the stranger takes the chill and the shake. Failure: the sufferer dies or the stranger is hurt worse than seen. Bearing on it: the sitters sit in the same way and cannot mend past an ordinary fever; the mendings of Years 1 to 4 held, each with days of shaking after; no child of the line fell ill; it mended none while it was away; the wounded man's wound was red and hot on the ninth day, and a fouled wound is a roll against death in the first ten days by the public work table. Written both ways: if mendings fail in whole or part, deaths rise by up to about 3, the Reckoner's winter count is higher by as many, and the sitters send for it less.
2. The Reading beside a brew, one case: an old woman with the wet chest (month 9), the younger sitter of the three nearest steadings, read before and after. The god expects the Reading closed on my people's sick and asks the sitter what ails her; it said it would, and did. What it reads in the body and what it says from her words is yours. Written both ways in the year file: the Reading closes as in Year 4, it asks and says what it can from her words, and she judges the brew by her own eyes and that; or it holds for the sick of the sitter's own steading and it tells her in plain words what changed in the body.
3. The bank ground in month 3, with the Keeper's records said aloud again, by month where his cords hold it (the wasting winter's rows, which the Keeper before him knotted by the moon, and the rows laid since the rise began in Year 2; the rest by year). It read the soil at the graves he pointed to, oldest, newest and between, at the rows laid since his Year 4 peg, at the wells below each, and the ground on the rise where he lays now. What it reads is yours. Written both ways: the foulness fades or holds under the older rows and the rows since the peg read sound, and the peg's limit stood and he went on laying on the rise; or it has spread to rows laid since the peg or toward the rise, and he pegged the rise back and laid no more at its near end. In either branch no grave moved and none rotted.
4. The summer walk, month 5, west to the treeline and into the forest no further than a day and a half, alone. Who tries: the stranger looks for the forest's edge, any other people, and any sickness the steadings do not have, reading the water at each spring and any burial ground or midden. Success: it finds and tells the Reckoner plainly. Failure: it finds nothing, which it tells her too. Written both ways: only edge, clean springs and empty ground, and the court said it walked for nothing; or sickness, a burial ground or people not in my record, and the talk was of what lay in the trees. If it met people or ground not in my record, that is yours.
5. The three test wells on the rise at the end of the wet season (month 9), read before any elder asked. The release says the water gave as last year, and the Warden's own tries found them as before; I wrote the rota standing. What it read is yours. Written both ways: sound, and the Warden dug no fourth before the thaw; foul, which the water did not suggest, and he covered that well and the rota drew from two.
Nothing else in my year is a fight, a search by my people or a persuasion of someone outside my people. The elders' answers, the Warden's answer on salt, the Judge-Mother's ruling, the old women's asking and the Hearth-Holder's answers are my people's and are written as they went.

## What turns on a power
The mendings and the shake after each (item 1). The Reading beside a brew and whether it closes (item 2). The Readings at the bank graves, the wells below them, the rows since the peg and the rise (item 3). The Readings on the walk (item 4) and of the test wells (item 5). The moss: I wrote nothing beyond the release, which says it cannot be told from common moss; the old women found it so in months 2 and 7, and the stranger read none of it. That it called the rot out of nothing: no carcass or spoiled store lay by a wellhead, none of Hasten the Rot was seen, and it did not mend on any day after. The salt: its words to the Warden rest on salt standing in the river; my people say it does not, and the Warden sent no runner and no reading of salt-touched water was made, so the god's ask for a ruling on that has nothing in my year to rest on. The three dim nights of the star (month 2 twice, month 7): not known to be anyone's doing; I wrote that the old women say the lamp has steadied and tie it to the seat being spoken for or to its going into the trees; that none fell while it was away; that two fell in the moon in which it mended a man (both before he was brought to Hedda) and one in a moon in which it mended no one; and that Hedda gave it her count at the thaw and it agreed with its own.

## What touches anyone else
Salt boats came up the river about five times, with salt in sacks; flax, linen and smoked eel went down. I took "no stranger came" to leave the ordinary boatmen, and no walker out of the forest came. No party left my people's land but the stranger alone: it set out at dawn on the ninth day of month 5 from the court steading, on foot, with a pack, by the western side across the fields and the marsh edge toward the trees on the west, and went into the forest no further than a day and a half as it said; my people did not see it for five days by their own count, and it returned at dusk of the fifth day by the western track. I give no distance or time for the way; if you price it longer or shorter, the end of the walk moves with it and the reaping runs as written. Nothing was carried to another people. The Reckoner's runners carried its words about the sitters and the days of its walk to steadings of my own people by runner and boat. The runner who came to Hedda in month 5 from a low steading was of my own people.

## Asked of the god
The old women of the three nearest steadings, month 2, at the mounds, through Hedda: whether the lamp is going out. Answered in front of Hedda and in their hearing: it does not know what the dimming is, it did not light it, and it does not know the star is going out.
The sitters of the three nearest steadings, month 2, through Hedda: one of the three, "why should it be so", that its reading is closed on their own people. Not answered; Hedda said she did not know. The younger asked nothing. The offer to read before and after a brew and to ask what ails the sick one was made to them and not asked of it.
The elder of the steading below the court, month 1, through the Reckoner: whether his steading should send to it or to its own sitter in a hard labor. Answered by its words, already carried.
The Reckoner, month 5: "What do you look for?" Answered in front of her: the forest's edge, any other people, and any sickness the steadings do not have.
Hedda, month 5, while it was away: a runner from a low steading, on the third day, for a child with the early rattle: whether it would come. Met silence, because it was away; the standing words answered it: Hedda said she had no answer to give and that he had its words, send to your own sitter first, and sent her apprentice with him. By its return the fever had broken.
Hedda, for each hard case, three times: whether it would sit with the sufferer. Answered by its going.
The younger sitter, month 9: that it come and read an old woman before and after her brew. Answered by its coming.
The Hearth-Holder, month 8, by runner through the Reckoner: that it sit at the seat's hearth on the night she named. Answered by its coming.
Two old women asked the Hearth-Holder, not the god, in month 7, that the seat be filled. Not asked of it.

## Names said aloud
None. The records the Keeper said aloud at the bank were by year, steading, number and row and not by name, and the god's name was not said aloud this year. The Reckoner told it that the woman and the child lived without naming either.

## What the people tell their god
The Reckoner, at the thaw, in her own words: "The cords are lower again. Where the wells are closed, fewer took ill twice. Where they were kept, as many as before. A third of those that kept have closed since the cords went round. The old elder keeps his and his people carry from the creek. You told me twelve days last summer, and I said so, and I will say it again if you ask. I told the Hearth-Holder what I count, in my words, and he did not ask for you. I hang what I count, and I do not say what it means."
The Warden of Waters: "The three on the rise carry thirty households and I have dug no fourth. I tried the water myself at the end of the wet and it gave as it gave last year. Salt does not come in the river. It comes in sacks. I stood at the landing for each boat and there was nothing for you to read, so I sent for no one."
The Keeper of the Mounds, if he speaks: "I said my rows aloud again, and by the moon where my cords hold it. I moved no body. The rest is in my cords."
Hedda, who asked for hard cases and kept its bed after each: "No child of the line fell ill. The star dimmed three times, two in the second month and one in the seventh. Your count and mine agree. Two fell in the moon you were sent for a man, before he came to me, and one did not, and none while you were gone. You were sent for, and you went. When you were away in the fifth month a runner came for a child and I gave him your words, and sent the apprentice with him. I told the old women only that you had given me the count."
The old sitter: "You said the same thing twice."
The sitters of the three nearest steadings: that the younger asked it to read an old woman before and after her brew and found it asked what she saw, and that one of the other two asked Hedda what she made of it.
The elder of the steading below the court: that a woman in labor never waited for a stranger, and that it was a decent thing for a guest to say.
The Hearth-Holder, through no one: that the seat is kept and not claimed, and that he asked again to see the cords.
The people at large: that the winter was ordinary, that no fever winter fell, that the barley stood and the eel ran, that salt came up the river about five times in sacks, that no stranger came, that the star stood three nights, and that you were gone five days into the trees and came back.
