# Year 5: the unnamed people of god-healing

Written by the civ agent. Month 1 is the first month of spring. What the god's power did is not settled here; where the year turns on it, the line says so, writes it both ways, briefly, and the report carries the question to the Keeper. Where the Keeper's line on Year 4 differs from my Year 4 file, the Keeper's line stood: the four mendings held, each with its days of shaking; the Tender's reading is closed on my people's sick; the bank ground is foul under the rows laid in the wasting winter and since, and in the wells below them, and sound under the older rows and on the rise, so the Keeper pegs a limit on rows laid in one place; the summer walk found the same foulness under the bank burials of the two emptied steadings and in their wells, the river clean, and no other steading or people below; the test wells read sound; and the dimmings of the star are not known to be anyone's doing. The numbers take the branch in which the mendings held.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The thaw was ordinary in height. The storehouses were thin at the end of the winter and held, and no steading was short of seed. The Warden of Waters had the wells cleaned as at every thaw. The shake was out of the stranger's hands from the thaw, as in the four springs before. It came out of the winter in undyed wool with the bone-handled knife at its belt, which Hedda saw it use on bread and cord only. No stranger came to the fen this year but the boatmen.

In the first days it went from Hedda's house to the small house of the Reckoner of Bodies by the court's yard, before any other door, and the yard saw it go. The Judge-Mother, told of it, said it was no longer a thing worth judging, and that a guest who keeps to one door has a custom, whatever else he has. The Reckoner received it at her door. It told her four things, in its own words. That her cords were lower again, and that it did not say what they meant, since she did not. That it had been far down the river last summer when a runner came for a woman in a hard labor, and no answer could be given. That it had not said how long it would be gone. That this was its to say and it did not say it. It asked her three things: what became of the woman and the child; that she carry certain words to the elder of that steading and to any steading that asked; and that she carry to the Warden of Waters a word about the salt. It asked her last to tell the Hearth-Holder, who had asked again to see the cords, what they say as she counts them, and told her that the cords were the court's and that it held no claim on them that the seat did not.

The Reckoner answered it as she answers. The woman and the child lived. The woman was weak until the reaping and was well by the thaw; the child, a girl, took the breast and thrived. Hedda's two sitters had sat the night as sitters sit. She said that it was wrong in one thing: it had told her, at her own door, about twelve days, and she had held her tongue as she said she would. It was Hedda to whom no days were given, and the runner went to Hedda. She said that she was saying what was, and not that it was wrong. On the cords she said she had never needed its leave to show them, that she had shown the Hearth-Holder at the last thaw, and that she would tell him what she counted, in her own words, as she always had. She did not take back her word of Year 1.

She carried the words that week. To the elder of the steading below the court where the woman lived she said, as she was asked: "When I am in the people's land and can be found, send to me through the Reckoner's door and I will come as I can. When I am gone, no one can answer, and a woman in labor must not wait for me. Send to your own sitter first." The elder heard them with his hands on his knees. He said that a woman in labor had never waited for any stranger and that he would not have his sitter told what she knew, but that it was a decent thing for a guest to say, and that he would have it said in his steading's hearing. Two other steadings sent to ask her for the words, and she gave them to the runners. Within some days they had gone down to the far steadings by runner and boat. The Reckoner said to all of them that it was a word and not an order, and that she carried it as she carried what she was given. In the yard some said the stranger had given its first order. The Judge-Mother was asked by the elders whether the words bound a steading, and said that they bound none, that a sitter sits first by the custom already, and that it was the stranger's to say where it would be found and no one's to be told what to do.

She carried the word on the salt to the Warden of Waters the same week. He heard it with his arms folded. He said that salt does not come up the river. It comes up in sacks in the boats, to the court steading's landing, and goes up from the landing on men's backs to the grain house and the beer cellars, and none is poured in the river or the creeks or any well, and none stands in any water but what a careless boatman spills on the landing stones, which the rain takes. He said he would not send a runner to a stranger's door for a sack. He said, as a man says of a guest who asks where the beer is kept, that the stranger was asking about a thing that was not there. If it wished to read the water at the landing by a boat, the landing was there, and he would not stop it. The Reckoner carried this back to the stranger in the same days, in his words.

In month 2 the star dimmed, the first of three nights this year, early in the month, before the mounds. The children were kept in. The stranger was at Hedda's hearth. The old women counted it as they count them. They said it was the first of the year and the year was young.

Also in month 2 the old women of the three nearest steadings went to the mounds at the first green, as they do each year, and took a pinch of the moss, dried and steeped it, and then a pinch of common moss from the marsh edge. They were the same, as in the two years before. The stranger went to the mounds in that month, 2.9 miles from the seat on foot, out and back in the one day, with Hedda and the sitters, and the old women set the pace. The Keeper of the Mounds came out and walked beside it, saying little. At the moss the stranger spoke first to Hedda, plainly, with the others near enough to hear it. It said that it counts the same star that she does, that its count is three, five, six and now seven, and that she should hear how it counted. It said the two pairs of the fifth and seventh months of Year 3 had fallen while it was far off and mending no one, and that her count of Year 4 and its own pointed different ways. It said it did not know what the dimming is, that it did not light it, and that it did not know the star is going out. It asked her to keep counting by the month and to set beside each night what it did in that moon, and to tell the old women what it had said and not what she thought it would like said. Hedda said that it was not so that it was far off. Of the pair in the fifth month of Year 3, the first fell some days after the mounds while it was at the court steading, and only the second fell while it was away on its walk. Of the pair in the seventh month, it was at her own hearth, and went to the mounds at the end of that month after the frost. She said she had told her count to the Judge-Mother, when asked, and that she would say it to it as well, as it asked. She said what it had said to the old women then and there, standing among the moss, in its words. They took it in differently. The old sitter said that it said the same thing twice, once last year to her face and once to Hedda, and that this was either honest or a habit. Some of the weir steading's women, who keep from the mounds, heard of it afterward and said a lamp that does not know whether it is going out is a lamp that is going out. The old women of the three nearest steadings said they were not answered, and that they had not been told anything new, only that it did not know.

To the sitters of the three nearest steadings, through Hedda, the stranger said that it knew no brewing and could not say what to give. It said it would read a sick one before and after a brew when it was sent for, and say what changed in the body, and that they might judge the brew by that. It said that it knew its reading was closed on their own people, and so it would ask them what ailed the sick one, and they must say it plainly. The younger sitter, who had thanked it before, said she would send as she had. One of the other two said that was a thing she had not been told before, that a reader of the dead and the well could not read her own people, and asked Hedda why it should be so. Hedda said she did not know. The other said nothing. The old women said afterward that its sight was closed on the fen's sick because the seat stands empty, and that filled, it would see.

In the last days of month 2 the star dimmed again, the second of the year. The old women said two in a month had not fallen since Year 3, and talked little. Also in the last days of month 2 a man of a low steading, who had cut his shin with an adze in the winter timber, came to Hedda's door on the ninth day with the wound gone red and hot to the knee, after the sitters had sat two nights. Hedda sent to the stranger, and it sat the night with its hand on him. This is the first mending of the year, and only the stranger's power can say how it came out (see the report). The people saw the man come through, and the stranger kept to the corner of Hedda's house some days after, with the shake. Hedda set the two dim nights beside it and wrote that both fell before the man was brought to her.

In month 3, after the sowing, the Keeper of the Mounds came to the bank ground. The Reckoner had told him at the thaw, as a thing she did on her own and not as the stranger's asking, that the rows laid since his peg had never been read and that the stranger would stand with him again if he wished it. He said at the bank that he had thought on it and that he, too, would sooner know how the ground ran with the years. He said his rows aloud as he had in Year 4: by year, steading, number and row, with the dead's steading marked and not their names. The stranger asked him, as it had said it would, to say which were laid in which month, and he said that his cords carry a moon-knot only on the rows of the wasting winter, which the Keeper before him knotted by the moon because they were so many, and on the rows laid since the rise began in Year 2, and that the rest are by year alone. He said them so. Hedda and the Reckoner's runner, the girl of the court steading whom the Reckoner is teaching the cords, set his words in order beside the pegs. He pointed to graves, oldest and newest and some between, and to the rows laid since his peg, and the stranger went to each, knelt, and laid a bare hand on the soil. The kin of the line's plot stood by. The old woman among them who had spat in Year 4 did not spit, and said nothing. The stranger told them again that it laid no hand on the dead and moved nothing. The Warden stood at the wells below each row, and the stranger stood at each wellhead after the grave. At the end the Keeper asked it to read the ground on the rise where he lays now, and it did. What it read at every grave, well and row is the Keeper's. Both ways: if the foulness fades or holds under the older rows and the rows laid since the peg read sound, the stranger said so, the Keeper wrote it in his cords, the peg's limit stood and he went on laying the court steading's dead on the rise for those whose kin would have it; if the foulness has spread to rows laid since the peg or toward the rise, the stranger said so, the Keeper pegged the rise back again and laid no more on its near end, and the Warden had the nearest rota well tried twice. In either branch no grave was moved and none was rotted.

Also in month 3 the sowing went in. The storehouses had held. The Reckoner's winter cords, hung in the hall again at the elders' meeting in month 2, had been counted by the elders as they were the year before: a third of the steadings that still kept their wells closed or drew them off before the sowing. The old elder kept his. The rota on the rise stayed at about thirty households. The households that had closed their wells since the thaw carried creek water, as many had always done, and asked the Warden for a fourth well. He said the three carried what they carried and that he would not dig a fourth before he had seen the three through a wet season again.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The summer was ordinary. The barley stood, the beans did well, the eel traps were full in the low creeks. Salt boats came up to the court steading's landing about five times and flax, linen and smoked eel went down. The Warden stood at the landing for each boat, as he does, and sent no runner to anyone's door. The boatmen went on as they do.

In month 5, on the first day of the month, the stranger came to the Reckoner's door and told her, as it had said it would, the days beforehand: that it would walk west to the treeline and go into the forest no further than a day and a half, reading the water at each spring and any burial ground or midden it found, and any creature that looked sick; that it would come out of the trees by the fifth day at the latest; and that while it was gone no one acted for it and a question put to it would wait. She asked what it looked for. It said the forest's edge, any other people, and any sickness the steadings do not have. She said it should be back before the reaping, that she would tell the steadings the days, and that she would hold her tongue about its errand until it had told her what it found. She sent runners to the steadings that week to tell them what it had said and that its words about the sitters stood. On the ninth day of month 5 it set out at dawn, on foot, alone, with a pack, out of the court steading by the western side, across the fields and the marsh edge toward the trees on the west. No one went with it and no one followed. My people did not see it for five days by their own count, as far as my year goes. It came in at dusk of the fifth day by the western track and went to the Reckoner's door before any other. If the Keeper prices the way longer or shorter, the end of the walk moves with it and the reaping runs as written. If its walk met ground or people not in my record, that is not mine to write. The court said it had gone to the trees to speak with the walkers who sing the names at the mounds, or with the quiet one who walks the marsh edge, or to find out where the forest keeps its own Tender. Hedda kept its bed ready. What it told the Reckoner, how far it went and what ground it saw are the Keeper's. Both ways: if it found only the forest's edge, clean springs and empty ground, the Reckoner told the Warden, the Judge-Mother and the Keeper that, which changed nothing in the cords, and the court said it had walked for nothing; if it found any other people, a burial ground or midden with sickness in it, or a creature sick with a thing the steadings do not have, she told the same three what it said, and the talk was of what lay in the trees. If it met any people in my record's silence or beyond it, that is not mine to write. The star dimmed none of the nights it was gone.

While it was away, on the third day, a runner came to Hedda's door from a low steading: a child with the early rattle, and the sitter of the steading had sat one night and the fever stood. He asked for the stranger. Hedda said it was away and would be back by the fifth day, that she had no answer to give, and that he had the stranger's words, which the Reckoner had carried to his steading: send to your own sitter first. He said his elder had heard them. He said he had come to ask all the same. Hedda sent him home, and sent with him the apprentice she was teaching, who is no sitter, to carry broth and sit up with the household. The sitter of that steading sat a second night. The child's fever broke on the fourth day. It was not the stranger's act and it was not its mending. The runner came back to Hedda's door after the stranger had come in to say that the child lived, and Hedda told it at her hearth, and it heard her.

The reaping took every hand in month 6, the stranger's among them for some days.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

In month 7, after the first frost, the old women of the three nearest steadings went to the mounds and found the moss as before. The star dimmed once, the third night of the year. It was the only night of the autumn and the winter. The stranger was at the court steading, at Hedda's hearth. Two old women of the nearest steadings went to the seat's hall at the end of the month and said the seat should be filled before the lamp went. The Hearth-Holder heard them and sent them home with beer. They said fewer came to ask this year than last, and the old women of the weir steading, who had come in Year 4, did not come.

The wet season began in month 8: the chest rattle in the small and the old, fewer in the low steadings that had closed their wells. The stranger went among the steadings one at a time and tended with broth and its hands the children and the old that the sitters could not reach. In that month a small child of a steading by the river took the rattle and the sitters failed, and Hedda sent to the stranger, and it sat the night. This is the second mending of the year. At the end of the month the Hearth-Holder sent a runner to the Reckoner, as in the three years before, to say that the stranger might sit at the seat's hearth again on the night she named. She named it, and went with the stranger at dusk to the seat's hall. The household sat along the walls, with the girl of eight beside her grandfather. The Judge-Mother sat across the fire. The Spearward sat by the door. No one asked it anything. The Hearth-Holder said it was welcome as long as it would sit there and that the seat was kept and not claimed. He did not take its word that it was the one the seat is kept for, because it did not give it to him and he did not ask. The Judge-Mother said afterward that it had said nothing again and that she had come to like that better than a great deal. The Spearward said nothing in the hall. His two men stood at the court steading's grain house that night as on every night since Year 3.

In month 9 the younger sitter of the three nearest steadings brewed for an old woman with the wet chest and sent for the stranger as it had said. It came to the old woman before the brew was given, put its hand to her chest and was still, and then asked the sitter in plain words what ailed her. The sitter said it plainly. It asked her again after the cup had been taken, and again said what it could. The sitter said afterward to Hedda that it asked as one asks who cannot see, and that it was the only one who had asked her what she saw. What it read in the old woman's body, if it read anything, and what it said, are the Keeper's. Both ways: if the Reading closed to it as it did last year, it asked the sitter what ailed the old woman and said what it could from her words, and she judged her brew by her own eyes and what it said; if the Reading held for the sick of the sitter's own steading, it told her in plain words what changed in the body, and she judged by that. In either branch the sitter told the other two, and one of them asked Hedda, in the winter, what the younger made of it. She did not herself send.

At the end of the wet season, in month 9, the stranger went on its own to each of the three wells on the rise, before any elder asked, and read each and told the Reckoner and the Warden what it read. The Warden tried the water by his own ways beside it, as he does, and found the wells as he had found them the year before, and the thirty households of the rota drew on without a sickness that the Reckoner could trace to the water. He said to the stranger, drily, at the third well, that no salt had come, and that it never would, and that he had thought it a pity for a reader to stand ready all year for a thing that was not there. What the stranger read is the Keeper's; the water gave as last year. Both ways: if it read sound, the Warden let the rota stand and said again that he would dig no fourth well before the thaw; if it read foul, which the water did not suggest, he covered that well and the rota drew from two. In month 9 the Reckoner took her autumn count with the Keeper of the Mounds. She said nothing to the stranger of what it showed, only that the wet season's rattle had been fewer again in the low steadings that had closed their wells, and as many as before in those that kept them.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

An ordinary sickness winter: coughs, agues, the old and small taken ill. No fever winter fell. The stranger stayed among the steadings through the long sickness, going where the need was greatest, mending only what the sitters had failed at, one at a time with long rests, and no child of the line fell ill. The Hearth-Holder's household kept well. In month 11 an old man of a steading near the weir, with the wet chest, was brought to Hedda's door after the sitters had failed; Hedda sent, and the stranger sat the night. This is the third mending of the year, three against four last year and seven the year before. The sitters of the three nearest steadings, and Hedda for the hard cases, sent for it four times in all, against six the year before, which is the count it said it wished. No star dimmed in the winter, and the old women, who had talked of little else the winter before, said that it was the first winter since Year 2 in which the star stood, and that the lamp had steadied. Some said it was because the seat had been spoken for, in the old women's asking, though the Hearth-Holder had said nothing. Others said it was because the stranger had gone into the trees and not downstream. Hedda told no one what she thought.

At the thaw, at the year's end, the stranger gave Hedda its count of the nights, three, with where its body was and whom it had mended within each moon. Hedda gave it hers, which agreed. She told it in turn that two of the three had fallen in the moon in which it had mended a man, both before he was brought to her, and the third in a moon in which it had mended no one, and none while it was away. She said she had told the old women only that it had given her the count. The Reckoner took her winter count. In the low steadings that had closed or drawn off their wells, fewer sickened again than the winter before. In the low steadings that kept their wells, as many sickened as before. The total was lower. She showed the cords to the Warden and to the Judge-Mother, and to the Hearth-Holder, who asked again. She told him in her own words what she counted, as the stranger had asked and as she would have done in any case, and said that the cords were the court's. He looked a long while and said nothing. He did not ask for the stranger. A third of the steadings that still kept wells closed or drew them off before the sowing, because the cords went round with the runner and the elders counted. The old elder kept his, and a number of his households carried creek water, as they had. Talk of going back to the two emptied steadings, which had risen each of the four winters before, did not rise this year. The Reckoner had told the Warden, the Judge-Mother and the Keeper what the stranger found below, that the same foulness lay in the bank graves and the wells of the two emptied steadings, and the families that had lost kin there said the Warden would first have to dig on the rise, and did not ask.

The year closed on the thaw, the flood not yet up.

## What the year cost and bought

The people followed no order of the god this year, for it gave none. It gave words, which the Reckoner carried as her own errand, and which the steadings heard as a guest's saying where it would be found. The Warden's answer on the salt, the Keeper's coming to the bank, the Judge-Mother's saying that the words bound no one, the Spearward's men at the grain house and the Hearth-Holder's asking again to see the cords are theirs, in their own offices. The stranger was wrong about the salt, and the Warden said so, and it was taken as a guest's error and not a stranger's falseness. The sitters of the three nearest steadings sent for it fewer times than any year the files hold, and the cords are lower for a fourth year. The star stood three nights, as few as the first year. The court is as near the stranger as it was, and no nearer to calling it what the line keeps the seat for.
