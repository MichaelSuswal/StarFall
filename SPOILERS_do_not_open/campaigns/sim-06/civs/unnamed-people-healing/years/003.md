# Year 3: the unnamed people of god-healing

Written by the civ agent. Month 1 is the first month of spring. What the god's power did is not settled here; where the year turns on it, the line says so, writes it both ways, and the report carries the question to the Keeper. The numbers take the branch in which the mendings held.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The thaw was ordinary in height, and the silt lay as it does. The storehouses were thin at the end of the winter and held. The Warden of Waters had the wells cleaned as at every thaw. The shake went out of the stranger's hands at the thaw, as Hedda had seen it go the year before. It came out of the winter in undyed wool as ever, and it wore at its belt a knife with a bone handle that it had not worn before. Hedda watched it cut bread and cord with the knife and nothing else. In the yard the Spearward's men looked at the knife and said nothing.

In the first days the stranger went from Hedda's house to the small house of the Reckoner of Bodies by the court's yard, before any other door, as it had the spring before, and the yard saw it go. It told her what it had read on its walk below the court the summer before: that the wells still open below the bank burials were foul, that the wells drawn off or closed were fainter and foul at the bottom, that the creek water was clean and the rise above the bank sound. It told her that it thought her winter count, which had fallen fastest where the wells were closed, meant what it looked like. The Reckoner said, dry as ever, that she had hung the cords herself and had shown them to the Warden and the Judge-Mother at the thaw, and did not need to be told what they meant, and that she did not say it was wrong. It asked her to carry its asking to the Warden of Waters: that he dig two or three test wells on the rise, with the elders' leave, and that it be let to stand at each and read the water before any drank of it, and again at the end of a season. She said she would carry it and bring back his answer. The Judge-Mother, hearing the stranger had gone to the count again before the throne, judged it manners a second time. Two or three in the yard said again that the Reckoner had her office in the stranger's pocket. She did not answer them.

She sent for the Warden to her house that week. He heard her with his arms folded. He asked what the stranger wanted of the wells, was told, and said that he dug wells where he judged and not where a stranger pointed, and that the rise was the court steading's ground and the elders' to give. He said he had meant to send to the steadings that kept their wells with the Reckoner's cords, as he had said at the thaw, and he did, by runner, that same week. About half of those that still kept their wells closed or drew them off before the sowing, because the cords went with the runner and the elders could count. The rest did not.

In month 1 the star dimmed, the first of six nights this year. The old women counted it as they count them, and the children were kept in.

In month 2 the elders of the court steading met on the Warden's asking for leave to dig on the rise. One old elder said no well should be dug on ground where the dead lie. The Keeper of the Mounds said that there was room at the far end of the rise from the laid rows, and that he would peg the rows and the Warden might peg the wells, and that no well would stand within the pegged ground of the dead. Leave was given with that one against. The kin who keep the line's plot at the bank were sore: the Warden would dig wells for the court's households on the rise while the wells below the bank were called foul, and they said so at the Reckoner's door. She said the count was not hers to bend. A woman of a low steading, a fever past the ninth day that the sitters could not break, was brought to Hedda's door in month 2. Hedda sent to the stranger, and it sat the night with its hand on her. This is the first mending of the year, and only the stranger's power can say how it came out (see the report). The people saw the woman come through, and the stranger kept to the corner of Hedda's house some days after, with the shake.

Also in month 2 the old women of the three nearest steadings went out to the mounds at the first green, as they do each year, and took a pinch of the moss, dried and steeped it. It did not bite the tongue or numb the lip. They took a pinch of common moss from the marsh edge and steeped that. It was the same. They came away saying little, and said nothing outside their own number for some weeks. They went again at the end of the month to be sure.

In month 3, after the sowing, the old sitter who had asked the stranger the year before whether the moss was dying or only tired came to Hedda's door and told her. Hedda told the stranger at her hearth that evening. It heard her and said that it would go to the mounds at midsummer with her and the sitters, as it had said it would. The Keeper of the Mounds, who sits with the old women's number at the autumn count, heard of it from Hedda that week, and said nothing.

Also in month 3, after the sowing, the Warden had three wells dug on the rise at the far end from the laid rows, by men of the court steading, and water was struck in each at an ordinary depth. The stranger stood at each wellhead before anyone drank, with the Warden, the Keeper of the Mounds and the elder who gave leave, and said in plain words what it read. What it read is the Keeper's. Both ways: if all three read sound, the Warden let the households nearest the rise draw from them from the month after, kept his own account of each by his own ways, and the court steading talked of the rise as sound ground for the living as well as the dead; if any read foul, the Warden covered that well and said only that he had never said the rise was better than the rest, and the talk of the rise as dry ground went quiet.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The summer was ordinary. The barley stood, the beans did well, the eel traps were full in the low creeks. Salt boats came up to the court steading's landing, and flax, linen and smoked eel went down. The boatmen went on as they do. In month 4 a man of a steading below the court, with a chest that would not clear, was brought to Hedda's door after the sitters failed. The stranger sat one night with him and rested. This is the second mending of the year.

In month 4 the stranger went to the old elder's steading, the one whose people had been asked to carry creek water and whose elder had kept his well. It read his well in front of his people and told him plainly what it read now. It asked nothing of him but his own judgment. He stood with his arms folded and said before his steading that his father Tolm had dug that well with his own hands and that his people had drunk of it since. What it read is the Keeper's. Both ways: if it read foul, the elder did not close the well and did not forbid anyone to carry creek water, and a number of his households, with the steading's sitter, began quietly to carry it; if it read sound, he said so aloud to the Warden, whom he sent for, and the Warden came and tried the water by his own ways and said the well might stand and that it was for the elder and himself to see it stood. In either branch his steading's winter was as the Reckoner's cords for the kept wells, and the cords were not his to dispute.

In month 5, at midsummer, the stranger went to the mounds, 2.9 miles from the seat on foot, out and back in the one day, with Hedda and the same three sitters as before. The old women set the pace. The Keeper of the Mounds came out to meet them and walked beside it, saying little. At the moss the stranger stood close by as it had before, a long while, as it looks at a body. It asked the old women whether the moss bit the tongue any more or less than at the thaw. They told it that it had not bitten at all since the second month, and that brewed it did nothing for a fever that common moss would not do, and that it looked as it always looked. The old sitter asked it straight, in front of the others, whether it was it that had done this, or whether the moss was going already. The stranger was quiet a long while and no answer was given that I can write. It mended no one on that walk. What it read in the moss is the Keeper's. Both ways: if its Reading found something the old women could not, it said so in plain words and they carried it home and weighed it against their saying, and did not drop the saying; if it found only common moss, it told them that, which they knew, and they were no better and no worse for it. The sitters of the three nearest steadings said they had been answered, though not what they had asked.

In month 5 the star dimmed twice, the second and third nights of the year. The first fell some days after the mounds, while the stranger was at the court steading. The second fell while it was away on its walk, and was seen by those awake and not by it in their sight.

In the middle of month 5 the stranger came to the Reckoner's door and asked her leave to walk the ground above the low steadings alone. She asked what it looked for. It said it was looking for where the foulness begins and where it stops, whether it lies under the bank graves, under the whole low ground, or comes down from upstream. She gave leave and said it should be back before the reaping. It set out at dawn from the court steading, on foot, alone, with a pack, upstream by the river bank, and was to go by the creeks that feed the river and then toward the trees on the west. It went from our sight for about fourteen days by the people's count, as far as my year goes. No one went with it and no one followed. The court said it had gone to ground, or to the head of the river to speak with the quiet one, or to find out who fouled the wells. The Reckoner alone knew what it looked for and told no one. Hedda kept its bed ready. It came in at dusk by the river path, early in month 6, before the reaping, and went to the Reckoner's door before any other. What it read on the walk, how far it went and what ground it saw are the Keeper's. Both ways: if the foulness lies under the bank graves and the low ground alone, the Reckoner told the Warden and the Keeper what it said, the Warden's rule on wells stood, and the Keeper began to talk of where else the dead might be laid; if the foulness comes down from upstream or from ground beyond my people's, the Reckoner told the Warden and the Judge-Mother only, the people's closing of wells cannot by itself clear it, and talk rose of sending upstream, and no one was sent in my year. If its walk met any ground or people not in my record, that is not mine to write.

The reaping took every hand in month 6, the stranger's among them for some days. At the end of the month the stranger stood again at each of the three wells on the rise and read each, at the end of the season as it had said. What it read is the Keeper's. Both ways: if the three still read sound, the Warden put his count of the court's households on them before the wet season and asked the elders whether more should be dug; if any now read foul, he covered it and the rise was no more than ordinary ground in the people's mouths.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

In month 7 the star dimmed twice more, the fourth and fifth nights. The old women, who say the nights come oftener, said they came oftener this year than last, and that the seat is half full. Hedda counted them against the sendings and found no tie, and said nothing. On the last days of the month, after the first frost, the stranger went again to the mounds with Hedda and the sitters, as it had said. The old women told it the moss was as it had been since the second month: not at all biting, nothing in the brew that common moss lacked. It stood by the moss a long while again and mended no one.

In that month the old women of the steading of the weir, the one that had seen the ox go to slurry, came to the Judge-Mother and asked that the stranger be kept from the mounds, since the moss had failed the year it was twice taken there to look at it. The Judge-Mother ruled that no one is barred from the ground of the dead for looking, which has never been a harm in the fen; that the stranger had not touched the moss on either walk; that the moss was weak before it came; and that the old women might hold their saying, and the court would not hold it for them. The Keeper of the Mounds wrote in his records the month the moss was found common, and the ruling. Some of the weir steading's women did not go to the mounds again that year.

At the end of month 7 three old women of the nearest steadings went to the seat's hall and asked the Hearth-Holder that the seat be filled, since the moss had failed with it empty. He heard them, sent them home with beer, and gave no answer.

The wet-season illnesses began in month 8, the chest rattle in the small and the old. The stranger went among the steadings one at a time, tended with broth and its hands the children and the old whom the sitters could not, and sat with two of them in the manner of the mendings after the sitters had failed: a small child with the chest rattle, and an old woman with a fever that would not break. These are the third and fourth mendings of the year. No carcass or spoiled store lay by any wellhead this year, and it called the rot out of nothing; the steading of the heap and the steading of the weir were not tried again. The star dimmed once in month 8, the sixth night of the year.

The Hearth-Holder, who is not a man who asks, sent a runner to the Reckoner in month 8 to say that the stranger might sit at the seat's hearth again, on the night she named. She named it, at the end of the month, and went with the stranger at dusk to the seat's hall. The household sat along the walls and the Judge-Mother sat across the fire. The Spearward sat by the door. The Hearth-Holder asked nothing of it this time, and the Judge-Mother asked nothing. The Spearward asked, from his place by the door, whether it would rot a granary, ours or another's, if the seat bade it, in war. The stranger answered in front of the hearth that it would not turn it on any store of the people, in war or out of it. Nobody spoke for a time. The Hearth-Holder said it was welcome at the hearth as long as it would sit there, that the seat was kept and not claimed, and that the seat was not kept for a moss. He did not take its word that it was the one the seat is kept for, because it did not give it to him and he did not ask. The Judge-Mother said to the Reckoner afterward that the stranger had said less than last year and she liked that no better. The Spearward said nothing in the hall. In the yard he put two men on the court steading's grain house by night, on his own word and no one's leave, and said to them that a promise is not a lock.

In month 9 the stranger sat with one more, an old man whom the sitters had failed. This is the fifth mending. The Reckoner took her autumn count at the end of the month with the Keeper of the Mounds. She said nothing to the stranger of what it showed, only that the wet season's chest rattle had been fewer in the low steadings that had closed their wells.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

An ordinary sickness winter: coughs, agues, the old and small taken ill. No fever winter fell. The stranger stayed among the steadings through the long sickness, going where the need was greatest, mending only what the sitters had failed at, one at a time with long rests. In month 11 it sat with one, a child of a low steading with the wet chest. In month 12 it sat with one, an old woman. These are the sixth and seventh mendings of the year, seven against nine last year and about thirty the year before. No child of the line fell ill. The sitters, who have no moss, brewed common moss and marsh herbs and sat as before, and sent for the stranger fewer times than the winter before. The star did not dim in the winter.

At the thaw, at the year's end, the Reckoner took her winter count. In the low steadings that had closed or drawn off their wells, fewer sickened again than the winter before. In the low steadings that kept their wells, as many sickened as before. The winter's total was lower than the winter before. She showed the cords to the Warden and to the Judge-Mother, and this year the Hearth-Holder asked to see them, which is the first thing the people recall his asking for, and looked a long while and said nothing. The Reckoner kept her office to the people.

At the thaw meal the Hearth-Holder said in the open that the seat is kept and not claimed, that no plant had ever been what it was kept for, and that the moss would come back or not by no one's word. The old women whispered afterward that he had taken the stranger for the Tender and was ashamed of it. He had not.

The year closed on the thaw, the flood not yet up.

## What the year cost and bought

The people did not follow any order of the god this year, for it gave none. The Reckoner carried its asking to the Warden in month 1 and gave it leave to walk in month 5; both were hers. The Warden's three test wells, the Keeper's pegs, the Judge-Mother's ruling on the moss and the Spearward's two men at the grain house are theirs, set in their own offices. The old women's moss is gone and the sitters are poorer for it. The low steadings are sicker less often than any winter the cords hold. The court is as near the stranger as it was, and no nearer to calling it what the line keeps the seat for.
