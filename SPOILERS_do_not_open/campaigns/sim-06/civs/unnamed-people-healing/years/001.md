# Year 1: the unnamed people of god-healing

Written by the civ agent. Month 1 is the first month of spring. What the god's power did is not settled here; where the year turns on it, the line says so and the report carries the question to the Keeper.

## Spring (months 1 to 3)

The thaw was ordinary and lower than the last three, so the fields took less silt and the flood did not climb the platforms. The storehouses were thin after the winter. The Warden of Waters had the wells cleaned as every thaw, and the court steading's barley seed was short by a few measures, made up from two neighbors.

In the first days a Keeper of the Mounds, walking out at first light to see the grave-ground after the frost, saw a stranger in undyed wool on the low mounds to the north west. He kept to the field edge and watched. The stranger went slowly over the mounds, knelt at some, laid a bare hand on the soil, stayed long at one of the oldest, and then went down to the river bank and stood with a hand in the water where it runs past the village. It took no grave goods, dug nothing and left the grass as it found it. The Keeper of the Mounds said nothing to anyone that week, which is his way, and marked which mound it had stayed longest at.

Three nights of the year the star stood dim. Two fell in month 1, some days apart. The fen has seen such nights before and counted them as it always has, with the old women muttering, the children kept in, and the beer jars watched.

The stranger came into the court steading at the edge, near midday, on foot, and asked at the first house for the midwife, the laying-out woman or whoever sits with the sick. The house was a reed cutter's. The woman of it sent a child running, and Hedda, the steading's midwife, came, a broad grey woman who gave her name to the stranger at the door because the stranger gave its need first and she would not be rude to a person who asked for work. The stranger offered its hands for food and a place by a hearth, nothing more. Hedda, who had no sitter's gift herself and was short of hands in a bad year, took it in. The stranger gave no name. Nobody asked it straight that season.

At Hedda's hearth the stranger asked to see the well and the grain store. The stranger knelt at the wellhead, laid a hand on the stones, was still a while, and then told Hedda and the Warden of Waters, when he was fetched, plainly and in the terms of the custom of boiling for the sick, what it said the well and the store held and what to do with it. The Warden, a dry man who dislikes advice, listened with his arms folded, had a part of the store set apart and the well drained and cleaned again, and said afterward that a stranger who knew boiling and wells was no worse for it. If the Reading showed nothing foul, strike the setting apart and the second cleaning: the Warden then only noted that the stranger knew the custom and kept hands out of the water, and the rest of the spring runs the same.

In late spring there were four cases the sitters and the bonesetter could not manage. A labor gone wrong in a lower steading, brought to Hedda's door. A man with a fever that would not break on the ninth day. A girl with a wound gone to red and hot. A child with the early rattle in the chest. The stranger was asked for by Hedda in each case, quietly, in the sitters' way. It sat through the night with its hand on each, one at a time, with days between. The people saw the sufferers come through. They also saw the stranger take a chill and a shake of the hands for some days after each, and keep to the corner of Hedda's house, taking broth. Hedda said it was the sitter's price, and that the stranger paid it harder than any sitter she had known. In the spring the court steading began to say that a very great sitter had come, and most of them were glad, and fewer were sure.

Those mended said later that with their eyes shut they knew when the stranger was in the room. They did not like it and did not say so loudly.

## Summer (months 4 to 6)

The summer was ordinary. The barley stood, the bean rows did well, the eel traps were full in the low creeks. A salt boat, then another, came up the river to the court steading's landing, and flax and linen and smoked eel went down. The boatmen saw the stranger at Hedda's door tending a child, and went on. No stranger had come before the stranger in wool whom the steadings did not already know.

In early summer the stranger mended four more, at long intervals, again at Hedda's asking. The two sitters of the nearer steadings began to come to Hedda's house in the evenings, because the stranger asked them what they knew, and listened, which sitters are not used to. It asked about the herbs, the mound moss, how they sat and for how long, what they did when the shake came. They told it more than they had told the Reckoner in years. The stranger gave them nothing back that they could name, and asked them to teach Hedda's apprentice.

In month 5 the star dimmed the third time. The old women took it as a sign. Someone in Hedda's household noted that the stranger had gone out under the sky that night and stood a long while looking up. Hedda put the two things in her head and said nothing.

In month 5 or 6 the stranger asked Hedda how it might see the Reckoner of Bodies. Hedda sent word. The Reckoner is a woman of fifty five, thin, bad hipped, who sits in a small house by the court's yard with her cords, and does not like to be asked for. She received the stranger one evening, sat it down, and asked what it wanted. The stranger said it wished to hear what the Reckoner counted each spring and autumn, in the words the sitters used, because it could not read the cords. The Reckoner took her time and then told it: births, deaths, by age and by what the sitters called the cause (fever, wet chest, flux, childbed, drowned, old), steading by steading, with the worst year's cords hung apart. She told it last winter's count, which was the worst she had hung. She did this partly because it was her office to answer a stranger who asked the right thing and partly because she wished to see what it did with the numbers.

At the second sitting the Reckoner asked it straight whether it was the one the seat is kept for. The stranger answered her truly. She sat a long while. She asked what it wanted of the court. The stranger said it asked nothing of the court that it had not first earned by work, and asked what the court would have it do. She did not answer that night. She said she would watch it through the wet season and count what she saw in her cords, and that she would take no one's word for what she had not counted. She asked the stranger its name, and it told her.

She told no one that week, not the Hearth-Holder, not the Judge-Mother. She did tell the Keeper of the Mounds, with whom she has sat at the autumn count for twenty years.

In late summer the stranger left the court steading for some days. It walked the river bank upstream and downstream and toward the western treeline, said it would be back, and was. The steading did not know what it did out there. A few thought it had gone to ground. Hedda kept its bed.

## Autumn (months 7 to 9)

The autumn was wet, as it is. The first illnesses of the wet season, the chest rattle in the small and the old, began in month 8. The stranger went from the court steading to the neighboring steadings, one at a time, and came back. It went to wells and grain stores in each, knelt and put a hand to them, told the elder and the sitter what it said, and helped with the cleaning. It tended the sick the sitters could not, one at a time, resting between. By the end of the season the sitters, who had at first looked at it sidelong, were sending runners for it only when they had done what they could. The stranger asked each of them to show it what they did, and mostly sat and listened.

In month 8 a spoiled heap of last year's barley and fish offal behind a grain house near the wellhead of one of the larger steadings, forgotten in the wet and running down toward the well, was put to rot by the stranger in an afternoon. The people saw it go down into black slurry and the smell rise and sink, at once, quicker than any heap should go. The elder of that steading was frightened and then angry, and the sitter of the steading was only frightened. The stranger mended no one that day and the next night, and sat with its back to the wall of the grain house. The steading said afterward that the stranger had called the rot out of the heap. This is the first the court heard, from the Warden of Waters, of anything the stranger did that no sitter could do. Whether the heap went down as the people saw it is a matter of the stranger's power and is carried to the Keeper.

The Reckoner took her autumn count at the end of month 9. It was lower than last year's by a margin she could not call chance. Her count of the deaths among those the sitters had given up and the stranger had sat with she kept apart on a cord of her own. She sent for the stranger and told it that she took its word. She said so in her own words, as the one who counts: that she would answer to it for the count, and that she would keep her office to the people and not to it, and that if it wanted anything of the court, it should come to her first. She told it she had not told the Hearth-Holder and that it was not hers to say. The stranger asked her to say nothing yet. She agreed and did.

## Winter (months 10 to 12)

An ordinary sickness winter: coughs, agues, old and small ones taken ill as in most winters. The wasting fever of last winter did not return. The stranger stayed among the people through the whole season, going where the need was greatest. It kept to the court steading and to the three steadings nearest, and then to two more by the river, a day's walk apart by the people's reckoning, and came back. The stranger mended a few more, resting between, and was sat up through the shakes by Hedda. A count was kept in the stranger's head, as it said, of whom and when. The Reckoner kept her own.

In month 11 the youngest grandchild of the Hearth-Holder, a girl of four of the line, took the wet chest. The court's sitters sat with her three nights and the shake came on them and grew worse. The Hearth-Holder is not a man who asks, but he sent to Hedda. Hedda sent to the stranger and said what the child was. The stranger came, sat with the child through a night with the court's women outside the door, and in the morning the girl's fever had broken. The stranger took the chill and the shake, and was carried to Hedda's house and kept to bed for some days. The people of the court say the child was dead by the third night and the stranger called her back. That is the people's saying and may be exaggeration. Whether the mending took as the people saw is the stranger's power and is carried to the Keeper. If the mending did not take, the child died in the morning, the Hearth-Holder's court went cold toward the stranger, the Judge-Mother asked that it be asked to leave, and the Reckoner stood alone for it. The rest of the year then runs on in smaller ways.

In the last month the Hearth-Holder sent a runner to Hedda's house with the words that the Hearth-Holder would be glad to have the stranger sit at the seat's hearth when it was well. The stranger was then at a neighboring steading and the invitation was carried on to it by a boy, and the year closed with it unanswered, as far as the court steading knew. The Judge-Mother, who is old, said the court should wait and see which way the stranger walked.

The year closed on the thaw, the flood not yet up. The people have come through a fever winter, then one ordinary winter, with fewer buried than they feared.

## What the year cost and bought

The people did not follow any order of a god this year, since the god made no demand of them. A helper's word was not taken by the court, only by the Reckoner, in private. Nothing was done in the god's name by anyone.
