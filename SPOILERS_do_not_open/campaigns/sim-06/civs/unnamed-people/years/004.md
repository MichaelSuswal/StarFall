# Sealed: the unnamed people, the stretch from the fifth day of the first month to the fourteenth day of the seventh month (Year 3 told again)

Written by the civ agent for the Keeper. This is what the people did, said and knew in the stretch out of the Weightward's sight and hearing, including what the eleven said among themselves and what is drawn from the sealed side release. The public file is `campaigns/sim-06/civs/unnamed-people/years/004.md`. Names are given here by the Keeper's list; none of them goes in a public file until a mortal says it in the Weightward's hearing.

## Who held what at the start of the stretch, and what moved it

From the sealed side release and the two earlier notes it names. The clerk from the kingdom upriver called on the old Weightward in the summer before his last winter, asked to see the old weights, was shown six and told the seventh was out for cleaning. The Tally-keeper (Annis Furrow) stood witness and holds the kept half, in the reed-lined chest under the hall. The boatman lent the old man a punt on a night of the spring flood in his last spring and had it back before light with silt in the bottom and a wet rope; the Stallreeve (Tam Wherry) has it. The coat and boots in the loft, stiff with silt above the knee: the Assay-master (Osric Marl), the Stallreeve and the clay head. The eleven have had all of it since the winter sitting of Year 2 behind the shut door. They had the hall, the old man's house, the landings and the channel looked in and gave the looking up. Where the weight is, nobody in the town knows. The ship owner of the open water (Davin Sculler) knows the house of the southern coaster's master by its name and has not said so to the Weightward, who did not make him answer.

None of it moved in the stretch. Nobody asked the Tally-keeper plainly, the Stallreeve was not asked of the boatman away from the yard, and nobody went looking again. The eleven's giving the looking up stood. I sent nobody anywhere because the sealed note mentions looking.

## Day 5, the gatepost, out of the yard's hearing

In the evening the Stallreeve went up to the hall with the second stick and the Tally-keeper's kept half, and the Assay-master, who had spoken up on the fired pieces, was there. They did not sit. The Stallreeve laid the second stick in the reed-lined chest under the hall with the other kept halves, and the Tally-keeper locked it. The Assay-master said it was a good word, and that he would have liked it said a week sooner and was glad it was not. The Stallreeve said it was the first time he had carried a thing that was not his and not the ring's and not a price. The Tally-keeper said nothing.

The ship owner of the open water (Davin) took the carrier's half home to his landing house and did not hang it. He put it in a reed case in his chest. He told his landing man what it said and that it was to be said as cut. Asked by the man what he would say of the Weightward's instructions he did not promise, he said that a man who promises a pair he has not heard the ring answer promises his own neck. He holds the house's name in his head. He did not say it at the post because the Weightward did not make him answer, and he does not mean to say it before the quay.

The fish seller (Hob Sedge) mended his own cord that night on his step with his wife holding the lamp. He is not angry now. He told his wife the Weightward had read his pan against him in front of the whole yard and he had been told how far, in a thing that was a fact, and that he had been angry for a breath and it was the breath he would not be ashamed of. He took the balance back on the eighth and said nothing.

## What the eleven thought of the second stick in the five months before they sat

They did not meet on it. They talked at the hall's door, at the pans, at the stalls. The positions that matter, and why, are in the sitting below. What they did between: the head of salt and the master of the weavers talked on the hall's step on the evening of the fifth month's first hard frost. The head of salt said the Weightward was honest in the way that costs a town. The master of the weavers said a thing that says what it does not know is either a fool or a thing with nothing to lose, and he did not think it was a fool. The head of the hill goods sent word up the old road with the first walker after the thaw about the stones and the second stick, and said nothing of the seventh seat to the walker.

## The sitting, the fifteenth of the sixth month

A day with no market. The ring met in the afternoon in the hall, the door shut, the Stallreeve at the door, the Tally-keeper at the end of the bench with her half and a fresh stick, her apprentice (Wat) at the door with nothing to call. The second stick lay on the cloth.

**The door.** The head of the hill goods moved that the yard be let in, since the hill kin dislike things set in back rooms. The Assay-master said the yard had heard every word on the stick at the post and would hear the counts at the post from the Stallreeve, who had said he would bring them. The head of salt, the master of the weavers and the master of the smiths said the same, more shortly. Eleven voted: three for (the head of the hill goods, the ship owner of the channels, the master of the boatwrights), eight against.

**Whether to send for the Weightward.** The head of reed and fish asked it. The clay head said it had said it would wait and would not push. The master of the smiths said nothing. The master of the weavers said nothing. The head of salt said he would not have it sent for, and the ring did not send for it, without a count. Nobody moved to have it asked in session what would break it. The master of the smiths, who had a right to move it, did not, and did not look at anyone while the ring went on.

**The stick, thing by thing, in its order.** The Tally-keeper read each thing from the second stick as she had cut it. The Assay-master spoke for each that is a plain figure.

1. The fifth is light by a hair and the hall says so. The Assay-master spoke for it: it was his own reading, light on two trials in Year 2 and light on the first trial in Year 3, and he would not carry a word that said less than his beam saw. For: the Assay-master, the head of the hill goods, the clay head, the master of the potters. The clay head said it cost the ring nothing the coast could not read in 766. The master of the potters said a chip is a chip and the fifth is chipped. Against: the head of salt (a weight is what it is, the fifth is the fifth, the ring has just named the six the town's weight and will not have the town say in the same breath that one of them is light); the master of the weavers (a hair is not a figure, say by how many ones or say nothing); the master of the smiths (a smith files a chip and says nothing); the head of reed and fish (she would not have the town tell the coast its new standard is flawed on the day it is named, and said so dry); the ship owner of the channels (his captains would cut a hair from every price). Not voting: the master of the boatwrights (his men use the old six from next autumn and he would not vote against the bench or for the hair) and the ship owner of the open water (a carrier does not vote on his load, he said, and he says that every time). Four for, five against, two not voting. Not taken.
2. The rack is cut for seven and the seventh is not in it, and the hall does not know what it weighed and has looked for it. The Assay-master spoke for it as the thing he said himself on the first day. Here the ring turned its heads, not far, toward the end of the bench where the Tally-keeper cut. Everyone in the room knows what she holds. Nobody put it to her in session, because she had answered it plainly in the shut sitting of Year 2 and the ring had it. The head of the hill goods said it was true and already said before the yard, and that a king's clerk who wanted it could hear it at any landing. The clay head said the ring had looked in every way open to it and given it up, and a thing looked for and given up is a thing a hall may say it does not know. The master of the smiths, who had asked across the ring where the seventh was, said he would have the hall say it, and that if the south hears a hall say what it does not know it may trust what the hall does say. The head of salt would not vote: a store of worth does not publish its holes, but he would not vote against what was true. The master of the weavers would not vote: he would not tell a coast what the hall did not know, but he did not say it was false. The ship owner of the open water did not vote. Eight for (the Assay-master, the head of reed and fish, the head of the hill goods, the clay head, the master of the potters, the master of the boatwrights, the master of the smiths, the ship owner of the channels), none against, three not voting. Taken. Those who know of the clerk (all eleven) weighed that when the empty seat was put to them, and the head of the hill goods said so aloud to the ring in the plain form: that a kingdom that sends a clerk to look at a keeper's weights will hear this one way or another. They still took it.
3. Four fired pieces set against the first bronze, about a twentieth heavy. The Assay-master spoke for it, a plain figure the head of the hill goods did aloud in the ring. The head of salt was against, as he had said on the day he first heard the sum: every southern captain who bought salt by the hall's pieces has a claim on a twentieth the moment the south hears it. The head of reed and fish said the ring had answered that with the clause that every bargain made before stands as made, and that the clause was his. The ship owner of the open water did not vote. Nine for, one against (the head of salt), one not voting. Taken.
4. The chipped piece and the cracked piece against the eight, about a twentieth and about a tenth. The Assay-master spoke for it. The head of salt was against for the same reason. The master of the weavers did not vote: a tenth is a figure and he would not say whether it is the hall's to say. The ship owner of the open water did not vote. Eight for, one against, two not voting. Taken.
5. The whole ten have not been weighed against the six piece by piece since, and the Tally-keeper has the figures on her stick of the first day. The Assay-master spoke for it. The Tally-keeper was asked plainly by the head of reed and fish whether she would let anyone who can read a notch read it, and answered plainly that she would. The master of the weavers did not vote, saying he had not seen the figures, and the ship owner of the open water did not vote. Nine for, none against, two not voting. Taken.
6. Any captain may bring a scale to the hall and have it read. The Assay-master spoke against: the hall is for the town's weights, a scale read under the hall's roof is said to be the hall's word, and a southern scale on the hall's bench is a thing the ring has not met. The head of salt said no stick should say a southern scale was true. The master of the weavers, the master of the smiths, the clay head, the master of the potters and the head of reed and fish voted against. For: the head of the hill goods (a hall that says it reads any scale and then reads none), the ship owner of the channels (trade), the master of the boatwrights (his men). The ship owner of the open water did not vote and said, once, that this was the one he had not promised. Three for, seven against, one not voting. Not taken.
7. The hall keeps no weight that anyone may not see weighed. The Assay-master did not speak for it, as it is not a figure, and said only that it was how he kept the bench. The room was still. It is a line that fits the old keeper's seventh out for cleaning and everyone in the room knew that. The head of salt said he would vote for that one with both hands. The ship owner of the open water did not vote. Ten for, none against, one not voting. Taken.
8. The asking that the word go in the order said, with nothing added. Ten for, one not voting. Taken.

**The leave for a reason.** The Stallreeve asked the ring for leave to give the Weightward its reasons, since it had asked which were refused so that the next word is not cut short. The Assay-master said the second refusal was a thing of place and not of weight and the ring could give it: a scale is read at the gate like any and the hall is for the town's. The ring agreed to that without a count. The head of salt said the fifth should go with no reason: the fewer reasons the town carries, the fewer it is held to. The ring agreed to that without a count too. The Stallreeve said he would give what he was given and make up nothing.

**What the ring left undone.** The second word for the carrier. The ship owner of the channels asked who would cut it. The Stallreeve said the Weightward had said it would not push. The head of reed and fish said the ring would not ask the Weightward what to cut for the town's own carrier. The clay head said the ring would not have it cut the town's word for it. They left it. The Stallreeve decided afterward, on his own and not as the ring's word, to ask it, at the post, as himself, what it would have cut. He knew it might not answer. He asked.

The ring rose at dusk. The Assay-master, the Stallreeve and the clay head stood at the foot of the old keeper's loft stair for the length of a long breath, and nobody went up it.

## Month 1 to month 7, the rest

- **The landings.** The stones brought are in the public year file and the report. The owners brought what they owned. Nobody brought a stone because they thought it was the seventh, except the old net-maker's son-in-law, who thought it might be and said so to his wife and not to the landing. The channel ship owner brought the first because he wanted it said and did not much believe it.
- **The old weigher.** He lodged with the head of the hill goods and the two of them talked at night of whether the seventh weighs what is owed. The old weigher said it was a boy's saying and that he had been a boy. The head of the hill goods said nothing and counted. The old man went home on the thirteenth of the second month with the carriers.
- **The household at the pans.** Told the night before, the household sat up. The head said they would turn out every basket so there would be nothing to turn. Her husband's brother said the Weightward would find clean salt and it would make no difference, since the town had made up its mind a year ago. She said then let the town's mind be made up with the baskets in front of it. After the Weightward went she sat by the shed with her hands in her lap and then sent her younger son to the head of salt with word that it was done. He said, "Good." The household had borrowed the clean salt to make good the sixth from the head of salt's own store, which is why he took their part, and it was repaid by the pans' last raking of the autumn. He told nobody this.
- **The old net-maker.** Her stone lay at her door from before she married into the house. She believes it came up from the old quay in her husband's grandfather's time, for the cost of carrying. She does not know and does not mind. She refused four offers because the door would blow in without it, not out of any belief that it is anything. The neighbor who caught the boy with the nail is her sister's husband. Her son-in-law did not tell her, until afterward, how the figure had been said at the landing: sixty-one, sixty-one, short by sixty-one.
- **The Assay-master and the stone.** He stood at her step with his hand flat on it and said it was never cut. He did not tell the Stallreeve that his hand was shaking a little. He went home and lit the lamp in the hall's small room before light the next day and sat before the empty seat with the chest locked, as he had after the weighing.
- **The master of the smiths.** He brought the hearth slab to see what the Weightward did with a thing that was not a weight. It read it. He asked nothing because he had settled in himself that he would ask nothing until the ring asked. He did not ask in session because he would not be the man who asked it for the second time and was told no. When the young carrier asked across the yard what would break it and it did not answer, he heard it and went on with his work. He said afterward to the head of the hill goods only: "Held. Then a rope is an edge." The head of the hill goods said it was a rope the ring could hold. The master of the smiths said, "Not if it comes together again."
- **The head of the hill goods.** He told the walker nothing of the seventh seat. He is still against the seat for the reason he gave, and he voted for the plain things at the sitting.
- **The weavers' master.** He watched the post on the fourteenth of the second month for what it did that was not a weight. It walked, looked, and weighed a basket. He told the Stallreeve it weighed a basket. He was satisfied with the answer and not pleased.
- **The potters' master.** She shaped the pieces from the fourth month by her hall's own scale read at the gate. She shaped them to the shrink she knew from her second nest and with a margin she kept in her head and told nobody. She said at the post on the fifth of the seventh month that they were drying on their boards because the Stallreeve asked, not because she meant to say so. She has nothing to hide and she will not volunteer.
