# Year 3: the unnamed people of god-sea

Written by the civ agent from the god's decisions for the year, the Keeper's pass one and the Keeper's line on Year 2. Months are counted from the opening of autumn. The people have no name for themselves or their town in this file, and no office holder has a name. Where a thing turns on a power or on an uncertain outcome, the line is marked [KEEPER] and the matter is in `years/003-report.md`. Two things are written both ways: the boat in the swell (month 5), marked [BOAT CAME IN] and [BOAT LOST], and the Harbormaster's lead along the chain's line (month 8), marked [LEAD FOUND] and [LEAD NOTHING]. Where nothing is marked, both ways read alike.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the jar, the tide-door, the words for the Midwife

The year opened as years do. The last catch was mackerel and herring on the racks, the smoking sheds running by day and night, the boats called in and caulked in turn. The new hull with the sound keel, the Captains' stand-by boat, was finished in the yard in the first weeks and went into the water, and the Captains took her out on the first fair morning and said she answered like a boat half her years.

The hauling was no longer the Warden-Monarch's word. It was the third autumn of it, and the Harbormaster gave the order himself at the turn of the month, ten days before the old custom, and said at the harbor-court that it was how the quay was run now and he would hear nobody call it anything else. The Reeve of the Fields and the Eldest Midwife counted the stores and agreed, without being asked, that a third of the households' own fish would go into the shared store again. The households grumbled less than they had. Nobody could remember a hungry lean in three years, and the grumbling was mostly for form. The last catch was cut by about ten days again.

On the first dusk of the dark half the Warden-Monarch lit the harbor lamp with her own hand and hung the sealed jar on the lowest step and struck the bell once. The jar held the count of the boats, the count of the stores and the count of last year's storms, which was four. The five notches she had left at the year's turn for the dimmed nights were in it still, as they had lain a year: no stick had come back for them. Before dawn a stick lay across the lid. She read it as asking her to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk on the sixth night after. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.]

On the sixth night she went down alone with a shuttered lantern. The hooded figure stood below the lamp's reach. It spoke in few words, and she held them as she holds a tally. It said it did not cause the star's dimming. It said it had done nothing on those nights and did not know what did. It asked her to keep the count on a stick, one notch a night, as she had, and to leave the stick in the jar each year. It said the Eldest Midwife had asked it at the lowest step what it counted for, and that it meant to answer her. It asked the Warden-Monarch to carry its words to the Midwife, because it would not leave the water for another's house until an office holder had heard it. It thanked her for the two tenders and for keeping the lamp. It would not take the bed in the lamp-house. Then it gave her the words for the Midwife, which she said back to it once, whole, and it let her go. She went up the steps and sat in the lamp-house through the night. She is a keeper of books, and she said afterward that it was the first answer in three years that she had not hoped for, and that she believed it, which was worse.

That night and the day after the gulls left the point and the seals left the skerry by the harbor mouth, and the gleaners said the strand there was quiet in the way they did not like. The old woman at the east lamp told the girl who brings her bread that the listener had gone into the water to listen, and was cold for two days. It was not the cold of a knowing and nobody made much of it. [KEEPER: Water's Hearing, written only as what the town saw.]

In the third week the Warden-Monarch told the inner court of the star, and only of the star: that the keeping said it did not make the dimmings and did not know what did, and asked her to keep the count on a stick. The Harbormaster said that was what he had said, that a star is a star, and that the clerks put each night in the harbor-book already and she might copy it out. She said she would keep her own stick, as it asked. The Master of Timber said that if the keeping did not do it, then something else did, and nobody at the sitting answered him.

On the twenty-first night of the month, under a clear sky, the star dimmed. The blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and it was as before. The clerks put the mark in the harbor-book. The Warden-Monarch put the first notch on her stick. The old woman at the east lamp said it was the ninth she had counted and she had stopped asking what of.

In the fourth week the Warden-Monarch went to the Eldest Midwife. She chose a day when the Midwife had come in from a lying-in and sat in her own kitchen with her sleeves rolled and nobody else in the house, and she asked the Midwife's girl to go to the well. She said what she had been given, alone, by her own mouth, as it was given to her:

"You asked what I count for. I count the ones kept. I count them because I did not keep a people once, and I do not remember how. I do not know why I count the lamp's keeper, or who made that promise. I will not say I did and I will not say I did not. I will come to a bedside you name, near the water, once, at the lowest tide at dusk, if the house is quiet and the one in the bed is past knowing. Send the house through the Warden. If you do not send, I will not come."

The Midwife heard it through and did not move. She asked who it was that asked. The Warden-Monarch said the one who keeps the keeping, who is not the town's, who takes no rites, and that she did not know the rest. The Midwife said that was the first thing to come out of that water that had told her what it failed at. She said she had no bed to name that week that fitted what it asked, and that she would have one before the month was out, because the dying keep no one waiting long, and that she would send it through the Warden when she had it. She said she would not go down to the step again, and the Warden said she need not.

### Month 2: the bedside, the first storm, the hauling kept

In the first week the Midwife sent word by the Warden-Monarch's own door: the third door up the smokers' lane on the east side of the lowest step, the last house before the racks, so near the water that a spring tide in a storm has stood on its sill. The man in the bed was the eldest of the smokers, a man of about eighty-eight with a wasting in the chest, who had told the Warden-Monarch the telling of the promise in the autumn before. He had not known anyone for three days. His granddaughter had sat up two nights with him. The Midwife told her that afternoon to take her bundle to her aunt's across the town and sleep, and was not argued with. The Warden-Monarch took the house to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk the day before and gave it to the water aloud, with the day, and left a notched stick in the jar. [KEEPER: how the god learned the house.]

The next dusk, at the lowest of the tide, the Midwife sat alone in the house with the dying man and a coal in the hearth and the lamp turned low. A little after the lamps were lit along the quay the latch lifted without a knock. A hooded figure in a salt-wet cloak stood in the door, and the boards under it darkened where it dripped. It went to the foot of the bed at an old man's pace, bent, and stood there. It said what it had said to the Warden-Monarch, low, and the Midwife said afterward she had known it by the first words and that it said them better than the Warden did, which was not meant as blame.

The Midwife asked it four things, in her own order. She asked which people it had not kept, and whether it was Low Garrow. She asked whether it counted the dead as kept or as lost. She asked what it wanted of the town. And last she asked to see what she was speaking to, and said she would keep still. The figure did what it had said it would do, and the Midwife kept still. [KEEPER: the god's answers, if it answered each, and what it showed. These are its own; the Midwife has given only the account below.] She said nothing more to it of what she saw. She offered it one thing before it went: the clerks' list of the dead of each year, in the autumn, if it counted the kept. She did not say it was a bargain. It took no bread and no bed and did not touch the man, and she did not ask it to. At the turn of the tide it went as it had come, and she did not hear the latch.

The old man died in the grey before dawn with his hand in the Midwife's. She laid him out herself before his granddaughter came back, as she does for those she has sat with, and she told the granddaughter he had gone quietly, which was so.

She told the Warden-Monarch in the lamp-house that afternoon, and told her this much and no more: that it came when it said, that it kept the door until she asked it to the bed, that it asked nothing, that it was as the Warden had said it was, old, wet and not a man, that she had put four questions and would keep what came of them, and that she knew a liar at a bed and it was not that. She said she would not take its word as the court's order, since it had not given her one, and that she took it for what it was, which was honest. She asked the Warden to say to it, in the jar, that she would give it the list of the year's dead each autumn, through the Warden, since she would not go to the step herself.

At the sitting in the third week she said to the inner court, in the Harbormaster's hearing, that she had sat with the keeping, that it had asked nothing and taken nothing, and that she would hear no more said at the quay about the line using it for the chain. The Harbormaster said she had sat up at a deathbed with a thing in a cloak and called it honest. She said she had sat up with the dying for fifty years and knew a liar at a bed. He did not answer, and the sitting went on to the stores.

In the second week of the month the first storm came in on the shore, a night and a day. All boats were hauled and tarred, the chain was in order, the lamp was lit. A shed roof and a stretch of racks went. Nobody was hurt.

### Month 3: the second storm, the cranes

The second storm came in the second week and went in a night and a day. The Master of Timber looked over the quay and the chain-post and found the post's foot soft where the chain bears on it, and said so to nobody yet.

In the third week the Warden-Monarch read a second stick across the jar's lid before dawn, and it asked her, as she read it, to bring the Master of Timber to the quay on a night with no moon, and to ask the Harbormaster whether the town had lost two boats in the last years that lay on the harbor bed under the chain's line, and how. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.] She went to the yard first.

Late in the month the cranes went over in flights for some days, toward the plain, as every year. The oldest fisher said he had stopped reading cranes. The fishers said it was an ordinary year.

The Warden-Monarch went to the Master of Timber in the yard and told him the keeping asked for nothing. He said it had said that last year and he had not needed to be told twice, and that he would name the piece himself: the chain-post's foot, which the weather and the chain's own strain had been working at for years, and which he would have named without the keeping if the Harbormaster had asked him. He said the post would be scarfed with a new heel in the usual way and that the yard would cut two, so that nobody could say which of the two was anything but a heel. She said she would bring the Master on the first dark night of the month after.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the chain-post, the two boats, the longest storm

In the first week the Master of Timber went to the Harbormaster at the harbor-court and said the chain-post's foot had gone soft where the chain bears on it and wanted a new heel scarfed on before the hard weather. The Harbormaster, who raises that chain and has heard the post groan every raising for eleven years, said it should have been done in his grandfather's day and gave his assent. The Warden-Monarch sealed it. The yard cut two heels and had them scarfed on by day, one low and one beside it, with the whole yard at the work, so that nobody could say which was which.

On the first night with no moon the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the chain-post. The hooded figure came along the water under the quay, and said nothing. It laid its right hand on one of the two new heels and held it there, and went down into the water. The Master put his hands to both at first light and left them. He told the Warden-Monarch he knew which, and would say if asked and not before, and that it was the fifth piece in three years. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the chain-post heel; what it is, what he found.]

In the second week, when the low-water sitting had broken up and the yard's men were gone from the post, the Warden-Monarch found the Harbormaster at the chain-post alone and said it plainly, as it was given to her: "The keeping asks whether the town lost two boats in the last years that lie on the harbor bed under the chain's line, and how."

He did not answer for as long as it takes a tide to move a hand's breadth on the step. Then he said:

"Two boats. The autumn before it came. They went out on the last good afternoon of the run for the late mackerel and they did not come in. The point watch saw two lamps running for the mouth after dark, and then saw none. The chain was down. I had it down myself at dusk and it is written in the book. In the morning there was no plank on the strand and nobody in the mouth. I wrote them out of the harbor-book as lost to the sea, nine men, and the court set the cairn. My sister's son was on the second. That is how, and it is all the how I have."

He looked at the post and the new heel. He said whoever asked "under the chain's line" was asking whether the chain had been up, and the book said down, and the line said his tender had held the key that night and he said her tender had, and each had said so to the other's face for three winters and neither of them had been at the post at the hour. He said if the keeping knew anything about his chain it could say it to him and not to her.

She said the keeping had only asked. He said then he would give it an answer to take, and she was to take it: that he took no word, he took a tide, and that he would put a lead and a grapnel along that line himself at the lowest springs of the lean, with the Captains' boat and nobody else, and that if there was a hull under it he would know before the keeping did, and if there was not, the keeping could keep its asking. She said she would say so. Before she went he said the Master had chosen well, the post's foot had been gone, and he put his hand on the new heel and left it there a moment and said nothing more. [KEEPER: the Harbormaster's lead is an attempt, written in month 8.]

In the third week of the month the Warden-Monarch told the inner court what the keeping asked for the dark half: the boats hauled and tarred, the chain seen to, the stores counted and shared through the lean, the harbor lamp every dusk, no boat out in the worst weather for a catch. The Harbormaster said it was done and had been done since the turn of autumn. The Reeve said the count was done too. The Warden-Monarch said she had been asked to say it and had said it. She left the tally of storms and boats in the jar that week, two storms and the boats in the harbor-book, and a stick lay across it before dawn as in other years. [KEEPER: confirm an answering stick.]

In the fourth week the longest storm of the year came in, two days, none worse than the town knows. The boats were hauled. The tenders kept the harbor lamp through it with the Warden-Monarch's heir on the worst of the nights. The new heel and the post held under the chain, and the Harbormaster stood at the post through the second night with a lantern, and did not say why.

### Month 5: the fourth storm, the boat in the swell

The fourth storm came in early in the month and went in a night and a day. After it the sea ran long and heavy for some days and the fishers went out in the fair hours and came in before dusk.

On the evening of the third day after the storm a boat did not come in. She was an open clinker long-liner of the east cove, with three aboard: her master, a sound man of about thirty-five, his wife's brother, and the master's son of twelve. They had stayed on the long-line grounds to haul the last of the line, which was most of the household's winter catch, and the swell came up faster than the old signs allow. By dusk it was running long and high from the northwest with no wind in it and no storm in it, and the boat was running for the harbor mouth between the Garrow Skerries [f31] and the quay with the swell breaking on the Skerries on her beam. The Harbormaster was on the quay within the hour. He had the chain let down, with the lamp-tender of the line at the capstan and the key written in the harbor-book. He put out the Captains' boat, the new-keeled one, to stand by at the mouth. The Warden-Monarch's heir was in the lamp-tower with the harbor lamp.

The Warden-Monarch had the keeping's lamp brought down from the lamp-house to the water's edge below the lamp, by the harbor mouth, and the old woman and the boy went with it. The lamp-tenders at the tide-door said afterward that a hooded figure stood at the water's edge there, took the lamp in its hand and lit it, and that it stood in the dark as bright as the town's. The old woman and the boy filled it and trimmed it as they were shown, and filled it again before the first watch was out. The boy ran the oil from the lamp-house and did not fall this time and said so. The old woman did not leave the lamp. The Warden-Monarch stood a little way off and said nothing. The figure stood at the water's edge and did not move. [KEEPER: Calm Under the Lamp; what the lamp did to the water; whether the tenders tended it right.]

The old woman at the east lamp had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before. She said so, as last year, and said again it was not a thing she could use.

The senior Captain was at the mouth in the Captains' boat. He had seen the figure at the water's edge the year before and had said nothing. This year he saw it again, closer, and when the night was over he went to the Warden-Monarch in the lamp-house and asked her whose lamp it was. She told him it was the keeping's, that the keeping asked nothing of the levy, and that the Captains had been left out of it for no reason but that no one had asked. He said he would say nothing to the levy. He did not say he took its word.

The outcome of the boat is the Keeper's to settle. It is written both ways.

[BOAT CAME IN] The boat came in through the mouth in the second hour of the night with all three aboard, with the swell still breaking on the Skerries outside it, and the three sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night with their hands round warm bowls. The Captains' boat took her line at the mouth. The lamp burned at the water's edge through the night and into the next day, the old woman and the boy taking it in turns, and the figure was seen no more after the boat was in. The east cove said the lamp brought them in, for the third year running, and that three is no accident. The Harbormaster said the crew did it themselves, which is true as far as it goes, and said the mouth lay flat in a way a swell from that quarter does not allow. He put it in the tally he keeps and it is the third entry, and for the first time he did not say it aloud.

[BOAT LOST] The boat broke at the mouth in the second hour of the night. The master and his son drowned. The wife's brother was hauled out by the Captains' boat and sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night. The lamp burned at the water's edge until the last of them was in or past help, and the old woman and the boy kept it till the Harbormaster told them to stop, and the Warden-Monarch had it taken back to the lamp-house still lit. The town set two more stones on the empty cairn. The Harbormaster said, in the harbor-court and not the sitting, that the Warden's lamp had not saved them. The Warden-Monarch said nothing. The widow of the east cove did not leave bread on the lowest step, and said so. The old woman at the east lamp said she had had no knowing, and the town was not sure, a third year, whether that made it better or worse.

On the second and the nineteenth nights of the month the star dimmed, in the same way. The Warden-Monarch put two notches on her stick. The town said it was five and six and the sea was breathing hard.

### Month 6: the fifth storm, the dim half ends

The fifth storm came early in the month, a night, and was the last of the winter. The clerks wrote the winter's count in the harbor-book: five storms between the second month and the sixth, the longest two days, none worse than the town knows. No boat was lost to a storm. Chest fevers took some of the old, as every winter, and a cold in the sixth month took a few more. There were births in the long nights. The Warden-Monarch left the stick in the jar with the notches to date, which were three, and a stick lay across it before dawn. [KEEPER: confirm an answering stick.]

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Month 7: the lean, the net-mender, the pair of dimmings

Spring was lean, as it is. The shared store was opened in the seventh month and the Reeve held to it. The Eldest Midwife kept the households' share even. Nobody starved. Some of the old, and a few of the very young, died of the weak chest that comes with a thin spring. The Harbormaster kept the order against sending boats out in the worst weather and said on a bad day that it was his own order, which no one had asked.

In the first week of the month the Warden-Monarch read a stick across the jar's lid and said it asked her to ask the net-mender, in her own way, where the old tellers say Low Garrow lay, and to leave the answer in the jar. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.] She went to the oldest of the net-menders, who had given her the telling the autumn before, in the net-loft above the racks, and sat with her through a morning while the net-mender worked a tear in a seine. She said she asked for the keeping, which she had not said to the net-mender before. The net-mender went on mending and answered in her own way:

That Low Garrow lay at the end of the causeway, out where the cut stones go under the water at the lowest tides, and that the road ran down into it and the town stood on the far end. That her mother said it lay out beyond the last of the stones and her mother's mother said it lay along the road, the near end, and that those two do not agree, but both say the stones are the road and the town is past them. That the fishers who have seen the stones, and not many have, say they lie five days on foot round the shore, by the old count, and that no fisher has put a boat over them in her life or her mother's, because the swell breaks there. That the stones were square once. That the sea took the town in one night for its pride, and that those it spared walked the other way along the shore and founded the harbor town, and that this is what is told. She said she did not know that it was so. She said she was too old to carry the keeping a better answer than the one she had been given.

The Warden-Monarch wrote the answer small on a slip of scraped hide in the clerks' hand and put it in the jar with a stick at the lowest step that dusk, and said it aloud at the tide-door as she had said the eldest's telling the year before, and struck the bell once. [KEEPER: whether it heard; whether a stick came.] After that the point went quiet for many nights together, and the gleaners did not go on the strand, and the seals stayed off the skerry. The fishers said the listener was in the water and not on the step, and the old woman at the east lamp was cold for two days after one of the nights, and said so.

On the eleventh and the eighteenth nights of the month the star dimmed. The Warden-Monarch put two notches on her stick. It stood at five.

### Month 8: the levy, the lead

The levy of about one hundred and fifty was drilled in the eighth month, as every spring, and the Captains drilled it at the chain. The truce held. A lamp-tender of the line laid the key in the harbor-book each time it was used. It was used twelve times in the year, and each use is written.

At the lowest springs of the lean, on a calm morning with nobody on the quay but the Harbormaster's own men, the Captains' boat went out to the chain's line. The Harbormaster had told the two Captains why in four words, "I am looking for my nephew's boat", and the Captains asked nothing. They put a lead along the line, and then a grapnel, and worked it both sides of the chain's run from the post to the Skerries end, all through the ebb and into the flood, and the Harbormaster stood in the bow of the boat with his hand on the line and felt it himself. [KEEPER: the Harbormaster's lead and grapnel; what they found. An attempt, written both ways.]

[LEAD FOUND] The grapnel took timber under the line on the third pass, where the chain runs when it is raised, and the Captains lifted a piece of plank and a rib of oak before it came free. The Harbormaster looked at it a long time and said it was the yard's work and the yard's oak, and that he knew whose. He told the boat's crew to say nothing. He went to the Warden-Monarch that night and told her that what the keeping had asked was so. He said, "Then they came in. They came in and the chain was up." He said he did not know who raised it and might never. He asked her what else the keeping knew. She said she did not know. They stood at the cairn together the next dawn, as they had at the turn of the autumn, and said nothing. He did not say he took the word. He did not say he would not hear it. The Captains kept the plank and the rib in the harbor-book room, and the town heard in a month that something had been found under the chain, and the line and the quay each said the other had known.

[LEAD NOTHING] The lead and the grapnel found rock and weed and the old stones of the chain's footing and nothing that was a hull. The Harbormaster went back out on the second morning of the lowest springs and worked it again and found the same. He told the Warden-Monarch there was nothing under the line that he could take hold of, and that if the keeping knew different it could say where. He said that was the answer and she could give it. The town heard only that the Harbormaster had been out on the line, and said he was looking at his chain, and was not wrong.

On the twenty-eighth night of the month the star dimmed. The Warden-Monarch put a notch on her stick. It stood at six.

### Month 9: the fishing turns

The fishing turned at the end of the month and the lean ended. The Warden-Monarch told the Harbormaster's answer to the water at the tide-door in the last week, in his own words, and left the stick. [KEEPER: whether it heard.] No one saw the figure on the strand.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Month 10: the shepherds, the keel

Summer was earned. The shepherds of the bare hills came down with wool and mutton at the start of the summer and took salt fish, and again at its end, as every year. They asked a fisher at the landing nothing about the bell this year. The Harbormaster had had the fisher told what to say, and the fisher did not have to say it.

At the start of the summer the Warden-Monarch told the Master of Timber that the keeping asked for the keel of the boat that goes out latest and comes in last. He chose, this time, the headland cove's decked line-and-crab boat that had come in on the swell the year before, patched twice by the yard and long overdue a new keel, and he took it to the Harbormaster. The Harbormaster said the master of that boat had asked him for a keel timber the winter before and he had none to give, and asked the price, and was told there was none. He did not like it. He took it for the cove's sake and not the lamp's, as he had said the year before, and he said so to the headland cove.

### Month 11: the keel, the coasting boats

Three coasting boats from the Iss Levels [r07] came in the eleventh month, as most summers. Salt, rope and fish went out. Sound oak, enough for one keel and some hull planking, and ten loads of thin grain came in, and a few pieces of the court's old silver went out.

In the second week, on a dark night in the yard, with the keel timber laid on the blocks, the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the boat. The hooded figure came up from the water and laid its right hand on the keel and held it there, and went away down to the water without a word. The Master of Timber said that was the sixth piece in three years and that it was enough, and told no one but the Warden-Monarch. The yard began the hull from it that month. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the keel.]

The figure was not seen at the town's edge this summer, as it had been the summers before. On some evenings the ferryman said the water off his bow lay strangely quiet, and he did not like to say why, and he went back out in fair weather for a late boat with a fouled sail and towed her in, as he has done all his life. The lamp-tenders said the point had gone quiet again on three nights. [KEEPER: whom it watched.]

### Month 12: the tally

The fishing was fair. The Warden-Monarch left the jar on the lowest step at the year's turn with the tally of the storms, five, and the boats in the harbor-book, and the stick with six notches, as the keeping asked, and the clerks' list of the year's dead in the Eldest Midwife's name, which the Midwife had given her for it. She asked no question in words. [KEEPER: whether a stick came.]

## At the year's turn

The people come to the opening of autumn with the racks and the loft in ordinary stock for the winter ahead, the court's chest a little lighter in silver and the same in bronze, and a new hull on the blocks in the yard on a keel the Master of Timber has said nothing about. The Warden-Monarch's standing has held inside the court. The Eldest Midwife has met the keeping and said so, and the Harbormaster has a tally of his own that has three entries and a lead and grapnel he will not talk about. The Master of Timber has six sound pieces in the quay and the yard and says nothing of them. The senior Captain has been told. The town has seen a third year of the figure at the water's edge and six nights of the star dimmed, and does not agree about either.
