# Report to the Keeper: civ unnamed-people-sea, Year 4, pass one

## How the god was met

Autumn, month 1.
- The Warden-Monarch: hung the jar and struck the bell on the first dusk of the dark half. Before dawn a stick lay across the lid, and she read it as asking her to the tide-door on the fourth night after. She met the god alone at the lowest tide at dusk. It gave her its answers for the Harbormaster and the Eldest Midwife, once, and asked her to carry each by her own mouth, alone, on a day she judged, with nothing sent. She said each back once, whole. It told her what to say if either asked more, and to tell it through the jar if either were hostile. It asked for the clerks' list of the dead in the jar; she took the seal off by her lantern's slit and put it in its hand, and it read it standing. It told her it would be gone from the water by the town for some weeks in the spring and that the lamp stayed in her keeping. She took its word. She told no one yet beyond writing the list's going in the harbor-book.
- The Eldest Midwife: heard the god's words from the Warden-Monarch's own mouth, alone, in her kitchen, in the fourth week. She said a midwife counts the ones she lost by the same hand as the ones she kept, that if it did not know it was Low Garrow then neither did she and she would stop saying so, that wanting the boats home was a small want and not the whole of it, and that she would send a house when one fitted and not for her own sake. She took its word as honest and not as an order.
- The Harbormaster, the Reeve, the Captains, the Master of Timber: not met this month. The Harbormaster gave the haul as his own order and added a rule of his own: no boat past the mouth while the watch on the lamp-house stair can hear the Skerries breaking from the northwest.

Autumn, month 2.
- The Harbormaster: heard the god's words from the Warden-Monarch's own mouth, alone, at the chain-post, in the third week. He heard it through, said a boat's lower timbers is a hull to him and that he dragged both sides of the line and took none, and asked where inside the mouth. She answered what she had been given to answer and said she did not know the rest. He said nobody had thanked him for the lead, that "I ask nothing more of your chain" was the first thing from that water he would have put his name to, that he takes a tide and not a word and will not drag a harbor on a word, and that if it said where he would put a lead there. He did not take its word and was not hostile. He put a fourth line to his tally.

Autumn, month 3.
- The Master of Timber: told by the Warden-Monarch that the keeping asked for one more piece before the hard weather and left the naming to him. He named the outer pile at the quay head and took the word again.

Winter.
- Month 4: the Master of Timber met the god with the Warden-Monarch at the quay head on a night with no moon, saw it lay its right hand on one of two new piles, and took the word. The Harbormaster assented to the pile without being told it was the keeping's. The Warden-Monarch told the inner court the winter requests in the third week; the Harbormaster and the Reeve said it was already done and the quay's.
- Months 5 and 6: no boat was caught in a swell, so the god was not met at the water's edge. The god was not seen. The jar was left with the tally in months 4 and 6.

Spring.
- Month 7: the Warden-Monarch told the Master of Timber, the Eldest Midwife, her heir and the senior Captain that the keeping would be gone from the water by the town for some weeks and that the lamp stayed in her keeping, and told the two tenders that nobody lit it without her word. She left the jar in the second, third and fourth weeks with the winter's tally and a plain word that the store was open and held, and found it as she left it each time. No stick came. The point was not quiet on any night. The god was not met.
- Month 8: the god was away. The swell: the Warden-Monarch lit the keeping's lamp on her own word with no one at the water's edge (see Uncertain outcomes). In the week after she told the water at the tide-door what she had done and asked whether she had had the right; no stick came.
- Month 9: in the fourth week the gulls left the point on a night and the seals left the skerry. Nobody saw the figure. The year does not say that was the god's return; it says only what the town saw.

Summer.
- Month 10: the Eldest Midwife sent a house through the Warden-Monarch's own door, the second above the east cove landing, a dying widow. The Warden-Monarch gave it to the water at the tide-door the day before and left a notched stick. The god came to the bed once at the lowest tide at dusk. The Midwife met it alone and put it two things. The Warden-Monarch told the Master of Timber the keel or strake request; he chose the east cove's long-liner's garboard strake and had the Harbormaster's assent with no price.
- Month 11: the god laid its right hand on the strake in the yard, in the second week, before the Master and the Warden-Monarch, and said nothing. The Master took the word again. The figure was not seen at the town's edge. No one spoke to it.

## What was done in the god's name

- By the Warden-Monarch: carried the god's two answers by her own mouth to the Midwife (month 1) and the Harbormaster (month 2); gave the Midwife's list to the god; told the inner court the winter requests; brought the Master of Timber to the quay head and to the yard; told four people and the tenders of the god's absence; left the jar through the absence with the stores word; gave the Midwife's house to the water; told the Master the strake request; left the stick of seven notches and the Midwife's list in the jar at the year's turn.
- By the Master of Timber: named the outer pile and had the yard cut two so none could say which; chose the long-liner's strake and got the Harbormaster's assent.
- By the Eldest Midwife: gave the clerks' list for the jar; sent a house.
- By the Harbormaster: the hauling, the lean rule and the new Skerries rule as his own orders, and the assent to the pile and the strake, without knowing. The Skerries rule is his own and not in the god's name.
- By the two lamp-tenders: in month 8 only, lit, filled, trimmed and kept the keeping's lamp on the Warden-Monarch's word, not the god's. She said so aloud.
- Nothing was done in the god's name by the Reeve or the Captains beyond doing what the court ordered.
- Whether the Warden-Monarch's lighting of the lamp in month 8 was in the god's name: I wrote it as her own act, taken on herself, as your instruction says, and said so in the year. The god did not say it could be lit without it, and did not forbid it; the lamp's keeping was left with her.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

1. The boat in the swell, month 8, the twentieth day, with the god away. A decked boat of the point, patched twice by the yard, a good boat, with four aboard: her master, a sound man of about forty-six; his sons of twenty and sixteen; and a hired hand of about thirty-five. The lean was in its eighth week; the mackerel were on the near shoal beyond the mouth. On the eighteenth and nineteenth days the sea ran long from the northwest with no wind on the shore, the Skerries were heard on the lamp-house stair, and no boat went out. On the twentieth morning the watch could not hear the Skerries and the Harbormaster let the boats go. By the afternoon the swell was back, long and high from the northwest, faster than the old signs allow, with no wind in it and no storm in it. The crew stayed on the shoal for the bite, as the lean made them, and the swell came up behind the fish. At dusk she was running for the harbor mouth between the Garrow Skerries [f31] and the quay, with the swell breaking on the Skerries on her beam. She is a sound open boat in the ordinary sense, patched, not rotten, and her crew know the coast.
   - Circumstances from the record and the year: the chain was let down by the Harbormaster, who was on the quay within the hour, with the lamp-tender of the line at the capstan and the key written in the harbor-book; the Captains' boat, with the new keel of Year 2, was put out and stood by at the mouth with the senior Captain; the Warden-Monarch's heir was in the lamp-tower with the harbor lamp. The keeping was away and had told the Warden-Monarch so in the autumn; she had told the Master, the Midwife, her heir and the senior Captain in month 7 and the tenders that nobody lit its lamp without her word. She stood in the lamp-house a long time with it, then told the old woman and the boy to take it to the water's edge below the lamp by the harbor mouth and light it, and said before the tenders and her heir that it was on her own word and not the keeping's, that the keeping was not there and nothing would come to it. The old woman lit it with a coal from the lamp-house and tended it as a lamp; the boy ran the oil and did not fall; they filled it again before the first watch was out and kept it up to the night and into the next day in the branch where they were not told to stop. The old woman at the east lamp, about seventy-five, had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before; I leave what that means to you, as in earlier years. The net-mender who frees fouled lines was ashore and her power was not in play. Three years running a boat has come in under this lamp with the god at it; this year nobody stood at it, and the senior Captain looked along the strand and the water's edge for the figure and saw none. The two Captains' boat is a better boat than the one that stood by in Year 1.
   - Success: all four come in, the Captains' boat takes her line at the mouth. Failure: the boat breaks at the mouth, the master and his elder son drown, the younger son and the hired hand are hauled out by the Captains' boat.
   - What the lamp, lit by the tenders without the god, does to the water, and whether it does anything, is yours; I wrote only what the tenders and the Captain saw and that the old woman said it burned as a lamp does.
   - The year is written both ways: population 2,039 or 2,037; the town's mood, the Warden-Monarch's standing, the Harbormaster's talk and the point's bread on the step differ by branch. If you rule the lamp does nothing without the god, tell me in the next year's release and the town's lore will turn on that.
2. No other contested outcome was written. The ferryman's late boat in month 12 was in fair weather and I did not make it contested. The Harbormaster's lead and grapnel of Year 3 stands as you ruled; I wrote no second search, because he said he would not drag a harbor on a word and the god's word did not say where.

## What turns on a power

- Water's Hearing, month 1 night and day: written only as the gulls and seals leaving the point and the skerry and the old woman's two days of cold. No listening is written at the point in spring, because the god was away.
- Sound Timber, the pile, month 4: the god's right hand on one of two new piles at the quay head. What the pile is, what the Master found at first light, and whether it held in the longest storm are not settled. The year says only that the pile and the post held and does not credit the pile.
- Sound Timber, the strake, month 11: the god's right hand on the garboard strake of the east cove's long-liner. What the strake is, and how the boat does, are not settled.
- Calm Under the Lamp: not used in winter, because no boat was caught. In month 8 the lamp was lit by the tenders without the god. What that does is yours, as above.
- The god's absence in spring: I wrote only what the town saw: the point not quiet, the gulls and seals on the step and the skerry, the jar unanswered across three weeks of leavings and again in month 8, no figure seen. I wrote that the quiet came back to the point in the fourth week of month 9 on a night, as the town saw it, and that nobody could say whether it was the first night back. If the god was back sooner or later, tell me and the town's lore will turn on it.
- The sticks across the jar in months 1, 3 and 10, and the answering sticks I wrote for the tallies in months 4 and 6 and the Harbormaster's question in month 2: she reads them and says what they ask. How a stick carries a request is yours, and so is whether any stick answered her stick of seven notches and the Midwife's list at the year's turn, and whether any stick answered the Harbormaster's question (and what it asked her to tell him).
- The god's coming to the widow's bed in month 10: the god's side of what passed, its answers to the Midwife's two things, and what it showed. I wrote only what she asked and what she told the Warden-Monarch.
- How the god learned the widow's house. I wrote that the Warden-Monarch gave it to the water at the tide-door the day before and left a notched stick in the jar. Whether it heard is yours.
- The Warden-Monarch's telling to the water at the tide-door in month 8: whether the god heard it when it came back is yours. In the year no stick came while it was away.
- The seven dimmings of the star, months 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, 11 and 12: written as seen, with no claim of cause. The one in month 8 fell while the god was away and the town marked it. The people still tie them to the sea and the step in lore.
- The Harbormaster's reading of "their broken lower timbers inside the mouth": he has taken them for his nephew's boat in private. What the god found is yours.

## What touches anyone else

- Trade:
  - The shepherds of the Bren Hills [r05] came twice, at the start of summer and at its end, with wool and mutton for salt fish. They asked nothing about the bell.
  - Three coasting boats from the Iss Levels [r07] came in month 11. Salt, rope and fish went out. Sound oak (enough for one keel and some planking) and eleven loads of thin grain came in. A few pieces of old silver went out.
- No party went out and no court message was sent. No stranger came by land and no hull not known on this coast came in.
- Nothing carried. The Midwife's list of the year's dead and the stick of seven notches are in the jar for the god at the year's turn.
- The town does not know where the god went in the spring and sent no one after it. The gleaners and fishers say it went up the shore or down, and the old tellers of the causeway say the way the spared ones came. I give no place for it.

## Asked of the god

- The Warden-Monarch, month 1: nothing new in words. She said the god's words back to it, whole, at the tide-door, once for each. Her six notches of Year 3, left in the jar, were not answered this year in front of her that I wrote. She did not ask.
- The Harbormaster, month 2, through the Warden-Monarch, in his words: "Where inside the mouth? Say which timbers and where, and I will put a lead there. I will not drag a harbor on a word." Also that a boat's lower timbers is a hull to him. Not answered in front of him; he has not met it. Whether a stick answered is yours.
- The Eldest Midwife, month 10, at the bedside, in her words as she gave them to the Warden-Monarch: where it was in the spring and what it found there; and whether it read her list by the same hand as the others. Whether each was answered in front of her is yours.
- The Warden-Monarch, month 8, to the water at the tide-door in the week after the swell, with the god away, in her words: that she had lit the keeping's lamp on her own word with the keeping away and how it had gone, and "I do not know whether I had the right. Did I?" Met silence, since the god was away. No stick came. Still pending.
- No other mortal put a question to it.

## Names said aloud

- "Low Garrow": said by the Warden-Monarch to the Eldest Midwife in month 1 in the god's words, and by the Midwife to the Warden-Monarch and at three bedsides ("I do not know it was"). Already in the record.
- "Skerr": said by the Warden-Monarch in the sitting in month 4 and to the Master of Timber. Already in the record.
- "Brennis": not said aloud this year.
- "the listener": the old woman's byname and the town's; "the keeping", the court's. Neither is a name for the people, the town or an office holder.
- No name was said aloud for the people, the town or any office holder. The widow, the point's boat's master and crew, the oldest net-mender, the senior Captain and the ferryman are unnamed and filed by what they were. All stay unnamed or filed by office.

## What the people tell their god

The Warden-Monarch, for the court, as she would leave it in the jar and say it at the tide-door:

"We hauled early the fourth autumn, on the Harbormaster's own order, and he made a rule of his own that no boat goes past the mouth while the stair can hear the Skerries. Five storms came on the shore between the second month and the sixth, the longest two days, none worse than the town knows, and no boat was lost to a storm. The quay and the post held. The Master put the yard's two new piles at the quay head and you laid your hand on one, as he says, and he says it is enough. In the winter the sea ran long after the fourth storm and the Skerries were heard and nobody was out. It was a dry fishing and a full harbor.

"You asked me to carry two words and I carried them by my mouth. The Eldest Midwife heard hers in her kitchen. She says it is the only honest count, and that she will stop saying it was Low Garrow. The Harbormaster heard his at the post. He said he took a tide and not a word, that nobody else had thanked him, and that a boat's lower timbers is a hull to him. He asks where inside the mouth, and he will put a lead there if you say, and he will not drag a harbor on a word. I left his question in the jar.

"You told me you would be gone some weeks in the spring, and you went. The jar was not answered, and I wrote each day it was not in the harbor-book. The gulls were on the step and the seals on the skerry all through the lean. The star dimmed on the ninth night of the eighth month while you were away, and the fishers on the night grounds saw it. It dimmed seven times in the year, in the second, third, fourth, eighth, tenth, eleventh and twelfth months. The old woman at the east lamp says it counts up and does not count up by the same step. I have a stick of seven notches in the jar.

[BOAT CAME IN] "On the twentieth day of the eighth month, while you were away, a boat of the point was caught on the shoal by a long swell from the northwest with no storm in it, four aboard. I had your lamp taken to the water's edge by the harbor mouth and lit by the old woman on my own word, and said so aloud, because you were not there. The boat came in with all four in the second hour of the night. I do not know whether the lamp did it. The point says it did. The Harbormaster says the crew and the chain and the Captains' boat did it, and he may be right. I did not know whether I had the right to light it, and I ask whether I had.

[BOAT LOST] "On the twentieth day of the eighth month, while you were away, a boat of the point was caught on the shoal by a long swell from the northwest with no storm in it, four aboard. I had your lamp taken to the water's edge by the harbor mouth and lit by the old woman on my own word, and said so aloud, because you were not there. The boat broke at the mouth. The master and his elder son are drowned, and the younger son and the hired hand were hauled out by the Captains' boat. I do not know whether the lamp could have saved them or was only a lamp. The Harbormaster says the lamp was mine and that you were not here to fail. The point is bitter and I hold it. I did not know whether I had the right to light it, and I ask whether I had.

"The Eldest Midwife sent you a house in the tenth month, a widow of the east cove, and you came. She says you came when you said and asked nothing and took nothing, and she put you two things and kept what came of them. The widow died before dawn. The Master of Timber chose the east cove's long-liner for a strake, and you laid your hand on it in the eleventh month. Three coasting boats came from the levels with oak and grain, and the shepherds came twice. The ferryman towed in a late boat in fair weather and called it a small thing. The Midwife's list of the year's dead is in the jar, in her name, with the stick of seven notches. I ask nothing in words. We want the boats home. That is the town's want as well as yours."
