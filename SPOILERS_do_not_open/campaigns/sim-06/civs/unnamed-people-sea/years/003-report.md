# Report to the Keeper: civ unnamed-people-sea, Year 3, pass one

## How the god was met

Autumn, month 1.
- The Warden-Monarch: hung the jar and struck the bell on the first dusk of the dark half. Before dawn a stick lay across the lid, and she read it as asking her to the tide-door on the sixth night after. She met the god alone at the lowest tide at dusk. It told her it does not cause the star's dimming, did nothing on those nights and does not know what does; asked her to keep the count on a stick, one notch a night, and leave it in the jar each year; said the Eldest Midwife had asked it what it counts for and that it means to answer her; asked her to carry its words to the Midwife; thanked her for the tenders and the lamp; would not take the lamp-house bed. She said the words back once, whole. She took its word. She told the inner court only about the star and the count, in the third week.
- The Eldest Midwife: heard the god's words from the Warden-Monarch's own mouth, alone, in her kitchen, in the fourth week. She asked who asked. The Warden answered what she was told (the one who keeps the keeping, not the town's, takes no rites, does not know the rest). The Midwife said it was the first thing out of that water to tell her what it failed at, and that she would name a bed within the month. She did not take its word yet.
- The Harbormaster, the Reeve, the Captains, the Master of Timber: not met this month. The Harbormaster gave the haul as his own order, as a custom now, and the Reeve and the Midwife agreed the shared store without being asked. The Master of Timber heard the star answer from the Warden-Monarch at the sitting.

Autumn, month 2.
- The Eldest Midwife met the god in person, alone, at the bedside she named: the eldest of the smokers, about eighty-eight, past knowing three days, in the third door up the smokers' lane on the east side of the lowest step, the last house before the racks, close enough to the water that a spring tide in a storm has stood on its sill. The granddaughter was sent across the town. At the lowest tide at dusk the figure came to the door without knocking, went to the bed's foot, and said its words. She put four questions and asked to see it, and kept still. She told no one what she saw beyond that it was as the Warden had said, old, wet and not a man. It took no bread and no bed and did not touch the man. It went at the turn of the tide. The man died before dawn of his wasting. Afterward she told the Warden-Monarch that it came when it said, kept the door until asked, asked nothing, and was honest, and that she would not take its word as the court's order. At the next sitting she told the inner court she had sat with it, that it asked nothing, and that she would hear no more said about the line using it for the chain. The Harbormaster answered her sharply. I count her as having taken its word for what it is and not for an order. Whether that makes her office a helper is yours.

Autumn, month 3.
- The Master of Timber: heard from the Warden-Monarch that the keeping asks for nothing, and said it had said so last year. Took its word. He named the chain-post's foot as the piece.

Winter.
- Month 4: the Master of Timber met the god with the Warden-Monarch at the chain-post on a night with no moon, saw it lay its right hand on one of two renewed heels, and took the word again. The Harbormaster assented to the post repair without being told, and did not know the heel was the keeping's. The Warden-Monarch asked the Harbormaster the god's question about the two boats; he did not take the word, answered it, and sent an answer back (see Asked of the god). He said the keeping could say what it knows about his chain to him and not to her.
- Month 4: the Warden-Monarch told the inner court the winter requests in the third week. The Harbormaster and the Reeve said it was already done.
- Month 5: the swell. The god was seen at the water's edge by the harbor mouth by the Warden-Monarch, the old woman, the boy, the lamp-tenders at the tide-door and the Captains' boat crew. It lit the lamp and stood. It spoke to no one. The senior Captain, who had seen it the year before, asked the Warden-Monarch whose lamp it was; she told him the keeping's. He said he would tell the levy nothing. He did not say he took its word. The Harbormaster was on the quay, had the chain let down and put out the Captains' boat, and did not come to the water's edge.

Spring.
- Month 7: the Warden-Monarch put the god's question to the oldest of the net-menders and carried the answer to the tide-door and the jar. The god was not met. No one spoke to it on the strand. The point went quiet on many nights.
- Month 8: the Harbormaster, who does not take the word, put a lead and grapnel along the chain's line himself (see Uncertain outcomes). The god was not met.
- Month 9: the Warden-Monarch told the Harbormaster's answer to the water at the tide-door.

Summer.
- Month 10: the Warden-Monarch told the Master of Timber the keeping asked for the keel of the boat that goes out latest and comes in last. He chose the headland cove's decked line-and-crab boat that came in on last year's swell and got the Harbormaster's assent with no price asked, on the ground that its master had asked him for a keel timber the winter before. The Harbormaster does not know the keel is the keeping's.
- Month 11: the god laid its right hand on the keel in the yard, in the second week, before the Master and the Warden-Monarch, and said nothing. The Master took the word again. The figure was not seen at the town's edge this summer. No one spoke to it.

## What was done in the god's name

- By the Warden-Monarch: carried the god's words to the Midwife; gave the house to the water; told the inner court of the star and of the count; kept a stick of six notches, one a night, and left it in the jar at the year's turn; relayed the winter requests; brought the Master of Timber to the quay and to the yard; asked the Harbormaster about the two boats; asked the net-mender where Low Garrow lay and left the answer in the jar with a spoken telling; carried the Harbormaster's answer to the water; told the senior Captain whose lamp it was; told the Master the keel request and that the keeping asks nothing; left the Midwife's list of the year's dead in the jar.
- By the Master of Timber: named the chain-post's foot and had the yard cut two heels so none could say which; chose the headland cove's crab boat for the keel and got the Harbormaster's assent.
- By the Eldest Midwife: told the inner court she had sat with it; gave the Warden-Monarch the clerks' list of the dead to leave in the jar.
- By the two lamp-tenders: filled, trimmed and kept the lamp in month 5, as shown.
- By the Harbormaster: the hauling and the lean rule, as his own order and the court's, and the assent to the post repair and to the keel, without knowing. The lead and grapnel on the chain's line was his own act on a question the Warden carried; he does not say it was done for the keeping.
- Nothing was done in the god's name by the Reeve or the Captains beyond doing what the court ordered.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

1. The boat in the swell, month 5, the third evening after the fourth storm. An open clinker long-liner of the east cove, with three aboard: her master, a sound man of about thirty-five; his wife's brother; and the master's son of twelve. They stayed on the long-line grounds to haul the last of the line, most of the household's winter catch, and the swell came up faster than the old signs allow. At dusk a long heavy swell from the northwest with no wind in it and no storm in it. The boat was running for the harbor mouth between the Garrow Skerries [f31] and the quay with the swell breaking on the Skerries on her beam.
   - Circumstances from the record and the year: the chain was let down by the Harbormaster, who was on the quay within the hour, with the lamp-tender of the line at the capstan and the key written in the harbor-book; the Captains' boat, the new-keeled stand-by boat, was put out and stood by at the mouth; the Warden-Monarch's heir was in the lamp-tower with the harbor lamp; the Warden-Monarch had the keeping's lamp brought to the water's edge by the harbor mouth below the lamp; the hooded figure took it and lit it by its hand and stood at the water's edge. The boat is a sound open boat, not old or rotten. The crew know the coast and stayed too long for the sake of the line, not for want of skill. Two years running a boat has come in under this lamp, and the Captains' boat is a better boat than the one that stood by last year.
   - What the lamp-tenders did: the old woman at the east lamp (about seventy-four) and the boy (about fifteen) filled and trimmed the lamp as shown, filled it again before the first watch was out; the boy ran the oil from the lamp-house and did not fall; the old woman did not leave the lamp. I do not know whether they tended it right; last year you said they did. They kept it until the Harbormaster told them to stop in the lost branch, or through the night and into the next day in the other.
   - The old woman's record gives her a knowing a day ahead when a boat will not come back, with a cold to the bone after. She had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before. I leave what that means to you, as last year. The net-mender who frees fouled lines was ashore and her power was not in play.
   - Success: all three come in, the Captains' boat takes her line at the mouth. Failure: the boat breaks at the mouth, the master and his son drown, the wife's brother is hauled out by the Captains' boat.
   - The year is written both ways: population 2,031 or 2,029; the town's mood, the Warden-Monarch's standing, the Harbormaster's talk and the east cove's bread on the step differ by branch.
2. The Harbormaster's lead and grapnel along the chain's line, month 8, at the lowest springs of the lean, from the Captains' boat with the two Captains and the Harbormaster, along the chain's run from the post to the Skerries end, through the ebb and into the flood, and again the second morning if nothing was found. The cause: the Warden-Monarch carried the god's question about the two boats; he answered that the town lost two boats the autumn before the keeping came (nine men, the second carrying his sister's son), that they went out on the last good afternoon of the run, were seen as two lamps running for the mouth after dark and then none, that the chain was down by his order and the book says so, that nothing came ashore, and that the line says his tender held the key and he says hers did, and neither was at the post at the hour. He said he takes a tide, not a word, and would look himself.
   - Circumstances from the record: the post's foot was found soft and was scarfed that winter. The people believe the boats were lost at sea and some whisper the chain.
   - Success: the grapnel or the lead takes timber that is a boat under the line. Failure: nothing is found that is a hull.
   - Written both ways in month 8 and in lore and state: [LEAD FOUND], [LEAD NOTHING]. If found, he tells only the Warden-Monarch and the crew know; the quarrel over the chain is held, not mended, and he asks what else the keeping knows. If nothing, he says so to the Warden-Monarch and the keeping may say where.
3. No other contested outcome was written. The ferryman's late boat in month 11 was in fair weather and was not made contested.

## What turns on a power

- Water's Hearing, month 1 night and day, and again on many nights through the lean and in month 11: written only as the gulls and seals leaving the point and the skerry and the old woman's two days of cold.
- Sound Timber, the chain-post heel, month 4: the god's right hand on one of two renewed heels. What the heel is, what the Master found at first light, and whether it held in the longest storm are not settled. The year says only that the post held and does not credit the heel.
- Sound Timber, the keel, month 11: the god's right hand on the keel of the headland cove's crab boat. What the keel is, and how the boat does, are not settled.
- Calm Under the Lamp, month 5: what the lamp did to the water, and whether the tenders tended it right.
- The sticks across the jar in months 1, 3 and 7, and the answering sticks I wrote for the tallies in months 4 and 6: she reads them and says what they ask. How a stick carries a request is yours, and so is whether any stick answered her stick of six notches at the year's turn and the Midwife's list of the dead.
- The god's coming to the Midwife's bed in month 2: the god's side of what passed, its answers to her four questions, and what it showed her. I wrote only what she asked and what she told the Warden-Monarch.
- How the god learned the Midwife's house. I wrote that the Warden-Monarch gave it to the water at the tide-door the day before and left a notched stick in the jar, as in other tellings. Whether it heard is yours.
- The Warden-Monarch's telling of the Harbormaster's answer to the water in month 9, and of the net-mender's answer in month 7: whether the god heard either is yours.
- The six dimmings of the star, months 1, 5 (two), 7 (two), 8: written as seen, with no claim of cause. The god told the Warden-Monarch it does not cause them. The people still tie them to the sea and the step in lore.

## What touches anyone else

- Trade:
  - The shepherds of the Bren Hills [r05] came twice, at the start of summer and at its end, with wool and mutton for salt fish. They asked nothing about the bell.
  - Three coasting boats from the Iss Levels [r07] came in month 11. Salt, rope and fish went out. Sound oak (enough for one keel and some planking) and ten loads of thin grain came in. A few pieces of old silver went out.
- No party went out and no court message was sent. No stranger came by land and no hull not known on this coast came in.
- Nothing carried. The oldest net-mender's answer about Low Garrow, written small on a slip of scraped hide, and the Midwife's list of the year's dead, are in the jar for the god. The Warden-Monarch's stick of six notches is in the jar.

## Asked of the god

- The Warden-Monarch, month 1: nothing new in words. She said the god's words back to it, whole, at the tide-door. Her five notches of last year, left in the jar, were answered this year in front of her (it does not cause the dimmings and does not know what does).
- The Eldest Midwife, month 2, at the bedside, in her words as she gave them to the Warden-Monarch: which people it had not kept, and whether it was Low Garrow; whether it counted the dead as kept or as lost; what it wanted of the town; and a request to see what she was speaking to, if she kept still. Whether each was answered in front of her is yours. Her offer, said to the god at the bed and then given to the Warden-Monarch to say in the jar: the clerks' list of the year's dead, each autumn, through the Warden, since she will not go to the step herself.
- The Harbormaster, month 4, to the god through the Warden-Monarch, in his words: "Whoever asks under the chain's line is asking whether the chain was up." If the keeping knows anything about his chain it can say it to him and not to her. And: if there is a hull under the line he will know before the keeping does, and if there is not, the keeping can keep its asking. Answered: no, not in front of him; he has not met it.
- The oldest net-mender, month 7, the answer the god asked for through the Warden-Monarch, in her words in the year file. Nothing asked of the god.
- No other mortal put a question to it.

## Names said aloud

- "Low Garrow": said by the Eldest Midwife to the god at the bedside in month 2, as a question, and by the oldest net-mender to the Warden-Monarch in month 7. Already in the record.
- "Skerr": no one said it aloud to the god this year. Already in the record.
- "Brennis": not said aloud this year.
- "the listener": the old woman's byname and the town's; "the keeping", the court's. Neither is a name for the people, the town or an office holder.
- No name was said aloud for the people, the town or any office holder. The eldest of the smokers, the oldest net-mender, the senior Captain and the boat's crew are unnamed and filed by what they were. All stay unnamed or filed by office.
