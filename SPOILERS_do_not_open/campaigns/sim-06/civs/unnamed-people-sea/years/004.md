# Year 4: the unnamed people of god-sea

Written by the civ agent from the god's decisions for the year, the Keeper's pass one and the Keeper's line on Year 3. Months are counted from the opening of autumn. The people have no name for themselves or their town in this file, and no office holder has a name. Where a thing turns on a power or on an uncertain outcome, the line is marked [KEEPER] and the matter is in `years/004-report.md`. One thing is written both ways: the boat in the swell in the eighth month, while the god was away, marked [BOAT CAME IN] and [BOAT LOST]. Where nothing is marked, both ways read alike. The Year 3 outcomes stand as the Keeper gave them: the long-liner came in with all three, and the Harbormaster's lead and grapnel took nothing that is a hull.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the jar, the tide-door, the two answers

The year opened as years do. The last catch was mackerel and herring on the racks, the smoking sheds running by day and night, the boats called in and caulked in turn. The headland cove's decked line-and-crab boat, on the keel the Master of Timber has said nothing about, was finished in the yard in the first weeks and went into the water. Her master took her out on the first fair morning and said she sat the water as the old boat had in her first year and not as she had in her last.

The hauling was the Harbormaster's own order for the fourth autumn, given at the turn of the month, ten days before the old custom. He added a rule of his own at the harbor-court, and said it was the quay's and nobody was to call it anything else. Three winters had given him three long swells and a boat caught in each, and he had counted that he could not tell a coming swell by the sky or the gulls, but he could hear the Skerries from the lamp-house stair when the swell was already on them. So: whenever the watch on the stair heard the Skerries breaking from the northwest, at dawn or at noon, no boat went past the mouth until the watch could not hear them. The east cove grumbled, and the Reeve of the Fields said that a rule that costs a few days of the catch and costs no crew was a rule the stores could carry. The Reeve and the Eldest Midwife counted the stores and agreed, without being asked, that a third of the households' own fish would go into the shared store again. Nobody in the town could remember a hungry lean in four years, and the grumbling was mostly for form. The last catch was cut by about ten days again, and by a day or two more for the Skerries.

On the first dusk of the dark half the Warden-Monarch lit the harbor lamp with her own hand and hung the sealed jar on the lowest step and struck the bell once. The jar held the count of the boats, the count of the stores and the count of last year's storms, which was five. The stick of six notches she had left at the year's turn for the dimmed nights was in it still, and the Eldest Midwife's list of the year's dead, as they had lain since the turn. Before dawn a stick lay across the lid. She read it as asking her to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk on the fourth night after. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.]

On the fourth night she went down alone with a shuttered lantern. The hooded figure stood below the lamp's reach. It said it had answers for the Harbormaster and for the Eldest Midwife, two questions that had stood a year and each put in good faith, and that a plain word was owed to each. It said them once, in front of her, one and then the other, and asked her to carry each by her own mouth, alone with the one it was for, on a day she judged to be right, in the autumn, and to carry nothing with them. She said each back to it, once, whole. It told her what to say if either asked her more: that it is the one who keeps the keeping, that it is not the town's, that it takes no rites, and that she does not know the rest. If either were hostile, she was to say nothing further and tell it through the jar. It asked for the clerks' list of the dead that lay in the jar. She took the seal off the jar by the slit of her lantern and put the slip in its hand, and it read it standing, a long time, and she did not see what it did with it after. It told her that it would be gone from the water by the town for some weeks in the spring, and that the lamp would stay in her keeping. She took its word, as she has taken it each year. She sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night, as she does, and in the morning put the seal back on the empty jar and wrote in the harbor-book that the list had been asked for and given.

On the first night after, and through the day after, the gulls left the point and the seals left the skerry by the harbor mouth, and the gleaners said the strand there was quiet in the way they did not like. The old woman at the east lamp told the girl who brings her bread that the listener had gone into the water to listen, and was cold for two days. It was not the cold of a knowing and nobody made much of it. [KEEPER: Water's Hearing, written only as what the town saw.]

In the fourth week the Warden-Monarch went to the Eldest Midwife. She chose a day when the Midwife had come in from a lying-in and sat in her kitchen with her sleeves rolled, and she sent the Midwife's girl to the well. She said it alone, by her own mouth, exactly as it was given to her:

"You asked which people I did not keep, and whether it was Low Garrow. I do not remember which. I remember that there was a people and that the water came up and I was not enough. I do not know that it was Low Garrow. I will not say it was and I will not say it was not. You asked whether I count the dead as kept or as lost. I count them as kept. I count the ones I did not keep by the same hand. I take your list each autumn and I read it. You asked what I want of the town. I want the boats home. I want no crew lost. I want nothing else of it, and I do not want to be asked for more than that. If you send me a house, I will come once, as before."

The Midwife heard it through and did not move, and then she was quiet longer than the Warden had seen her be. She said that a midwife counts the ones she lost by the same hand as the ones she kept, that no other count is honest, and that she had not looked to hear it from the water. She said that if it did not know it was Low Garrow then she did not know it either, and that she had said so at three bedsides and would stop. She said that wanting the boats home was a small want for a thing that old, and that she did not believe it the whole of it and would not ask it to be. She asked whether it had read the list. The Warden said it had, standing. The Midwife said that was more than the clerks did. She said she would send a house when she had one that fitted, as before, and that she would not send one for her own sake. The Warden did not press her.

### Month 2: the first storm, the Harbormaster at the post

In the second week the first storm came in on the shore, a night and a day. All boats were hauled and tarred, the chain was in order, the lamp was lit. Two boat-sheds on the east cove's slip went, and a stretch of racks. Nobody was hurt.

On the fourteenth night of the month, under a clear sky, the star dimmed. The blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and it was as before. The clerks put the mark in the harbor-book. The Warden-Monarch put the first notch on her stick. The old woman at the east lamp said it was the fifteenth she had counted.

In the third week, when the storm had gone and the yard's men had left the chain-post, the Warden-Monarch found the Harbormaster at the post alone, as she had the year before, and said it as it was given to her, by her own mouth:

"I asked about the two boats because I found their broken lower timbers inside the mouth, and I wanted to know if it was a storm, the chain, or something else. I do not know whether the chain was up. I heard no hull in your waters that was not yours. I do not take the book's word as wrong, and I do not take it as the whole. You took a lead and grapnel to the line and found no hull. I thank you for that. I ask nothing more of your chain."

He did not answer for as long as it takes a tide to move a hand's breadth on the step. Then he said that a boat's lower timbers is a hull to him, and that he had dragged both sides of the line from the post to the Skerries end through an ebb and again the second morning and had taken rock, weed and the old stones of the chain's footing, and that if the keeping had found timbers inside the mouth then they lay where his lead had not gone, or the mud had them, or they were other timbers. He asked where inside the mouth. The Warden said she did not know. He asked who it was that asked, and she gave him what she had been told to give: that it is the one who keeps the keeping, that it is not the town's, that it takes no rites, and that she did not know the rest. He said that was what it had said of itself to the Midwife, as he had it from the sitting.

He said nobody had thanked him for the lead. Not the court, not the line, not the families of the boats, who had not known he went. He said he did not need it from the water and did not say he did not like it. He said that "I ask nothing more of your chain" was the first thing out of that water that he would have put his own name to, and that the chain was the quay's and had been asked about long enough. He said he took a tide and not a word, and would not drag a harbor on a word, but that if it said where, he would put a lead there and she might tell it so. He did not say he took the word. He did not call it a liar. He said he had put a fourth line to his tally, and did not say what it was. She said she would carry the question, and she left it in the jar that dusk with a notched stick. The Harbormaster went to the post the next morning with a lantern and stood a while and went away. [KEEPER: whether a stick answered his question, and what it asked her to say to him.]

### Month 3: the second storm, the cranes, the pile

The second storm came in the second week and went in a night. The Master of Timber looked over the quay head, where the swell of the Skerries bears, and found the outer pile at the seaward corner leaning and worked at the foot, as it has been leaning since his master's day. He said so to nobody yet.

On the nineteenth night the star dimmed, in the same way. The Warden-Monarch put the second notch on her stick.

In the third week a stick lay across the jar's lid before dawn, and she read it as asking her to bring the Master of Timber to the quay on a night with no moon in the month after, and to have him name the piece himself, with the Harbormaster's assent. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.] She went to the yard and told him the keeping asked for one more piece before the hard weather and left the naming of it to him. He said he would name the outer pile at the quay head, which he would have named without the keeping if the Harbormaster had asked him, and that the yard would cut two, so nobody could say which of the two was anything but a pile.

Late in the month the cranes went over in flights for some days, toward the plain, as every year. The oldest fisher said he had stopped reading cranes. The fishers said it was an ordinary year.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the pile, the longest storm

In the first week the Master of Timber went to the Harbormaster at the harbor-court and said the outer pile at the quay head leaned and wanted replacing before the hard weather. The Harbormaster said he had been meaning to say so to the Master for two winters and had not liked to give him the pleasure, and gave his assent. The Warden-Monarch sealed it. The yard cut two piles and drove them side by side at the seaward corner, with the whole yard at the work, so that nobody could say which was which.

On the first night with no moon, in the second week, the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the quay head. The hooded figure came along the water under the quay and said nothing. It laid its right hand on one of the two new piles and held it there, and went down into the water. The Master put his hands to both at first light and left them. He told the Warden-Monarch he knew which, would say if asked and not before, and that it was the seventh piece in four years. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the pile; what it is, what he found.]

On the twenty-second night the star dimmed. The Warden-Monarch put the third notch on her stick.

In the third week the Warden-Monarch told the inner court what the keeping asked for the dark half: the boats hauled and tarred, the chain seen to, the stores counted and shared through the lean, the harbor lamp every dusk, no boat out in the worst weather for a catch. The Harbormaster said it was done and had been done since the turn of autumn and was the quay's. The Reeve said the count was done too. The Warden-Monarch said she had been asked to say it and had said it. She said Skerr in the sitting, as she has each year, and the Harbormaster did not remark on it. She left the tally of storms and boats in the jar that week, two storms and the boats in the harbor-book. [KEEPER: confirm an answering stick.]

In the fourth week the longest storm of the year came in, two days, none worse than the town knows. The boats were hauled. The tenders kept the harbor lamp through it with the Warden-Monarch's heir on the worst of the nights. The new pile and the post held, and the Harbormaster stood on the quay head through the second night with a lantern and did not say why.

### Month 5: the fourth storm, the Skerries heard

The fourth storm came in early in the month and went in a night and a day. After it the sea ran long and heavy for some days, and for the first time in four years nobody was out in it. The watch on the lamp-house stair heard the Skerries at the second dawn, and the Harbormaster kept every boat inside the mouth for six days. The east cove said it was a dry fishing. The Harbormaster said it was a dry fishing and a full harbor, and that he would take it. The old woman at the east lamp had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before. The keeping's lamp stayed in the lamp-house. No boat was caught in a swell this winter.

### Month 6: the fifth storm, the dim half ends

The fifth storm came early in the month, a night, and was the last of the winter. The clerks wrote the winter's count in the harbor-book: five storms between the second month and the sixth, the longest two days, none worse than the town knows. No boat was lost to a storm. Chest fevers took some of the old, as every winter, and a cold in the sixth month took a few more. There were births in the long nights. The Warden-Monarch left the stick in the jar with the notches to date, three, and the winter's tally. [KEEPER: confirm an answering stick.]

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Month 7: the lean, the word of the absence

Spring was lean, as it is. The Reeve opened the shared store in the first week and held to it. The Eldest Midwife kept the households' share even. Nobody starved. Some of the old, and a few of the very young, died of the weak chest that comes with a thin spring. The Harbormaster kept the order against sending boats out in the worst weather, and on a bad day said it was his own order, which no one had asked.

In the first week the Warden-Monarch told four people what the keeping had told her at the tide-door in the autumn: the Master of Timber, the Eldest Midwife, her heir, and the senior Captain. She said the keeping would be gone from the water by the town for some weeks in the spring, that it had not said where or why, and that the lamp would stay in her keeping. She told the Master and the Midwife so that nobody who had met it would put a question to the water and wait on a stick. She told the senior Captain because the Captains had been left out of it once for no reason but that nobody had asked, and she is exact. The Captain asked whether the lamp would be lit at the water's edge if a boat were caught. She said she did not know. He said nothing more of it, and did not say he would tell the levy. She told the two tenders the same, and that nobody lit it without her word.

In the second week she left the jar on the lowest step at dusk with the winter's tally, the stick of three notches, and a plain word that the store was open and held. She struck the bell once. No stick lay across the lid before dawn. She took the jar up and found it as she had left it. She left it again in the third week and the fourth, with the same word, and found it as she left it. She wrote each day it was not answered in the harbor-book, as she writes a boat not come in. The point was not quiet on any night of those weeks, the gulls were on the lowest step every evening, and the seals were on the skerry. The old woman at the east lamp said the listener had gone from the step and from the water, and was not cold. The fishers said it had gone up the shore, or down, or to sleep for the lean. Nobody knew. [KEEPER: the god away; what it found where it went.]

### Month 8: the levy, the star, the boat in the swell

The levy of about one hundred and fifty was drilled in the first week, as every spring, and the Captains drilled it at the chain. The truce held. A lamp-tender of the line laid the key in the harbor-book each time it was used.

On the ninth night of the month, under a clear sky, the star dimmed, in the same way, with the keeping gone from the water. The Warden-Monarch put the fourth notch on her stick. The old woman at the east lamp said it was the eighteenth. The fishers who saw it from the night grounds said it had dimmed with nobody on the step. The Warden-Monarch wrote the night in the harbor-book beside the unanswered jar and said nothing of the two.

On the eighteenth and nineteenth days the sea ran long from the northwest with no wind on the shore. The old fishers read it as the tail of a far gale. The Skerries were heard on the stair, and no boat went out. On the twentieth morning the watch could not hear them, and the Harbormaster let the boats go. The lean was in its eighth week and the mackerel were on the near shoal beyond the mouth. By the afternoon the swell was back, long and high from the northwest, faster than the old signs allow, with no wind in it and no storm in it.

A decked boat of the point did not come in. She was patched twice by the yard, a good boat, with four aboard: her master, a sound man of about forty-six; his sons of twenty and sixteen; and a hired hand of about thirty-five. They had stayed on the shoal for the bite, as the lean made them, and the swell had come up behind the fish. By dusk she was running for the harbor mouth between the Garrow Skerries [f31] and the quay, with the swell breaking on the Skerries on her beam.

The Harbormaster was on the quay within the hour. He had the chain let down, with the lamp-tender of the line at the capstan and the key written in the harbor-book, and put out the Captains' boat to stand by at the mouth, with the senior Captain. The Warden-Monarch's heir was in the lamp-tower with the harbor lamp.

The Warden-Monarch stood in the lamp-house with the keeping's lamp in her hands a long time while the light went. Then she told the old woman and the boy to take it to the water's edge below the lamp, by the harbor mouth, and to light it, as they had been shown. She said before the tenders and before the heir that she did it on her own word and not the keeping's, that the keeping was not there, that nothing would come to it, and that if anyone asked afterward it was she who had ordered it. She went down with them and stood a little way off and said nothing more. The old woman lit it with a coal from the lamp-house, and it burned. She and the boy filled it and trimmed it as they were shown, and filled it again before the first watch was out, and the boy ran the oil from the lamp-house and did not fall. The old woman did not leave the lamp. She said later that it burned as a lamp does and she tended it as one, and that she could not have said it did anything else. The senior Captain, at the mouth, looked along the strand and the water's edge for the figure and saw none, and said so to the Warden-Monarch in the morning. The old woman at the east lamp had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before. She said so. [KEEPER: what a lamp lit by the tenders without the god did to the water, if anything; whether they tended it right; whether the Warden's act was in the god's name.]

The outcome of the boat is the Keeper's to settle. It is written both ways.

[BOAT CAME IN] The boat came in through the mouth in the second hour of the night with all four aboard, with the swell still breaking on the Skerries outside it, and the four sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night with their hands round warm bowls. The Captains' boat took her line at the mouth. The lamp burned at the water's edge through the night and into the next day, the old woman and the boy taking it in turns. The point said the lamp brought them in, and said it with the keeping away, and the town did not agree what that meant. The Harbormaster said the crew and the chain did it and the Captains' boat, which is true as far as it goes, and said no more. He put a fifth line in his tally and did not say what it was.

[BOAT LOST] The boat broke at the mouth in the second hour of the night. The master and his elder son drowned. The younger son and the hired hand were hauled out by the Captains' boat and sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night. The lamp burned at the water's edge until the last of them was in or past help, and the old woman and the boy kept it till the Harbormaster told them to stop, and the Warden-Monarch had it taken back to the lamp-house still lit. The town set two more stones on the empty cairn. The Harbormaster said at the harbor-court, and not at the sitting, that the lamp had not saved them, and that it was not the keeping's to save them, since the keeping was not here, and that nobody was to say the keeping failed. He said the Warden had lit it on her own word and had said so, and let it be hers. The Warden-Monarch said nothing. The widow of the point did not leave bread on the lowest step, and said so. He put a fifth line in his tally and did not say what it was.

In the week after, the Warden-Monarch went down to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk and said aloud to the water what she had done and how it had gone, in the plain order of a clerk's entry, and then said that she did not know whether she had had the right, and asked whether she had. She left the jar with a notched stick and struck the bell once. No stick lay across the lid before dawn. She left it again and the next week and found it as she left it. [KEEPER: the god was away; the question stays pending.]

### Month 9: the fishing turns

The fishing turned at the end of the month and the lean ended. In the fourth week the gulls left the point on a night and the seals left the skerry, and the gleaners said the strand was quiet again. The old woman at the east lamp said the listener was back, or was somewhere else listening, and that she could not tell which. Nobody saw the figure. The Warden-Monarch went down to the step the next dusk with the jar and the harbor-book's count of the weeks it was not answered. [KEEPER: when it came back, and whether a stick came.]

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Month 10: the shepherds, the house, the strake

Summer was earned. The shepherds of the bare hills came down with wool and mutton at the start of the summer and took salt fish, and again at its end, as every year. They asked a fisher at the landing nothing about the bell.

In the first week the Eldest Midwife sent a house by the Warden-Monarch's own door: the second house above the east cove landing, a short drag from the water, quiet, with its door on the shingle. The woman in the bed was a widow of about seventy-six with a wasting in the chest, past knowing for four days, and the husband she had lost to the sea thirty years before has a stone on the empty cairn. Her daughters work the fish-houses and the Midwife sent them to their aunt's across the town that afternoon and was not argued with. The Midwife said she sent the house because it fitted what was asked, and not for her own sake, and that she would not say what she meant to put to the one that came. The Warden-Monarch took the house to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk the day before and gave it to the water aloud with the day, and left a notched stick in the jar. [KEEPER: how the god learned the house.]

The next dusk, at the lowest of the tide, the Midwife sat alone in the house with the woman and a coal in the hearth and the lamp turned low. A little after the lamps were lit along the quay the latch lifted without a knock. A hooded figure in a salt-wet cloak stood in the door, and the boards under it darkened where it dripped. It went to the foot of the bed at an old man's pace. It said what it had to say, low. The Midwife put it two things and kept what came of them: where it had been in the spring and what it found there; and whether it read her list by the same hand as the others. [KEEPER: the god's answers, if it answered; these are its own. The Midwife has given only the account below.] It took no bread and no bed and no name, and did not touch the woman, and the Midwife did not ask it to. At the turn of the tide it went as it had come. The woman died in the grey before dawn with the Midwife's hand in hers, and the Midwife laid her out herself before the daughters came back.

She told the Warden-Monarch in the lamp-house that afternoon that it came when it said, kept the door until she asked, asked nothing, took nothing, and was as honest as before, and that she would put no more of it in the sitting than she had. She said it had not sat as the dying sit. She did not say what she meant.

On the seventeenth night of the month the star dimmed. The Warden-Monarch put the fifth notch on her stick.

At the start of the summer the Warden-Monarch told the Master of Timber, as a stick across the jar's lid had asked her to, that the keeping asked for the keel or a strake of the boat that goes out latest and comes in last. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.] He did not choose the ferryman's boat again, nor the Captains' stand-by boat, nor the crab boat. He chose the east cove's open long-liner, the boat that stayed on the grounds in the swell of last winter and came in, whose garboard strake had started along the keel where the Skerries' swell had worked her and had been caulked twice since. The Master of Timber took it to the Harbormaster for the strake and asked the price and was told there was none. The Harbormaster did not like it. He took it for the cove's sake and not the lamp's, and said so, and said the east cove had asked him for nothing else in four years.

### Month 11: the strake, the coasting boats

Three coasting boats from the Iss Levels [r07] came in the eleventh month, as most summers. Salt, rope and fish went out. Sound oak, enough for one keel and some planking, and eleven loads of thin grain came in, and a few pieces of the court's old silver went out. No hull came in that was not known on this coast.

On the third night the star dimmed. The Warden-Monarch put the sixth notch on her stick.

In the second week, on a dark night in the yard, with the new strake laid on the blocks beside the hauled long-liner, the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the boat. The hooded figure came up from the water and laid its right hand on the strake and held it there, and went away down to the water without a word. The Master of Timber said that was the eighth piece in four years and that it was enough, and told no one but the Warden-Monarch. The yard fitted the strake and re-caulked the boat that month. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the strake.]

The figure was not seen at the town's edge this summer. On some evenings the ferryman said the water off his bow lay strangely quiet, and he did not like to say why. The lamp-tenders said the point went quiet again on three nights. The Midwife's girl said the strand below the east cove landing went quiet on two. [KEEPER: whom it watched.]

### Month 12: the tally

The fishing was fair. In the third week the ferryman went back out in fair weather for a late boat with a fouled sail and towed her in, as he has done all his life, and called it a small thing. On the twenty-sixth night the star dimmed, the seventh time in the year. The Warden-Monarch put the seventh notch on her stick. The old woman at the east lamp said the nights had been three, five, six and seven in four years, that it counts up, and that it does not count up by the same step each time.

The Warden-Monarch left the jar on the lowest step at the year's turn with the tally of the storms, five, and the boats in the harbor-book, and the stick of seven notches, as the keeping had asked, and the clerks' list of the year's dead in the Eldest Midwife's name, which the Midwife had given her for it. She asked no question in words. [KEEPER: whether a stick came.]

## At the year's turn

The people come to the opening of autumn with the racks and the loft in ordinary stock for the winter ahead, the court's chest a little lighter in silver and the same in bronze, and no new hull on the blocks, only the long-liner re-caulked and oak bought for the next keel. The Warden-Monarch's standing has held inside the court and is tested outside it by the boat in the eighth month. The Eldest Midwife has met the keeping twice and has heard its answers, and says it is honest and does not know it was Low Garrow. The Harbormaster has heard that it thanked him and asks nothing of his chain, and has a tally of his own with five lines and a question still standing in the jar. The Master of Timber has eight sound pieces in the quay and the yard and says nothing of them. The senior Captain has seen the lamp lit with nobody at the water's edge. The town has seen a spring with no quiet at the point and no figure on the step, a lamp lit at the water's edge by its own tenders, and seven nights of the star dimmed, one of them with the keeping away, and does not agree about any of it.
