# Year 2: the unnamed people of god-sea

Written by the civ agent from the god's decisions for the year and the Keeper's pass one. Months are counted from the opening of autumn. The people have no name for themselves or their town in this file, and no office holder has a name. Where a thing turns on a power or on an uncertain outcome, the line is marked [KEEPER] and the matter is in `years/002-report.md`. Where the year turns on the boat in the swell (month 5), it is written both ways, and the two branches are marked [BOAT CAME IN] and [BOAT LOST].

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the jar, the tide-door, the quiet again

The year opened as years do. The last catch was ordinary, mackerel and herring salted on the racks, the smoking sheds running by day and night, the boats called in and caulked in turn. The Eldest Midwife and the Reeve of the Fields counted the stores against the winter and found them enough if nothing went wrong, which is what they say every year.

On the first low-water day of the month the court stood at the empty cairn for the two boats lost the autumn before last, as it does. The Harbormaster stood at it and said nothing, as he has every year. The Warden-Monarch stood beside him through the whole of the sitting and said nothing either. The Reeve said afterward it was the first year anyone could remember the two of them standing side by side at it, and he did not know what to make of it.

On the first dusk of the dark half the Warden-Monarch lit the harbor lamp with her own hand, and then, as the custom of her house requires, hung the sealed jar on the lowest step and struck the bell once. She had done this twenty-two autumns with no word and one autumn with a word. This year the jar held the count of the boats in the harbor-book and in the yard, the count of the stores, and the count of the year's storms, which was five. Before dawn a second stick lay across the lid. She read it and said it asked her to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk on the third night after. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request; she reads it and tells no one the whole of it.]

On the third night she went down alone with a shuttered lantern. The hooded figure stood below the lamp's reach, as before. It told her three things, in few words. That it did not know the name Brennis and could not say it was the one who had spoken to Brennis. That it asked her to tell it what the eldest remember of the promise and who made it. That it thanked her for the bed in the lamp-house and would not take it, because a roof over it was a place to be found in, and it would keep to the water and the step. Then it went down into the water.

The Warden-Monarch sat in the lamp-house through the night. She had spent a year taking the keeping as the thing her line had been promised, and it had told her plainly that it was not claiming the promise. She is a keeper of books and does not like a sum that comes out other than she counted it. She did not tell the court. She had the bed in the lamp-house made up again that week, as it had stood all year, and the household girls were told to leave it as it was and not to ask.

On the first night after the tide-door, and through the day after, the gulls left the point and the seals left the skerry by the harbor mouth, as they had a year ago. The gleaners said the strand there was quiet in the way they did not like. The fishers said it was back. The old woman at the east lamp said, to the girl who brings her bread, that the listener on the lowest step had gone into the water to listen there, and was cold for two days after. It was not the cold of a knowing, so no one made much of it. [KEEPER: Water's Hearing, written only as what the town saw.]

At the low-water sitting in the third week the Warden-Monarch told the inner court what the keeping asked, which was what it had asked last year, and early: the boats hauled, caulked and tarred before the first blow, the chain seen to, the stores counted and shared evenly through the lean, the harbor lamp lit every dusk of the dark half, and no boat sent out in the worst weather for a catch. She did not say a word about Brennis. The Harbormaster said he had meant to haul at the turn of the month on his own count, after last year, and that he would give the order as the court's. The Reeve said the count wanted doing. The Eldest Midwife asked again for the lean to be shared evenly and for a third of the households' own fish to go into the shared store, and the Reeve agreed. The households grumbled less than last year, because nobody went hungry in last year's lean.

So the haul began in the last days of the month, about ten days before the town's old custom, and the last catch was cut again, a little more than last year.

When the boats were hauled, the Master of Timber looked over the point ferryman's boat from keel to stem, as a boatwright does every autumn. The keel, a full year in salt water, showed no worm, no check and no softening. He said so to the ferryman, and to nobody else, and the ferryman said it was a good keel and the yard had been kind. [KEEPER: confirm a clean keel at one year.]

In the fourth week the Warden-Monarch went to the yard and carried the god's two answers to the Master of Timber in her own words. To what he owed for it: nothing. To why it must be secret: that a quay known to be a god's work would be leaned on, and a god that is known would be asked for what it cannot give, and it would rather the town trusted its own timber and its own hands. She asked him to keep which piece was the keeping's, as he had. He heard her out. He said the first answer was more than he had expected and the second was more than he would have liked, and that the second was probably true. He said he would keep it. He had asked the two questions a year before and had given up looking for an answer.

### Month 2: the boards, the first storm

In the first week the Master of Timber went to the Harbormaster about the quay. The lowest three step-boards had checked along their edges in the weather of the year before, and he said they wanted renewing before the first blow. The quay is the Harbormaster's, and a quay repair takes his assent and the Warden's seal, as the custom is, and he gave the one and she gave the other. The Harbormaster did not ask why the Master of Timber was in a hurry. He supposed it was the usual argument for timber.

The yard cut three boards of sound oak from the planks in store and fitted all three by day, with the yard working at it, so that no one could say which of the three was anything other than a board. On a night with no moon the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber down to the quay. The hooded figure came up out of the water to the lowest step and said nothing to either of them. It laid its right hand on one of the three boards, and held it there, and then went down into the water. The Master of Timber put his hands to all three at first light and left them. He told the Warden-Monarch that he knew which, and that he would say if asked and not before. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the quay board: what it is, what he found.]

In the third week the first storm of the year came in on the shore, a night and a day. All the boats were hauled and tarred. The lamp was lit. The storm did the harm storms do: the roof of a drying shed, a stretch of racks, a gutter of the smokehouse. Nobody was hurt. The Harbormaster said again that he would have hauled the boats himself within the week, and this year the town did not count it as news.

On the twelfth night of the month the star dimmed. The blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and it was as before. The clerks put the mark in the harbor-book. The old woman at the east lamp said it was the first of a counting.

### Month 3: the telling, the long storm, the cranes

Through the first three months the Warden-Monarch did as the keeping asked and gathered what the eldest remember of the promise. She did not ask in the sitting. She went herself, one at a time: to the oldest of the net-menders, to the eldest of the smokers, to the old woman at the east lamp, and to the Eldest Midwife. She did not say why she asked. What she was told, set together, was this.

- That the promise is older than the walls: whoever keeps the lamp is kept count of.
- The eldest of them said it was made to a lamp-warden named Brennis, by something that came up the quay steps and went down them again, and that the bell was hung so that it could be called.
- Most of the town says Brennis made the promise herself, to the town, and that nothing came up any steps.
- The oldest net-mender added the telling of Low Garrow, the town the sea took in one night for its pride, which the town says was founded by the ones the sea spared. She added that her mother said the promise was older than Low Garrow, and her mother's mother said it was younger.
- The old woman at the east lamp said only that her grandmother had said the thing on the step "did not ask for a name, and Brennis did not give one," which is the first anyone had heard of that.

In the second week the longest storm of the year came in, three days, the worst the town had seen that year and none worse than it knows. All the boats were hauled. The lamp-tenders kept the harbor lamp through it, with the Warden-Monarch's heir tending it on the nights of the worst blow, as she does. Two roofs were lost. One old man died of a chill after he went out to a shed, and the town called it the storm's. The quay held.

On the lowest tide at dusk of the third week of the month the Warden-Monarch went down to the tide-door alone. She said to the water what she had been told, in order, in her plain way, leaving out nothing and adding nothing, and said whose words they were. She left a stick in the jar with one notch for "told," and struck the bell once. At dawn the jar lay on the step as she had left it. What it bore she did not say. [KEEPER: whether the god heard it, and whether any stick came.]

Late in the month the cranes went over in long flights, toward the plain, as they do every year. This year the fishers did not say a hard winter was coming. The oldest of them said the cranes had told them one thing last year and the sea another, and that he trusted the sea.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the two tenders

Winter was mending, boiling salt, spinning and waiting. In the fourth month the Warden-Monarch read a stick that had come across the jar's lid and said it asked her to choose two of the lamp-tenders and show them how to fill and trim a lamp and how it is lit. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.] She chose the old woman at the east lamp and the boy who tends the lamp at the west end of the quay. They were not told of the keeping. She told them it was the keeping's lamp, that it was not to be lent, snuffed or carried off, and that it would stay in her keeping when it was not at the water. She told them nothing else, because she had been told to say nothing else. The old woman asked nothing. The boy asked whose it was, and the Warden-Monarch said the keeping's, and the boy said nothing more, and was seen afterward to look at the water at dusk.

In the same month the Warden-Monarch told her heir what she had told no one else, because the heir tends the lamp on storm nights and would sooner or later have seen it: that the keeping is real and has answered her, and that it does not claim the old promise. She did not tell her what she had been shown.

She left the jar with the tally of storms and boats, which stood at two storms, and a stick lay across the lid before dawn, as it had last year. [KEEPER: confirm an answering stick.]

### Month 5: the third storm, the boat in the swell

The third storm of the year came early in the month and went in a night and a day. After it the sea ran long and heavy for some days, as it does, and the fishers went out in the fair hours and came in before dusk.

On the evening of the ninth day after the storm a boat did not come in. She was a decked line-and-crab boat of the headland cove, patched twice by the yard in the last years, with four aboard: her master, a sound hand of about forty, his brother, his brother's son of fifteen, and a hired hand. They had stayed on a good shoal at the outer grounds. The gulls were still working it, the cloud gave them no sign, and the swell came up faster than the old signs allow. By dusk it was running long and high from the northwest, with no wind in it and no storm in it, and the boat was running for the harbor mouth between the Garrow Skerries [f31] and the quay with the swell breaking on the Skerries on her beam. The Harbormaster was on the quay himself within the hour. He had the chain let down. He put out the two Captains' boat to stand by at the mouth. The Warden-Monarch's heir was in the lamp-tower with the lamp.

The Warden-Monarch had the keeping's lamp brought down from the lamp-house to the water's edge below the lamp, by the harbor mouth, and the old woman and the boy went with it. The lamp-tenders at the tide-door said afterward that a hooded figure stood at the water's edge there, and that it took the lamp in its hand and lit it, and that the lamp stood in the dark as bright as any of the town's. The old woman and the boy kept it. They filled it and trimmed it as they were shown, and filled it again before the first watch was out, and the boy ran the oil from the lamp-house and fell on the quay stones and bruised his knee and did not stop. The old woman did not leave the lamp. The Warden-Monarch stood a little way off. She said nothing. The hooded figure stood at the water's edge and did not move. [KEEPER: Calm Under the Lamp; what the lamp did to the water; whether the tenders tended it right.]

The old woman at the east lamp had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before. She said so afterward, and said it was not a thing she could use. [KEEPER: her knowing comes a day ahead, and was not used.]

The outcome of the boat is the Keeper's to settle. It is written both ways.

[BOAT CAME IN] The boat came in through the mouth in the second hour of the night with all four aboard, with the swell still breaking on the Skerries outside it, and the crew sat in the lamp-house through the rest of the night with their hands round warm bowls. The Captains' boat took her line at the mouth. The lamp burned at the water's edge through the night and into the next day, and the old woman and the boy took it in turns, and the figure was seen no more after the boat was in. The town said the lamp brought them in, for the second year running, and that the keeping is a thing that looks to the boats. The Harbormaster said the crew did it themselves, which is true as far as it goes, and said the mouth lay flat in a way a swell from that quarter does not allow, and said no more. He put the thing in the tally he keeps in his own head, and it is the second entry. The old woman at the east lamp said the lowest step had a listener on it and the water had a keeper, and she was cold for no more than a night.

[BOAT LOST] The boat broke at the mouth in the second hour of the night. The master and his brother drowned. The brother's son and the hired hand were hauled out by the Captains' boat and sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night. The lamp burned at the water's edge until the last of them was in or past help, and the old woman and the boy kept it till the Harbormaster told them to stop, and the Warden-Monarch had it taken back to the lamp-house still lit. The town set two more stones on the empty cairn, and it said the lamp had lit the mouth and had not saved them. The Harbormaster said in the harbor-court, not in the sitting, that the Warden's lamp had not saved them. The Warden-Monarch said nothing. The line was not blamed in the open and was held to it in the quiet. The old woman at the east lamp said she had had no knowing, and the town was not sure whether that made it better or worse.

### Month 6: the fourth storm, the dimming

The fourth storm of the year came in early in the month, a night and a day, and was the last of the winter. The clerks wrote the winter's count in the harbor-book: four, one fewer than last year, the longest of three days. No boat was lost to a storm. Chest fevers took some of the old, as every winter, and a cold in the sixth month took a few more. There were births in the long nights.

On the twenty-second night of the month, under a clear sky, the star dimmed a second time in the year, in the same way. It was the fifth time in two years that it had been marked. The old woman at the east lamp said, to the boy, that it was a counting, and that she did not know what it counted. The boy said the keeping would know. [KEEPER: the star, written as seen.]

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Month 7: the lean

Spring was lean, as it is. The shared store was opened in the seventh month and the Reeve held to it. The Eldest Midwife kept the households' share even. Nobody starved. Some of the old, and a few of the very young, died of the weak chest that comes with a thin spring. The Harbormaster kept the order against sending boats out in the worst weather, and on one bad day in the month he said it was his own order and not the keeping's, which no one had asked.

In the same month the Eldest Midwife went down to the lowest step alone at dusk and spoke to the water. A midwife's girl who had followed her to carry her bag saw it and said what she heard. She said: "I deliver what comes and bury what goes. You are the one that keeps a count, and I would like to know what you count for. If you want to be answered, come to a bedside. I will not come to the step again." She left no bread. [KEEPER: whether it heard.] [BOAT LOST] The two widows of the headland cove were hers to tend that month, and she said it again to the water more sharply on a second night and did not go down a third time. [BOAT CAME IN] She said nothing more to the step.

### Months 7 to 9: the water, not the strand

In the spring of last year a hooded old man was seen three times walking the strand at the lowest tides. This year no one saw him on the strand. On a good many nights through the lean, more nights than in the first autumn, the gulls left the point and the seals left the skerry, and the gleaners said the strand there was quiet, and the fishers said the listener was in the water and not on the step. The old said the tally-voices had gone into the sea. Nobody found any new mark on the rocks. The old ones from last spring were still there at the tideline, and the gleaners still would not touch them. [KEEPER: the god in the water, and what it found.]

### Month 8: the levy

The levy of about one hundred and fifty was drilled in the eighth month, as every spring, and the Captains drilled it at the chain. The truce over the chain held. The Harbormaster answered for its raising and lowering, and a lamp-tender of the Warden-Monarch's household laid the key in the harbor-book each time. It was used eleven times in the year, and each use is written. Each of them said again in the hearing of his own side that it was a truce and not a peace.

### Month 9: the dimming, the fishing turns

On the sixteenth night of the month the star dimmed for the third time in the year, and the sixth in two years. The fishers on the night grounds saw it from their boats. One crew came in early. The fishing turned at the end of the month, and the lean ended.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Month 10: the shepherds, the yard

Summer was earned. The shepherds of the bare hills came down with wool and mutton at the start of the summer and took salt fish, as every year. They asked a fisher at the landing whether the sea town still rang for the sea. The fisher said it was a custom and no more, and the Harbormaster, who heard of it, said the next time a man was asked he should say the same.

At the start of the summer the Warden-Monarch told the Master of Timber that the keeping asked for the keel of the boat that goes out latest and comes in last. He did not choose the point ferryman's boat again. He went to the Harbormaster and said the keel of the Captains' stand-by boat, which goes out last of any and comes in last of all, had checked in the winter and the yard had a keel timber in store. The Harbormaster asked the price and was told there was none. He did not like it. He took it, for the boat's sake and not the Warden's lamp, and said so to the Captains.

### Month 11: the keel, the coasting boats, the ferryman

Three coasting boats from the Iss Levels came in the eleventh month, as most summers. Salt, rope and fish went out. Sound oak planks, enough for one keel and some hull planking, and nine loads of thin grain came in, and a few pieces of the court's old silver went out.

In the second week, on a dark night in the yard, with the keel timber laid, the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the boat. The hooded figure came up from the water and laid its right hand on the keel and held it there, and went away down to the water without a word. The Master of Timber said that was the fourth piece in two years and that it was enough, and he told no one but the Warden-Monarch. The yard built the hull from it that summer. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the keel.]

On some evenings of the month a hooded figure was seen at the town's edge by the lamp-tenders, and twice at dusk by the point ferryman from his boat on the water. It did not speak and was not spoken to. The ferryman told his wife and no one else. In the same month he went back out in fair weather for a late boat with a fouled sail and towed her in, as he has done all his life. He said it was a small thing. [KEEPER: whom it watched.]

On the third night of the last week of the month the star dimmed for the fourth time in the year, the seventh in two years.

### Month 12: the last dimming, the tally

The fishing was fair. On the ninth night of the month the star dimmed for the fifth time in the year, the eighth in two years. The clerks wrote the five nights together in the harbor-book, and the town counted them, and said that three the first year and five the second was a rise, and nobody said what it was a rise toward. The Harbormaster said a star is a star. The old woman at the east lamp said it was a counting and she had stopped asking what of.

At the year's turn the Warden-Monarch left the jar on the lowest step with the tally of the year's storms, four, and of the boats in the harbor-book, and five notches in a row for the nights the star dimmed. She did not ask a question in words. [KEEPER: whether a stick came.]

## At the year's turn

The people come to the opening of autumn with the racks and the loft in ordinary stock for the winter ahead, the court's chest a little lighter in silver and the same in bronze, and the hull with the new keel nearly done in the yard. The Warden-Monarch's standing has held. The Harbormaster has done what was asked, still does not take the word, and has begun to keep a tally of his own that he tells no one. The Master of Timber has four sound pieces in the quay and the yard and will say nothing of them. The Eldest Midwife still waits. Two tenders and an heir now know there is a keeping, and the town has seen a second year of the figure at the water's edge. [BOAT CAME IN] The town leaves a heel of bread on the lowest step with a word said over it, and some have begun to leave a pinch of salt. [BOAT LOST] The town leaves the heel of bread as it always did, and fewer say the word.
