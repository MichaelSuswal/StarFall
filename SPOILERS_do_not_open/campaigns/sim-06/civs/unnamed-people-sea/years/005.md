# Year 5: the unnamed people of god-sea

Written by the civ agent from the god's decisions for the year, the Keeper's pass one and the Keeper's line on Year 4. Months are counted from the opening of autumn. The people have no name for themselves or their town in this file, and no office holder has a name. Where a thing turns on a power or on an uncertain outcome, the line is marked [KEEPER] and the matter is in `years/005-report.md`. One thing is written both ways: the boat in the swell in the eleventh month, marked [BOAT CAME IN] and [BOAT LOST]. Inside it, whether the god came to the bell is marked [GOD CAME] and [GOD NOT THERE]. Where nothing is marked, both ways read alike. The Year 4 outcomes stand as the Keeper gave them: the decked boat of the point broke at the harbor mouth on the twentieth day of the eighth month, her master and his elder son drowned, the younger son and the hired hand were hauled out by the Captains' boat, and the lamp lit by the tenders without the keeping's hand burned as a lamp does and calmed nothing.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the cairn, the tide-door, the two words, the stones

The year opened as years do. The last catch was mackerel and herring on the racks, the smoking sheds running by day and night, the boats called in and caulked in turn. The point kept its shed shut. The decked boat's berth at the point's landing stood empty, and nobody had moved the blocks.

On the first low-water day the court stood at the empty cairn, as it does, and the cairn had two stones more than it had at the opening of the last year. The Harbormaster stood at it and said nothing. The Warden-Monarch stood beside him, as she has stood beside him since the second year. The widow of the point's master stood a little off with her younger son, now seventeen, and the hired hand, and did not look toward the lamp-house side. The Reeve said afterward it was the first year he had seen the point stand apart from the court at the cairn, and he did not say what he made of it.

The hauling was the Harbormaster's own order for the fifth autumn, given at the turn of the month, ten days before the old custom, with the Skerries rule kept beside it: whenever the watch on the lamp-house stair hears the Skerries breaking from the northwest, no boat goes past the mouth until the watch cannot hear them. The Reeve of the Fields and the Eldest Midwife counted the stores and agreed, without being asked, that a third of the households' own fish would go into the shared store again. The households grumbled less than they had. Nobody could remember a hungry lean in five years. The last catch was cut by about ten days, and by a day or two more for the Skerries.

On the first dusk of the dark half the Warden-Monarch lit the harbor lamp with her own hand and hung the sealed jar on the lowest step and struck the bell once. The jar held the count of the boats in the harbor-book, with the point's decked boat struck out in the clerks' hand, the count of the stores, the count of last year's storms, which was five, the stick of seven notches for the dimmed nights, and the Eldest Midwife's list of the year's dead, as they had lain since the turn. Before dawn a stick lay across the lid. She read it as asking her to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk on the fourth night after. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.]

On the fourth night she went down alone with a shuttered lantern. The hooded figure stood below the lamp's reach. Before she had said anything it spoke, low, in the order of a clerk's entry, and she said afterward she knew it for the answer to the question she had put into the silence four months before:

"You had the right. It was your word and your hand, and you said so aloud, which was honest. The lamp is a lamp in any hand but mine. It does not still water for you. Say that to no one outside the court. The boat broke because I was far and the swell was long. That is mine to carry and not yours. Do not carry it."

She kept still for as long as it takes a tide to move a hand's breadth on the step. She said afterward, to her heir and nobody else, that she had asked for four months and had made up her mind she would not be answered, and had not been ready for a plain yes. She said to the figure that she would try. It said nothing to that. It asked her to carry its words to the Harbormaster and to the Eldest Midwife, one and then the other, and said them once, in front of her, and asked her to carry each by her own mouth, alone with the one it was for, on a day she judged to be right, in the autumn, and to carry nothing with them. She said each back to it, once, whole. It told her what to say if either asked her more: that it is the one who keeps the keeping, that it is not the town's, that it takes no rites, and that she does not know the rest. If either were hostile she was to say nothing further and tell it through the jar. It asked for the clerks' list of the dead. She took the seal off the jar by the slit of her lantern and put the slip in its hand, and the stick of seven notches with it, and it read the list standing, a long time, and she did not see what it did with either after. It told her that this year it would not go far from the bell. It asked her to strike the bell once for a word and twice for a boat in trouble, and said it would come at once, by water, from wherever it lay within a night's swim of the quay. It thanked her for the two tenders and for the lamp lit at the water's edge. She took its word, as she has each year. She sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night. In the morning she put the seal back on the emptied jar and wrote in the harbor-book that the list had been asked for and given and that a question put in the eighth month of the year before had been answered, and did not write what the answer was.

On the first night after, and through the day after, the gulls left the point and the seals left the skerry by the harbor mouth, and the gleaners said the strand there was quiet in the way they did not like. The old woman at the east lamp told the girl who brings her bread that the listener had gone into the water to listen, and was cold for two days. It was not the cold of a knowing and nobody made much of it. [KEEPER: Water's Hearing, written only as what the town saw.]

In the second week the Warden-Monarch went to the Eldest Midwife. She chose a day when the Midwife had come in from a lying-in and sat in her kitchen with her sleeves rolled, and she sent the Midwife's girl to the well. She said it alone, by her own mouth, as it was given to her:

"You asked two things at the widow's bed. I heard both. I have no whole answer to either, and I will not give a half one. I count the widow among the kept. I took nothing from the house. I take your count of the year's dead as the only honest count and I read the list. I do not say Low Garrow and I do not say not. If you send me a house, I will come once, as before."

The Midwife heard it through and did not move. She asked who it was that asked, though she knew, and the Warden gave her what she had been given to give. The Midwife said a half answer called a half was the only kind a midwife trusted, and that it was the third time the water had told her what it could not tell, which was three more times than the clerks had. She said that if it was somewhere in the spring that it would not say, it had told her so, and that was more than a fisher's wife told her about a husband. She said the widow's daughters had asked her three times since the burial whether it had been in the house, and that she would tell them yes, and that it counts their mother kept, and no more. She said she would send no house this year. She said she sent a house when she had a bed that fitted and a question that wanted a bed, and she had neither, and she would not make a death into an errand. She said the list was longer by two the sea took, and that she had put those two at the head of it, because she would not have it skip them. The Warden did not press her.

In the third week, at the low-water sitting, the Warden-Monarch told the inner court what she had been told of the lamp. She is a keeper of books and does not shorten an entry. She said it as it was given, all of it but the last four words, which she kept. The Harbormaster said nothing for a time. Then he said that he had said in the harbor-court that the lamp was the Warden's and the keeping was not there to fail, and been called hard for it, and that it was the first time the water had said a thing he had already said. He said "I was far" was said as a man says a thing he is ashamed of, and he knew the sound, and he would not say the keeping had failed and would not say it had not. He said then the lamp was a lamp, and he would have it lit where a boat could see it. The Reeve said the stores did not care whose lamp it was. The Master of Timber said that if it was far, then it could be near, and that he would like to know how it meant to be. The Eldest Midwife said it was honest, as it had been at the bed, and that it had not said it would have saved them, only that it was far, and the court should not say more than it was told. The Warden-Monarch said she had been told she had the right, and that the court might take that as it liked.

She told the senior Captain the same, and the bell, in the lamp-house, the next day, and said the second Captain was his to tell or not. He said he would tell him that the bell had two strokes and not what they were for. She told her heir, and the two tenders. The old woman said it was what she had said, that it burned as a lamp does. The boy asked whether the bell would be answered. The Warden-Monarch said the keeping said it would come at once. He asked how fast. She said she did not know. He said nothing more, and was seen after to look at the water at dusk.

On the night of the lowest tide of the autumn, in the third week, the watch on the quay head saw nothing and heard nothing, and the mouth lay as it lies. Before dawn a stick lay across the jar's lid. She read it as telling her that the stones were laid, three, in a row, along the chain's line, and that the Harbormaster was to be given where. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request. The laying of three stones by hand is ordinary craft by the god's own word.]

In the fourth week, on the day after the court's last sitting of the month, she found the Harbormaster at the chain's post alone, as he is every autumn before the haul, with the chain run out along the quay for the yard's men to look over link by link. She said it by her own mouth, as it was given to her:

"You asked where inside the mouth. Along the line the raised chain makes there lie the broken lower timbers of two boats built as the town builds. I do not say they are the two you lost. I do not say what broke them. At the lowest tide of the autumn I will lay three pale stones in a row on the bed beside them. A lead dragged along the chain line will ring on the stones. After that it is yours to say. You said nobody else had thanked you for the tide. I thank you. I ask nothing more of your chain."

He heard it through with his hand on a link. He did not answer for as long as it takes a tide to move a hand's breadth on the step. Then he said he had said the lead, not the tide, and that he would let that stand, since the rest was plain. He said it had said where, and that a lead on a line could be put there in one morning, and that he would put it at the next lowest springs and not before, because a calm morning was wanted and a man did not drag a harbor on the first of the ebb. He asked her who else knew. She said it had told no one but her. He asked what "after that it is yours to say" meant. She said she did not know. He said he knew what it meant, and did not thank it for the knowing. Then he said the Captains' boat would carry the Master of Timber, because the yard would know its own, and nobody else, and that he asked her to say nothing to the line's household until he had something in his hand. She said she would say nothing. She left the stick in the jar that dusk with the Midwife's answer and his, as far as they went, and the hauling went on in the last days of the month.

### Month 2: the lead rings, the cairn, the first storm

At the lowest springs, on the fourth morning of the month, on a calm sea with nobody on the quay but the Harbormaster's own men, the Captains' boat went out to the chain's line with both Captains, the Harbormaster in the bow with his hand on the line, and the Master of Timber in the stern. The Harbormaster had told the two Captains why in four words, "I am told where." The second Captain, who had not been told of the keeping, asked nothing and looked at the senior, and the senior looked at the chain.

They put the lead along the line from the post toward the Skerries end, through the ebb. On the second pass it rang on stone in three places, a short stride apart, clean and hard, unlike the dull ring of the chain's old footing, and the Harbormaster held it there a long time and said nothing. They put the grapnel to the bed beside the three. On the third cast it took timber and the crew lifted a broken length of oak strake with its iron rivets still in it. On the fifth it took a rib. The Master of Timber took the strake in both hands and turned it a long time. He said he had laid that strake, nineteen years ago, in a decked boat of the point. The rib he said was the yard's work of his own master's day, and the fastenings were not his. The Harbormaster said that was enough, and had the crew stop. They lifted no more. He said the rest lay where it was and the sea could keep it. The lead and grapnel had found, within a few strides of one another and where the chain crosses when it is raised, the broken lower timbers of two boats built as the town builds.

The Harbormaster went to the Warden-Monarch in the lamp-house that night. He said it was so. He said the chain had been down at dusk by his order and written, and the timbers lay where it crosses when it is up. He said he did not raise it and could not say who did, and that a chain does not raise itself, and that the lead could not say whether it had been up and could not say it had been down. He asked whose hand held the key. She said the line's roll said what it had always said, that the key was laid on the quay's man at dusk, and his book said it never was, and each said the other's, as for five winters, and nobody who could be asked could be asked. He said then he would say only what he had in his hand. He asked her to tell it that the lead rang. She said she would. She asked him what he wanted done with the pieces. He said they would go to the cairn, and the nine families would hear it from him before they heard it from the quay. He did not say he took the word. He said it had told him where, and the where was so, and that if it had meant him harm it could have said more.

On the next low-water day, at the cairn, before the nine families and the court, he laid the strake and the rib on the cairn's flat stone. He said two boats lay under the chain's line where it crosses, that the lead had rung where he was told to look, that the Master of Timber knew one for his own work, and that he did not know and would not say how they came to lie there. He said the harbor-book said down. He said the line's roll said otherwise of whose hand held the key, and he would have the key written by two hands from this day at every raising, the quay's man and the line's tender, both. The Warden-Monarch said beside him that the line's household agreed to it, and that she would read the harbor-book's entry of that night aloud if the families wished. The families wished. She read it standing, in the clerk's order, and it said what he had said it said. A mother of one of the nine said her son's boat had lain a stride off the line for five winters and nobody had looked. Nobody answered her. The Harbormaster asked her whether the families would go out to the line once, at slack water, in the Captains' boat, and let a stone fall for each of the nine, and she said they would. They went on the third morning after, nine families, a handful of shingle each, and the Harbormaster stood in the stern and said nothing.

The town heard it by evening, and the quay and the line each said the other had known. The line's old tenders said the quay's man had let the chain up by mistake and the quay had hidden it. The quay's men said the line's tender had left the key in the hook, and a man of the quay said it in the harbor-court. The Harbormaster said he would break the head of any man of the quay who said it again, and the Warden-Monarch said the same of her household, and the truce held, as a truce does, and neither of them called it a peace. The town said the keeping had found the nine for the Harbormaster. The Harbormaster put a sixth line in his tally, and took the old question out of the jar. [KEEPER: the lead rings on the stones and takes timber beside them, as your pass one has it. The cause of the loss is not given; none is claimed.]

On the ninth night of the month, under a clear sky, the star dimmed. The blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and it was as before. The clerks put the mark in the harbor-book. The Warden-Monarch put the first notch on her stick. The old woman at the east lamp said it was the nineteenth she had counted.

In the second week the first storm of the year came in on the shore, a night. All boats were hauled and tarred, the chain was in order, the lamp was lit. A shed roof on the east cove's slip went and a stretch of racks. Nobody was hurt.

On the twenty-fourth night the star dimmed a second time, in the same way. The Warden-Monarch put the second notch on her stick. The fishers said it was two in a month and the sea was breathing again, and the old woman said she had stopped adding.

In the third week, at the lowest tide at dusk, the Warden-Monarch went down to the tide-door alone and said to the water what the two had answered, in order, in the clerk's plain way: the Midwife's words, that a half answer called a half was the only kind she trusted, that she sent no house this year, that the widow's daughters were told yes; and the Harbormaster's, that he had said the lead and not the tide, that the lead rang, that the where was so, that the cause was his to say and he did not know it, that the pieces lay on the cairn and the families had let their stones fall, and that he would not lift the three stones and the keeping might say if it wished them lifted. She left a notched stick in the jar and struck the bell once. [KEEPER: whether it heard; whether a stick came.]

### Month 3: the second storm, the cranes, the question of the keel

The second storm came in the second week and went in a night. The yard's men looked the chain over again and found it sound.

In the third week a stick lay across the jar's lid before dawn. The Warden-Monarch read it as asking her to ask the Master of Timber whether the point meant to build a boat to replace the one it lost, and if it did, whether he and the point would take a keel of the yard's oak for her, joined by the keeping's hand in the dark, with the Harbormaster's assent and her seal, and no price asked. [KEEPER: how a stick carries a request.] She went to the yard and said it to him plainly, and said the keeping had said it asked for nothing. He said it had said that before, and that he did not need telling twice. He said the point was not hers to ask or the keeping's, and that he would ask it himself.

Late in the month the cranes went over in flights for some days, toward the plain, as every year. The oldest fisher said he had stopped reading cranes. The fishers said it was an ordinary year.

In the fourth week the Master of Timber went by himself to the point, on a day the boats were in, and sat a long time in the widow's kitchen. The widow's brother-in-law spoke for the household and said the point had a younger son and a hired hand and no boat, and the shed was shut. The point ferryman spoke for the boats. He said the yard's keel on his own boat had stood five winters in salt with no worm and no check, that the yard had been kind to him twice and that he was not too proud to say so, and that a boat that is not built is a household that is not fed. The widow said she would not have a hand put to it that was the lamp's. The Master said the oak was the yard's, and the keel was the yard's, and the price was none, and that was so. She asked whether the keeping had a hand in it. He did not lie. He said that if she asked him straight whether any hand but the yard's would lie on the keel, he would answer her straight, and that she had not yet asked him that, and that he would not have her ask it for his sake. She said nothing for a time. Then she said her younger son would build her, with the yard's men, and that she would not set her own hand to the work and would not forbid any other. The Master told the Warden-Monarch he had not told her the keeping's hand would be on it, and that he had told her he would if asked, and that he had meant it.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

### Month 4: the keel, the winter words, the longest storm

In the first week the Master of Timber went to the Harbormaster at the harbor-court and said the point wanted a keel and the yard had oak in store to give it. The Harbormaster said the point had asked him for nothing in five years and he had given it nothing, and asked the price, and was told there was none. He did not like it. He took it for the point's sake and not the lamp's, and said so, and asked whether the yard meant the same to the east cove next. The Warden-Monarch sealed it. The yard cut two keel timbers of one length and laid both on the blocks side by side, with the whole yard at the work, so that nobody could say which of the two was to be the boat's.

On the first night with no moon, in the second week, the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the yard. The hooded figure came up the slip from the water and said nothing. It laid its right hand on one of the two keel timbers, at the place where the keel's two lengths are scarfed, and held it there, and went away down the slip into the water. The Master put his hands to both at first light and left them. He told the Warden-Monarch he knew which, would say if asked and not before, and that it was the ninth piece in five years, and said nothing more of it. The yard set one of the two on the blocks for the point's boat and the other in the loft for the next. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the keel; what it is, what he found.]

In the third week a stick lay across the jar's lid before dawn. The Warden-Monarch read it as asking her to tell the court what it had asked in the last four winters, and one thing more, which she was to give as the court's own and not as the keeping's. She told the inner court at the sitting: the boats hauled and tarred, the chain seen to and logged in the book, the stores counted and shared through the lean, the harbor lamp lit every dusk, no boat out in the worst weather for a catch, and the Skerries rule kept. The Harbormaster said it was done and had been done since the turn of autumn and was the quay's. The Reeve said the count was done too. She told them the lamp might be lit at the water's edge in any swell, as a light to steer by, and said aloud by whoever lit it that it was a light, and she said she would so say it herself. The Harbormaster said he would have said so on the quay before the keeping said anything.

The one thing more she did not say as the keeping's. She went to the Harbormaster alone after the sitting and said she thought the court should say, as its own, that when a long swell ran with no storm in it, the Captains' boat and her crew stood at the mouth with a line, and no boat of the point went out to meet it. He asked whose it was. She said it was the court's, to be said by the court. He looked at her and said it was his Skerries rule with a second half, and the second half was a hard one, and he would say it as the quay's. At the next sitting of the harbor-court he said it. The east cove asked whether it meant them, and he said it named the point, because the point was the landing that went out, and because he had buried enough of it. The point heard it named. The widow said they named the point because the point had lost one. The ferryman said in the street that he had towed in a late boat all his life in fair weather, that a long swell was not fair, and that a rule that kept a man ashore while a boat ran for the mouth was a rule he would keep when he had buried his own, and he did not say he would not keep it.

She left the tally of the year's two storms and the boats in the harbor-book in the jar that week, and the stick of two notches, and struck the bell once. [KEEPER: confirm an answering stick.]

On the twenty-second night of the month the longest storm of the year came in, a day and a night, none worse than the town knows. The boats were hauled. The tenders kept the harbor lamp through it with the Warden-Monarch's heir on the worst of the night. The yard shed over the point's keel held. The new pile and the post held, and the Harbormaster stood on the quay head with a lantern and did not say why.

### Month 5: the long sea

After the longest storm the sea ran long and heavy for some days. The watch on the lamp-house stair heard the Skerries at the second dawn and the Harbormaster kept every boat inside the mouth for four days. On the first of the four, with no boat out and nothing to meet, he sent the Captains' boat to the mouth with a long line made fast to the quay head's outer pile, and had her stand there through the day, so that the Captains' crew would stand it once before they had to. The point stayed in. The east cove said it was a dry fishing again, and the Harbormaster said a dry fishing and a full harbor. The old woman at the east lamp had no knowing that week and was not cold. The keeping's lamp stayed in the lamp-house. No boat was caught in a swell this winter.

In the same month the yard laid the garboards and ribs on the point's keel, with the younger son of the point at the work every day from the first light, and the hired hand beside him.

### Month 6: the fourth storm, the dim half ends

The fourth storm came early in the month, a night, and was the last of the winter. The clerks wrote the winter's count in the harbor-book: four storms between the second month and the sixth, the longest a day and a night, none worse than the town knows. No boat was lost to a storm. Chest fevers took some of the old, as every winter, and a cold in the sixth month took a few more. There were births in the long nights. The Warden-Monarch left the stick of two notches in the jar with the winter's tally and struck the bell once. [KEEPER: confirm an answering stick.]

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

### Month 7: the lean, the quiet at the point

Spring was lean, as it is. The Reeve opened the shared store in the first week and held to it. The Eldest Midwife kept the households' share even, the point's with the rest. Nobody starved. Some of the old, and a few of the very young, died of the weak chest that comes with a thin spring. The Harbormaster kept the order against sending boats out in the worst weather, and on a bad day said it was his own order, which no one had asked.

The point went quiet on a good many nights through the lean, more than in the second year, and on some of them the gulls left the step's foot and the seals left the skerry, and the gleaners would not walk the strand. The fishers said the listener was in the water and had not gone up the shore this year. Some said the east cove's salt on the step had kept it. The old woman at the east lamp was not cold and said the listener was near, and that it was not the nearness of a knowing. The Warden-Monarch left the jar in the second week with the winter's tally, the stick of two notches and a plain word that the store was open and held, and struck the bell once. [KEEPER: the god near; what it found; whether a stick came.]

On the thirteenth night of the month, under a clear sky, the star dimmed, in the same way. The Warden-Monarch put the third notch on her stick. The old woman said the nights had been three, five, six, seven and now three, and that it did not count up, and that she did not know what it counted.

### Month 8: the levy

The levy of about one hundred and fifty was drilled in the first week, as every spring, and the Captains drilled it at the chain and, for the first time, at the mouth, with the long line made fast to the quay head's outer pile and the heaving line thrown from the Captains' boat to a skiff held in the swell by two men, over and over until the levy could do it without a word. The truce held. Each use of the chain was written by two hands, the quay's man and the line's tender, and the Harbormaster and the Warden-Monarch each said, in the hearing of his own side, that it was a truce and not a peace.

### Month 9: the shoal, the word of the strake, the fishing turns

In the second week a second stick lay across the jar's lid before dawn, with a long row of short notches in groups and one mark cut across at one place. A word came with it, and she read it as asking her to give it to the Harbormaster to draw into his book, if he would. [KEEPER: the shoal off the point; what the notches and the mark are.] She took it to the harbor-court the same day and laid it on the step before him.

He took it in both hands and turned it a long time. He said he took a tide and not a word, but that a stick with notches was a thing he could lay a lead against. He read the groups as fathoms, taking the length of the first cut for the length of his own lead-line's first mark, and the single mark across as the place where the long swell stands up on the shoal. He said the mark lay on the near shoal beyond the mouth, where the point's boat had stayed for the bite. He said whoever cut the notches had known a lead line. He said he would sound it himself, on calm mornings, and he did: on three mornings in the last weeks of the month and the first of the next, with the Captains' boat and a boy at the lead, along the line the stick's groups ran and across the mark. He drew the near shoal on a hide, at the harbor-court's table, with a lamp-black ink for what his lead agreed with and charcoal for what it did not. He told the Warden-Monarch which was which, and told the Captains nothing. He said he would not send a boat down a line drawn by a stick until his own lead had been down it in a long swell three times, and that was a thing he could not ask of the sea. [KEEPER: what the lead found along the notched line, and how much of the hide is ink and how much charcoal.]

At the start of the month's last week a third stick lay across the lid, and she read it as asking her to tell the Master of Timber that the keeping asked for a strake of the boat that goes out latest and comes in last, with the Master to choose. She carried it to the yard that day.

The fishing turned at the end of the month and the lean ended. The gulls were on the step through the last week. The old woman at the east lamp said the listener was where it had been all spring, or was somewhere near it listening, and that she could not tell which.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

### Month 10: the shepherds, the launching, the strake

Summer was earned. The shepherds of the bare hills came down with wool and mutton at the start of the summer and took salt fish. They asked a fisher at the landing nothing about the bell.

In the first week the point's new boat was launched, a decked boat smaller than the one lost, built by the point's younger son and the yard's men on the keel of the oak the yard gave. The widow's brother-in-law was her master, with the younger son and the hired hand for crew. The ferryman came to the slip. The widow did not. The younger son left a heel of bread on the lowest step the morning she first went out, with no word said, and the widow herself left none. The Master of Timber looked the boat over from keel to stem and said she was a sound boat, and said no more.

The Master of Timber chose the point ferryman's boat for the strake: the sheer strake on the side where the tow-line chafes, which a boat that goes out latest and comes in last wears fastest, and which had checked along its lower edge in the weather of the year before. He did not choose the keel, which was clean. He took it to the Harbormaster, who asked the price and was told there was none. He did not like it. He took it for the ferryman's sake, and said so, and said the yard was kind to the point every year and he was beginning to wonder what it was repaying. The ferryman said the yard had been kind to him twice, and that he had begun to wonder too.

### Month 11: the coasting boats, the strake, the boat in the swell

Three coasting boats from the Iss Levels [r07] came in the eleventh month, as most summers. Salt, rope and fish went out. Sound oak, enough for one keel and some planking, and nine loads of thin grain came in, and a few pieces of the court's old silver went out. No hull came in that was not known on this coast.

In the second week, on a dark night in the yard, with the new strake laid on the blocks beside the hauled ferryman's boat, the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the boat. The hooded figure came up from the water and laid its right hand on the strake and held it there, and went away down to the water without a word. The Master of Timber said that was the tenth piece in five years and that it was enough, and told no one but the Warden-Monarch. The yard fitted the strake that month. [KEEPER: Sound Timber, the strake.]

The ferryman said again that the water off his bow lay strangely quiet on some evenings, and that it was the same quiet as in the years the yard had been kind, and that he did not like to say why. The lamp-tenders said the quay went quiet on four nights, below the lowest step, and that the gulls would not stand there. The Midwife's girl said the strand below the east cove landing went quiet on one. [KEEPER: whom it watched.]

On the nineteenth day of the month the sea ran long from the northwest from the first light, with no wind on the shore and a fair sky. The watch on the lamp-house stair heard the Skerries at midday, and the Harbormaster, who had had the stair watched from the first light under his own rule, sent the quay boy down the landings to call in what was out near. The near boats came in. A decked line-and-ling boat of the quay's own households did not. She was a sound boat of eleven years, with four aboard: her master, a man of about thirty-eight; his wife's brother, about thirty-three; the master's son of seventeen; and a hired lad of thirteen. They were on the outer ling grounds beyond the near shoal, an hour farther out than the nearest boats, hauling the last of the long line, which carried most of the households' autumn salt fish, and they stayed to haul it, as the summer's run makes men stay. By an hour before dusk the watch had her in sight outside the shoal, low in the water with the line aboard and the swell standing up behind her, running for the mouth between the Garrow Skerries [f31] and the quay with the swell breaking on the Skerries on her beam.

The Warden-Monarch went down to the lowest step and struck the bell twice, at about an hour before dusk. The Harbormaster had the chain let down, with the lamp-tender of the line and his own man at the capstan and both names written in the harbor-book. He put out the Captains' boat, with both Captains, to stand at the mouth on the harbor side of the chain's line with the long line made fast to the quay head's outer pile and a heaving line coiled in her bow. The Warden-Monarch's heir was in the lamp-tower with the harbor lamp. The Warden-Monarch had the keeping's lamp taken to the water's edge below the lamp, by the harbor mouth, and the old woman, now seventy-six, and the boy, now seventeen, went with it. She said aloud before the tenders and her heir, and again when it was lit, that it was a light, to steer by, and that she had struck the bell. The old woman lit it with a coal from the lamp-house and it burned. They filled it and trimmed it as they were shown, and filled it again before the first watch was out, and the boy ran the oil from the lamp-house and did not fall. The old woman did not leave the lamp.

The point stayed in. The ferryman stood on the quay head with the younger son and the hired hand, and the widow stood behind them. He said to the Harbormaster that he had not stood on a quay and watched a boat run for the mouth in his life, and that he would not choose to again, and he stood. The Harbormaster said nothing to him. The senior Captain, at the mouth, said afterward that the point's boats had not so much as been put on their rollers.

In the first hour of the night, with the boat still outside the Skerries' end and not yet in the mouth, the Warden-Monarch struck the bell twice more. The old woman at the east lamp had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before. She said so. [KEEPER: the bell struck twice at about an hour before dusk and twice in the first hour of the night; whether the god heard it and how soon it came is yours. What a lamp lit as a light and said aloud to be a light does to the water, if anything.]

[GOD CAME] A hooded figure stood at the water's edge below the lamp in the first hour of the night, a little after the second pair of strokes, and the tenders said it came up out of the water and did not speak, and that it took the lamp from the old woman's hands and lifted it, and that the lamp flared and stood in the dark as bright as the harbor's, as in the first three years. It stood there and did not leave the lamp. The senior Captain, at the mouth, saw it and said nothing. The Warden-Monarch stood a little way off and said nothing. [KEEPER: Calm Under the Lamp; what the lamp did to the water.]

[GOD NOT THERE] Nobody stood at the water's edge. The tenders looked along the strand, and the senior Captain looked from the mouth, and the strokes went unanswered. The Warden-Monarch said nothing. She stood by the lamp with her hands folded, which is what she does when she is frightened.

The outcome of the boat is the Keeper's to settle. It is written both ways.

[BOAT CAME IN] The boat came in through the mouth in the second hour of the night with all four aboard, with the swell still breaking on the Skerries outside it, and the Captains took her on the long line and the heaving line together. The four sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night with their hands round warm bowls. The lamp burned at the water's edge through the night. [GOD CAME] The point said the keeping came to the bell and the boat came in, and that the bell is a thing that can be called, and the east cove put two pinches of salt on the step. [GOD NOT THERE] The point said the line and the long rope and the Captains did it, and the bell was struck and nothing came to it, and the Harbormaster said the rule held and the point stayed in and that was the whole of the matter. The ferryman said he had stood on the quay head and was not sorry. The Harbormaster put a seventh line in his tally.

[BOAT LOST] The boat broke at the mouth in the second hour of the night. The master and his wife's brother drowned. The son and the hired lad were hauled out by the Captains' boat on the long line and the heaving line together, and sat in the lamp-house the rest of the night. The lamp burned at the water's edge until the last of them was in or past help, and the old woman and the boy kept it till the Harbormaster told them to stop, and the Warden-Monarch had it taken back to the lamp-house still lit. The town set two more stones on the empty cairn, and the cairn has now had stones for the quay's boats two years running. [GOD CAME] The town said the keeping came and the sea took two, and some said it came too late and some that it came as it could, and the widow of the quay's boat did not say. [GOD NOT THERE] The town said the bell was struck twice and twice again and nothing came, and the east cove said the keeping was not within the bell's reach, or would not come. The Harbormaster said at the harbor-court that the rule held, the point stayed in, the line was used, and two were lost, and that a rule was not a lamp and was not a keeper either. The ferryman said that he had stood on the quay head and watched, and that he did not know any longer which was worse. The Harbormaster put a seventh line in his tally.

### Month 12: the tally

The fishing was fair. In the third week the ferryman went back out in fair weather for a late boat with a fouled sail and towed her in, as he has done all his life, and called it a small thing.

The Warden-Monarch left the jar on the lowest step at the year's turn with the tally of the storms, four, and the boats in the harbor-book, and the stick of three notches, as the keeping had asked, and the clerks' list of the year's dead in the Eldest Midwife's name, which the Midwife had given her for it, with the two from the point at the head of it as before. She asked no question in words. [KEEPER: whether a stick came.]

## At the year's turn

The people come to the opening of autumn with the racks and the loft in ordinary stock for the winter ahead, the court's chest a little lighter in silver and the same in bronze, the point with a boat again, and the yard with sound oak for one keel and a second keel timber in the loft. The Warden-Monarch has been told she had the right and has told the court so, and her standing outside it is mended by the year in one branch and not in the other. The Eldest Midwife has been told twice that the keeping has no whole answer and says it is the first honest half she has had. The Harbormaster has two boats on the cairn's flat stone, a mouth he has sounded and a hide half in ink, and does not say he takes the word. The Master of Timber has ten sound pieces in the quay and the yard and says nothing of them. The senior Captain has seen the bell struck and has stood the long line. The town has seen three nights of the star dimmed, a spring with the point quiet and no figure on the step, and the nine found under the chain, and does not agree about any of it.
