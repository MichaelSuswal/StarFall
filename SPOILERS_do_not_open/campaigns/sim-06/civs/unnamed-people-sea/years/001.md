# Year 1: the unnamed people of god-sea

Written by the civ agent from the god's decisions and the Keeper's pass one. Months are counted from the opening of autumn. The people have no name for themselves or their town in this file, and no office holder has a name. Where a thing turns on a power or on an uncertain outcome, the line is marked [KEEPER] and the matter is in `report-001.md`.

## Autumn (months 1 to 3)

### Month 1: the shore goes quiet

The year opened as years do here. The last catch was ordinary: mackerel and herring salted on the racks, the smoking sheds running day and night, the boats hauled in turn and caulked. The Eldest Midwife and the Reeve of the Fields counted the stores against the winter as they do, and found them enough if nothing went wrong. The two boats lost last autumn were still not spoken of in the Harbormaster's hearing.

In the first days the fishers noticed that the gulls had left the foot of the quay. The seals that lie on the skerry by the harbor mouth were gone from it for the first night and the day after, and came back by ones and twos. No one saw why. Two evenings later the gulls were gone again from the quay's lowest step and stayed away on some evenings of the month and not others. The gleaners at the point said the strand there was quiet in a way they did not like, and left it at that. The old woman at the east lamp said, to the girl who brings her bread, that the lowest step "has a listener on it," and was cold for two days after, which is less than she is cold after a knowing, and so no one made much of it. The Warden-Monarch's lamp-tenders lit the harbor lamp at every dusk as the custom has it. The Warden-Monarch herself lit it on the first dusk of the dark half, as her line has done, and walked the quay after, as she does.

Twice in this month the star dimmed, on the ninth night and the seventeenth. Each time the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour in the middle of the night, and it was as before. The people who were awake saw it. They have seen it in other years. The clerks put a mark in the harbor-book for each night, as they do. Some fishers said it was a thing that happened when the sea breathed in. Two said it was new this year, that it had not been twice in a month before; the old said it had, once, in their grandfathers' time, and no one could say what year.

### Month 2: the tide-door

The Warden-Monarch had been told by her lamp-tenders of the quiet at the step, and she went down to the tide-door at the lowest tide at dusk on the fourth night of the third week, alone, with a shuttered lantern, as her mother had told her one went, in the old tellings, when the bell had to be struck. She had hung the sealed jar and struck the bell once every autumn of her rule, as the custom of her house requires, for twenty-two autumns, and no word had come back.

This night something was at the foot of the step. A hooded figure in a cloak that ran with salt water stood in the dimness below the lamp's reach, and said it was the one who keeps the keeping. It said it wanted the boats hauled and the stores counted before the first storm. It said a word left on the lowest step in a sealed jar, with the bell struck once, would reach it, and that it would answer with a stick and a mark before dawn. It said its lamp, the harbor lamp, was not to be lent or snuffed, and nothing more of the lamp. It asked her to tell the court in her own way, and to name it to no one outside the court. The Warden-Monarch kept still. She was given leave to see its shape, which the figure showed her once, in the dim, only because she kept still, and she has told no one all that she saw. What she says is that it was old, that it was wet, that it was not a man; she says nothing more. [KEEPER: what she saw.]

She asked it its name. It said, Skerr. She asked whether it was the one to whom Brennis had spoken, and the figure said nothing she has repeated. She offered it a bed in the lamp-house, and it went down into the water without answering. [KEEPER: any answer to the question about Brennis.]

She did not give her answer that night. She went up the quay steps, sat in the lamp-house through the night, and at the next dusk, before the lamp-tenders lit the lamp, she set a sealed jar on the lowest step with a notched stick in it: the count of boats in the harbor-book, the count of boats in the yard, and the count of the court's stores in their order. She struck the bell once with her own hand. Before dawn the jar was where she had left it, sealed, with a second stick laid across its lid. It bore a mark she read. She said it was an answer. She will not say what it said. [KEEPER: the stick and its mark.] No one living had had one. She had been taking the promise as a kind of manners, the thing one does. By this she took the word of the keeping, as a lamp-warden of the old line, and for no other reason than the stick. She had not told anyone of the jar's answer before the end of the month.

### Month 3: the court, the early hauling, the pile

At the low-water sitting on the quay steps in the first week of the third month she told the court what she had been told. She told it to the four who sit on the steps beside her: the Harbormaster, the Master of Timber, the Reeve of the Fields and the Eldest Midwife. The two Captains were not there. She said, "the keeping has spoken at the tide-door, and it asks that the boats be hauled early, caulked and tarred, the late boats drawn in before the first blow, the chain kept ready, and the lamp lit every dusk through the dark half, as it is every year." She said that the keeping gave its name as Skerr. She did not say what it looked like. She told them she was to name it to no one outside them.

The Harbormaster heard her out and said nothing for a time. He is a man whose sister's son was on one of the lost boats last autumn, and the quarrel with the line over the chain was already between them. He thought, and said in the sitting, that the word was a convenient one for a Warden who wanted the chain. He did not say she lied. He said, in the sitting and then to the Captains, that the early hauling was a Warden's order and that he would see it done "for the sake of the court's peace." He did not take the word of the keeping. He took the order. The Master of Timber said he would believe it when he had something in his hand. The Reeve of the Fields said the count of stores wanted doing anyway. The Eldest Midwife said the keeping had a deal to answer for if it was the thing the old people said, and that she would wait to hear what it said to her. She asked that stores be shared evenly through the lean, as in a bad year, and the Reeve agreed.

So the boats were hauled early. The Harbormaster drew them in about ten days before he would have, in the middle of the month, with the late fishing boats called in from the outer grounds. The fishers lost those days of the last catch, which was a poor catch to begin with, and they grumbled. The yard caulked and tarred. The chain was inspected, with the Harbormaster and a lamp-tender both at the capstan, each saying the other had the key. The stores were counted and written in the harbor-book.

In the third week the Warden-Monarch brought the Master of Timber to the quay in the dark on a night with no moon. She stood a little way off. The hooded figure came up the lowest step, and said, to the Master of Timber, that one piece at the quay was its own, and that he should not say which. The Master asked what it owed for that, and why it should be secret. [KEEPER: any answer.] The figure laid its right hand on a pile at the quay, the one by the lowest step, and held it there while the Master watched, and then went down into the water. The Master of Timber went at first light and put his hands on the pile. [KEEPER: what he found; what the pile is.] He told no one which piece he had put his hand on, and he told the Warden-Monarch only that he took the word, and that he would keep the thing quiet. He said, to his own yard, that he had been looking for sound timber for a year and that this was what a long look found.

A day later the Warden-Monarch had the jar hung again with the word "done" in a stick. There was no answer, and she took none to be needed.

Late in the third month the first winter storm came. All the boats were hauled and tarred. The court's lamp was lit. The storm lasted a night and a day. No boat was lost and no roof was lost. The town was dry. It was said in the town, by those who knew that the Warden had hauled the boats early, that she had had the weather in her ear. The Harbormaster said that any fool could have known a storm was coming at the end of autumn, and that he would have hauled the boats himself within the week. He was heard to say this twice in the harbor-court and he was right. [KEEPER: the effect of the pile in the storms, if any.]

At the same time, for some days, the cranes passed overhead in long flights, going inland. The fishers said cranes going inland late meant a hard winter.

## Winter (months 4 to 6)

Winter was mending, boiling salt, spinning and waiting. The storms were counted, in the tally sticks and the harbor-book: five through the winter. Cranes had said hard. It was not. The longest was a blow of four days in the fifth month; none was worse than the old have seen. The Reeve of the Fields kept the stores in the shared store, a third of the households' own fish being put in by the Warden-Monarch's word and the Midwife's agreement, to be shared in the lean.

On the first night of the sixth storm count, the sixth, which turned out not to come, the Warden-Monarch left a tally stick in the jar on the lowest step, as the figure had asked: the storms counted, the boats in the harbor-book. The stick bore the count to date, which was three storms, as of the fourth month. Before dawn a second stick lay on the jar. The Warden-Monarch read it, and it was much as before.

In the fourth month, between the second storm and the third, a small decked boat, one of the late ones, with five aboard, was caught out at dusk with a swell rising and no storm yet in it. It came toward the harbor mouth between the skerries and the quay, in the dusk, with the chain down and the lamp-tender's lamp lit, and the swell breaking on the Garrow Skerries on its beam. The Harbormaster put out the two Captains' boat to stand by at the mouth. The lamp-tenders at the tide-door said afterward that a hooded figure stood at the water's edge below the lamp, and that the lamp flared when it lifted its hand. [KEEPER: Calm Under the Lamp; what the lamp did to the water.] The outcome of that boat is the Keeper's to settle. Written both ways:

- If the boat came in: five came home, and the crew went to the lamp-house and sat there, and the town said the lamp had brought them in. That is true as far as it goes, the town said, and the Warden-Monarch took it as meaning more than it was. The Harbormaster said the crew had done it themselves, which is also true as far as it goes.
- If the boat was lost: three of the five were drowned in the harbor mouth, and two were hauled out by the Captains' boat. The town set three more stones on the empty cairn, and the Harbormaster said, not in the sitting, that the Warden's lamp had not saved them. The Warden-Monarch said nothing. The line was not blamed in the open, and the thing was held against it in the quiet.

In the fifth month, on a clear night between the long blow and the next storm, the star dimmed again, in the same way: the blue deepened for about an hour, the star stood dim for about an hour, and it was as before. This was the third time in the year. The clerks wrote the three nights together in the harbor-book, and the old woman at the east lamp said that it was a counting.

Winter deaths were chiefly among the old: chest fevers and a hard cold in the sixth month. There were births in the long nights, as there always are.

## Spring (months 7 to 9)

Spring was lean, as it is. The shared store was opened in the seventh month, and the Reeve held to it. The Eldest Midwife kept the households' share even. Nobody starved. Some of the old and some of the very young died of the weak chest that comes with a thin spring. The court, on the Warden-Monarch's word as the keeping's word, sent no boat out in the worst weather for the sake of a catch, and the Harbormaster, to his credit, kept it. No boat was lost in the spring.

Some of the gleaners at the low springs saw a figure walking the strand west and east of the point, slowly, at night, a hooded old man at an old man's pace. They saw it at the lowest tide. It was seen three times, by different gleaners. It did not speak. It marked things on the rocks. The marks were found afterward at the tideline, small and few, and nobody in the town set them. One of the old fishers said the walkers had come back, the tally-voices, who sang names on the quay when the old were children. The Warden-Monarch said it was nothing for the gleaners to be afraid of, and said no more. [KEEPER: the marks, and what the walker found.]

The levy of about one hundred and fifty was drilled in the eighth month, as every spring, and the Captains drilled it at the chain. The Warden-Monarch and the Harbormaster, with the clerks, settled the quarrel over the chain in the only way it could be settled for the year: the Harbormaster answers for the chain's raising and lowering, and a lamp-tender of the Warden-Monarch's household holds its key and lays it in the harbor-book each time. It was a truce and not a peace, and each of them said so in the hearing of his own side.

## Summer (months 10 to 12)

Summer was earned. The fishing was good in the tenth and eleventh months, fair in the twelfth. The shepherds of the bare hills came down with wool and mutton and took salt fish, twice, once at the start of summer and again at its end. The coasting boats from the river town on the levels came, three of them, in the eleventh month, as most summers. The Master of Timber traded salt, rope and fish for sound oak planks from their boats, enough for the keel and some hull planking of two boats, and a few pieces of the court's old silver went out with them. Grain came in thin, twelve loads.

At the start of the summer the Warden-Monarch told the Master of Timber that the keeping asked him to choose the boat that goes out latest and comes in last. He chose the point ferryman's boat, which has gone back out for late boats for as long as the ferryman has lived. In the eleventh month, on a dark night in the boatyard, with the keel timber already laid, the hooded figure put its right hand on the keel and held it there, and went away down to the water. [KEEPER: the keel.] The Master of Timber said that was the second, and that was enough, and he told no one but the Warden-Monarch. The court's boatwrights built the hull from it that summer. The ferryman did not know why his boat had the first sound keel of the yard's year; he thought the yard was repaying a debt, and said so.

No ferryman, midwife or lamp-tender was told of the keeping. A hooded figure was seen at the town's edge on some evenings in the summer, by the lamp-tenders and by one of the midwife's girls, and it was not spoken to. [KEEPER: whom it watched.]

A fisher's wife, at the shepherds' second landing, told a shepherd, who had asked why the bell was struck so often on the quay, that "the bell on the quay was answered." She meant it in confidence. It went to the hills.

## At the year's turn

The people come to the opening of autumn with the racks and the loft in ordinary stock for the winter ahead, the court's chest a little lighter in silver and the same in bronze, a Warden-Monarch whose standing within the court has risen and whose standing outside it is one of whispers, a Harbormaster who has done what was asked and does not take the word, a Master of Timber who has two sound pieces in his keeping and will say nothing of them, and a town that has had two things it did not have: a tally answered, and a figure seen on the shore at night.
