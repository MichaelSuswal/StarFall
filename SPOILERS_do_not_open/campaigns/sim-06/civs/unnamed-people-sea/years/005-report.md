# Report to the Keeper: civ unnamed-people-sea, Year 5, pass one

## How the god was met

Autumn, month 1.
- The Warden-Monarch: hung the jar and struck the bell once on the first dusk of the dark half. Before dawn a stick lay across the lid, and she read it as asking her to the tide-door on the fourth night after. She met the god alone at the lowest tide at dusk. It answered her question of the eighth month of Year 4 first, in the words of its decisions, and she kept still and said she would try (to not carry it). It gave her its words for the Harbormaster and the Eldest Midwife, once, and asked her to carry each by her own mouth, alone, on a day she judged, with nothing sent. She said each back once, whole. It told her what to say if either asked more. It read the Midwife's list and the stick of seven notches standing. It told her it would not go far from the bell this year, asked her to strike once for a word and twice for a boat in trouble, and said it would come at once by water from within a night's swim of the quay. It thanked her for the tenders and the lamp. She took its word. In the third week she told the inner court what it had said of the lamp and the right, all but the last four words. She told the senior Captain, her heir and the two tenders the same, and the bell's two strokes. After the stones were laid a stick across the jar's lid told her so, and she carried the Harbormaster's words in the fourth week.
- The Eldest Midwife: heard the god's words from the Warden-Monarch's own mouth, alone, in her kitchen, in the second week. She said a half answer called a half is the only kind a midwife trusts, that it is the third time the water has told her what it could not tell, that she would tell the widow's daughters yes, it was in the house, and no more, and that she sends no house this year. She took its word as honest and not as an order.
- The Harbormaster: heard the god's words from the Warden-Monarch's own mouth, alone, at the chain's post, in the fourth week. He heard it through with his hand on a link. He said he had said the lead, not the tide, and let that stand; asked who else knew; said he knew what "after that it is yours to say" meant and did not thank it for it; asked that the line's household be told nothing until he had something in his hand. He did not take its word and was not hostile.
- The Master of Timber, the Reeve: heard in the sitting what the Warden-Monarch said of the lamp. The Master said that if it was far it could be near, and that he would like to know how it meant to be. The Reeve said the stores did not care whose lamp it was.
- The senior Captain: told by the Warden-Monarch in the lamp-house the next day. He said he would tell the second Captain that the bell had two strokes and not what they were for.

Autumn, month 2.
- The Harbormaster put a lead along the chain's line on the fourth morning, at the lowest springs, from the Captains' boat with both Captains and the Master of Timber, and told the Captains why in four words, "I am told where." It rang on three stones and the grapnel took a strake and a rib beside them (see Uncertain outcomes, 2). He told the Warden-Monarch that night that it was so, and said before the nine families at the cairn what he had found, and laid the pieces on the cairn. He asked the Warden-Monarch to tell it that the lead rang. He did not take its word.
- The Warden-Monarch told the water at the tide-door in the third week what both had answered, and left a notched stick in the jar and struck the bell once.

Autumn, month 3.
- The Master of Timber: asked by the Warden-Monarch whether the point meant to build a boat and would take a keel of the yard's oak, joined by the keeping's hand, no price. He said he would ask the point himself. He went to the point in the fourth week and told the widow, who did not ask it straight, that the oak and the keel were the yard's; he said he would answer her straight if she asked whether any other hand would lie on it. He took the word.

Winter.
- Month 4: the Master of Timber met the god with the Warden-Monarch in the yard on a night with no moon, saw it lay its right hand on one of two keel timbers at the scarf, and took the word. The Harbormaster assented to the keel for the point's sake and without being told it was the keeping's. In the third week the Warden-Monarch told the inner court the winter requests and the lamp as a light; the Harbormaster and the Reeve said it was already done and the quay's. She asked the Harbormaster, alone, to say as the court's own that in a long swell with no storm the Captains' boat and crew stand at the mouth with a line and no boat of the point goes out to meet it. He said it was his Skerries rule with a hard second half and gave it at the harbor-court as the quay's. The point heard itself named.
- Months 5 and 6: no boat was caught in a swell. In month 5 the Captains stood the new rule once with no boat out. The god was not met. The jar was left with the tally in months 4 and 6.

Spring.
- Month 7: the point went quiet on many nights through the lean. The Warden-Monarch left the jar with the winter's tally, the stick of two notches and the store word. The god was not met.
- Month 9: a stick with notches and a mark lay across the lid in the second week; she took it to the Harbormaster the same day. He read the notches as fathoms and the mark as where the long swell stands up, and sounded the near shoal on three mornings. A third stick in the last week asked her to tell the Master the keeping asked for a strake; she did.

Summer.
- Month 10: the Master of Timber chose the point ferryman's boat for the sheer strake and had the Harbormaster's assent with no price. The Harbormaster does not know the strake is the keeping's.
- Month 11: the god laid its right hand on the strake in the yard in the second week, before the Master and the Warden-Monarch, and said nothing. The Master took the word. On the nineteenth day the bell was struck (see Uncertain outcomes, 1). No mortal spoke to the god in the year except the Warden-Monarch at the tide-door in month 1.

## What was done in the god's name

- By the Warden-Monarch: carried the god's two words by her own mouth to the Midwife and the Harbormaster; gave the Midwife's list and the stick of seven notches to the god; told the inner court, the senior Captain, her heir and the tenders what it said of the lamp and the bell; told the water the two answers in month 2 and left the jar; asked the Master of Timber about the point's keel and the strake; brought him to the yard twice; told the inner court the winter requests and that the lamp may be lit as a light and said aloud; asked the Harbormaster, as the court's own and not the keeping's, to say the new rule; carried the shoal's notched stick to the Harbormaster; kept a stick of three notches for the star; struck the bell twice and twice again on the night of the swell and had the lamp lit as a light and said aloud that it was one; left the jar at the year's turn with the tally of four storms, the stick of three notches and the Midwife's list.
- By the Master of Timber: went to the point himself and offered the keel as the yard's; had the yard cut two keel timbers so none could say which; chose the ferryman's boat for the strake and got the Harbormaster's assent.
- By the Eldest Midwife: gave the clerks' list for the jar. Sent no house.
- By the Harbormaster: the hauling and the Skerries rule as his own orders; the assent to the keel and the strake without knowing; the lead and grapnel on the chain's line on a word the Warden carried, his own act, and he does not say it was for the keeping; the rule naming the point, given as the quay's from a word the Warden brought as the court's; the sounding of the near shoal and the hide. None of these is in the god's name by his saying.
- By the two lamp-tenders: in month 11 only, lit, filled, trimmed and kept the keeping's lamp as a light on the Warden-Monarch's word, which she said was to be said aloud.
- By the two Captains: stood the long line at the mouth in month 5 and month 11 on the Harbormaster's order.
- Nothing was done in the god's name by the Reeve.

## Uncertain outcomes for the Keeper

1. The boat in the swell, month 11, the nineteenth day, in the fair weather of the summer's run. A decked line-and-ling boat of the quay's own households, eleven years old, sound, not patched twice, with four aboard: her master, a man of about thirty-eight; his wife's brother, about thirty-three; the master's son of seventeen; a hired lad of thirteen. They were on the outer ling grounds beyond the near shoal, an hour farther out than the nearest boats, hauling the last of the long line, which held most of the households' autumn salt fish, and they stayed to haul it. The sea ran long from the northwest from first light with no wind on the shore and a fair sky. The watch on the lamp-house stair heard the Skerries at midday, under the Harbormaster's own rule, and the quay boy called in the near boats; the near boats came in. By an hour before dusk the watch had the boat in sight outside the shoal, low in the water with the long line aboard and the swell standing up behind her, running for the mouth between the Garrow Skerries [f31] and the quay with the swell breaking on the Skerries on her beam. Her crew know the coast and stayed for the line, not for want of skill.
   - Circumstances from the record and the year:
     - The chain was let down by the Harbormaster, who was on the quay within the hour, with the lamp-tender of the line and his own man at the capstan, both names written in the harbor-book.
     - The Captains' boat, with the new keel of Year 2, with both Captains aboard, stood at the mouth on the harbor side of the chain's line, with a long line made fast to the quay head's outer pile (the pile of Year 4) and a heaving line coiled in her bow. The levy had drilled the heaving line to a skiff in the swell in month 8.
     - The Warden-Monarch's heir was in the lamp-tower with the harbor lamp.
     - The point's boats stayed ashore under the court's new rule. The ferryman stood on the quay head with the point's younger son and the hired hand.
     - The bell was struck twice at about an hour before dusk, and twice again in the first hour of the night with the boat still outside the Skerries' end.
     - The Warden-Monarch had the keeping's lamp taken to the water's edge below the lamp by the harbor mouth, and said aloud, before the tenders and again when it was lit, that it was a light, to steer by, and that she had struck the bell. The old woman (seventy-six) lit it with a coal from the lamp-house and tended it as shown, filled it again before the first watch was out, and did not leave it. The boy (seventeen) ran the oil and did not fall. The net-mender who frees fouled lines was ashore and her power was not in play.
     - The old woman at the east lamp had no knowing that week and had not been cold in the days before. I leave what that means to you, as in earlier years.
     - The Harbormaster has a hide of the near shoal, half in ink and half in charcoal, from the god's notched stick and his own lead. He did not give it to the Captains or the crew and had not proved it in a swell. Whether the real shoal bears on the boat's course is yours.
     - Three years running a boat came in under this lamp with the god at it; in Year 4 it was lit by the tenders without the god and calmed nothing, by your word. This year it was lit as a light, said aloud to be one.
   - Whether the god heard the bell and how soon it came is yours. I wrote the hours both ways: [GOD CAME], a hooded figure at the water's edge below the lamp in the first hour of the night, a little after the second pair of strokes, taking the lamp from the old woman and lifting it, the lamp flaring as in the first three years; [GOD NOT THERE], nobody at the water's edge and the strokes unanswered. If you rule that it came sooner, the year reads the same with the figure at the first pair of strokes; if you rule that it heard and came late, move it to after the boat is at the mouth. What the water does if it came is yours (Calm Under the Lamp).
   - Success: all four come in, the Captains take her on the long line and the heaving line at the mouth. Failure: the boat breaks at the mouth, the master and his wife's brother drown, the son and the hired lad are hauled out by the Captains' boat.
   - The year is written both ways: population 2,045 or 2,043; the town's mood, the Warden-Monarch's standing outside the court, the Harbormaster's talk and the east cove's salt differ by branch; the lore carries [GOD CAME] and [GOD NOT THERE] lines under each.
2. No other contested outcome was written. Two things I did not make contested:
   - The Harbormaster's lead and grapnel on the chain's line, month 2: written as your pass one has it (the lead rings on stone where the three pale stones lie and the drag finds the broken lower timbers of two boats built as the town builds, where the chain crosses). I had the grapnel lift one strake and one rib by ordinary seamanship, as in Year 3's unfound branch, and no more. If lifting two pieces of a hull from the bed is beyond what the town can do, tell me and I will revise.
   - The point taking the keel: the point took it, on the ferryman's word that the yard's keel had stood five winters and on the widow's saying she would set no hand to it and forbid none. That is the point's own act.

## What turns on a power

- Water's Hearing, month 1 night and day: written only as the gulls and seals leaving the point and the skerry and the old woman's two days of cold.
- The three pale stones: the god says laying them by hand is ordinary craft. I wrote them only as what the lead rang on, per your pass one, and as what the town makes of them (a grave; the causeway's stones). Please settle whether anything of them is more.
- Sound Timber, the keel, month 4: the god's right hand on one of two keel timbers at the scarf. What the keel is, what the Master found at first light, and how the point's boat does are not settled. The year does not credit the keel.
- Sound Timber, the strake, month 11: the god's right hand on the ferryman's sheer strake. What the strake is, and how the boat does, are not settled.
- Calm Under the Lamp, month 11: lit by the tenders as a light, said aloud to be a light. What that does to the water, if anything, is yours; and whether the god lit it, if it came, and what the water did.
- The bell: whether it was heard, by whom, how soon the god came. The hours are above.
- The sticks across the jar in months 1 (twice), 3, 4, 9 (twice): she reads them and says what they ask. How a stick carries a request is yours, and so is whether any stick answered her leavings in months 2, 4, 6, 7 and 12.
- The Warden-Monarch's telling to the water at the tide-door in month 2 (both answers, the lead ringing, the stones left): whether the god heard it is yours.
- The god's nearness in spring: written as the point quiet on a good many nights and the gulls and seals leaving. I gave no place for it.
- The shoal off the point, month 9: what the notches and the mark are is yours. The Harbormaster read them as fathoms and a mark on the near shoal and sounded it on three mornings; what the lead found along the line, and how much of his hide is ink and how much charcoal, is yours. I wrote only that he sounded and drew and that he will not send a boat down the line until his lead has been down it in a long swell three times.
- The three dimmings of the star, months 2 (the ninth and twenty-fourth nights) and 7 (the thirteenth night): written as seen, with no claim of cause. The people tie them to the sea, the step and the keeping's nearness.
- The summer watching: written as the ferryman's quiet water, the lamp-tenders' four nights below the lowest step and the Midwife's girl's one night. Whom it watched is yours.
- The Midwife's household was not visited. She sent no house, so the god did not come to a bed in Year 5.

## What touches anyone else

- Trade:
  - The shepherds of the Bren Hills [r05] came twice, at the start of summer and at its end, with wool and mutton for salt fish. They asked nothing about the bell.
  - Three coasting boats from the Iss Levels [r07] came in month 11. Salt, rope and fish went out. Sound oak (enough for one keel and some planking) and nine loads of thin grain came in. A few pieces of old silver went out.
- No party went out and no court message was sent. No stranger came by land and no hull not known on this coast came in.
- Carried inside the town only: the god's two words to the Midwife and the Harbormaster, the Harbormaster's pieces to the cairn, the notched stick to the Harbormaster's step. Nothing left the town.
- The second Captain learned in month 2 that the Harbormaster was "told where", and that the keeping exists as the lamp-tenders and the crews tell it; he was not told more. The nine families know the two boats were found under the chain's line and that a lead was put there on a word. They do not know whose word. The town says the keeping, from the crews' talk.
- The town does not know where the god was in the autumn or the spring and sent no one after it.

## Asked of the god

- The Warden-Monarch, month 8 of Year 4, to the water at the tide-door: "I do not know whether I had the right. Did I?" Answered in front of her, at the tide-door in month 1: "You had the right. It was your word and your hand, and you said so aloud, which was honest."
- The Harbormaster, month 2 of Year 4, through the Warden-Monarch: "Where inside the mouth? Say which timbers and where, and I will put a lead there. I will not drag a harbor on a word." Answered through the Warden-Monarch's mouth in month 1 (the words of the message). Not answered in front of him; he has not met it.
- The Eldest Midwife, month 10 of Year 4, at the bedside: where it was in the spring and what it found there; whether it read her list by the same hand as the others. Answered through the Warden-Monarch's mouth in month 1 (no whole answer to either; the widow among the kept; the list read as the only honest count). Not answered in front of her this year.
- The Warden-Monarch, month 1, at the tide-door, in her words: "I will try," to "Do not carry it." No reply in front of her.
- The Harbormaster, month 2, through the Warden-Monarch, in his words: that the lead rang; that he said the lead and not the tide; that he will not lift the three stones and the keeping may say if it wishes them lifted. Not met in front of him.
- The Eldest Midwife, month 1, through the Warden-Monarch, in her words: that she sends no house this year; that she has put the two from the point at the head of the list. No question.
- The tenders' boy, month 1, to the Warden-Monarch, not to the god: "Will the bell be answered?" and "How fast?" She told him it said it would come at once, and that she did not know how fast. She did not carry it to the god and I leave it with you.
- The Master of Timber, month 1, in the sitting, in his words: that if it was far then it could be near, and that he would like to know how it meant to be. Said to the court, not to the god.
- The widow of the point, month 3, to the Master of Timber, not to the god: she would not have a hand put to it that was the lamp's; she did not ask him straight whether the keeping had a hand in it.
- No other mortal put a question to it.

## Names said aloud

- "Low Garrow": said by the Warden-Monarch to the Eldest Midwife in month 1 in the god's words ("I do not say Low Garrow and I do not say not"), and by the Midwife at three bedsides. Already in the record.
- "Skerr": not said aloud in anything I wrote this year. Already in the record.
- "Brennis": not said aloud this year.
- "the listener": the old woman's byname and the town's; "the keeping", the court's. Neither is a name for the people, the town or an office holder.
- No name was said aloud for the people, the town or any office holder. The widow of the point, her sons, the hired hand, the master and crew of the quay's boat, the nine families, the ferryman and the lamp-tenders are unnamed and filed by what they were. All stay unnamed or filed by office.

## What the people tell their god

The Warden-Monarch, for the court, as she would leave it in the jar and say it at the tide-door:

"I have tried not to carry it. I carry it less than I did. I told the court what you said of the lamp and the right, all but the last four words, and I told the senior Captain and my heir and the tenders, and the bell's two strokes. The old woman says it burns as a lamp does and she tends it as one. The boy asked whether the bell would be answered, and how fast, and I did not know.

"The Eldest Midwife heard your word in her kitchen. She says a half answer called a half is the only kind a midwife trusts. She sends no house this year. She put the two from the point at the head of the list, and the list is in the jar. She told the widow's daughters yes, it was in the house.

"The Harbormaster heard your word at the chain's post. He said the lead and not the tide, and let it stand. On the fourth morning of the second month he put the lead along the line where you said and it rang on the three stones, and the grapnel took a strake and a rib beside them. The Master of Timber knew the strake for his own, laid in a boat of the point nineteen years ago. He laid the pieces on the cairn and the nine families let a stone fall for each at the line. He does not know how the boats came to lie there and will not say, and says the book said down. The line and the quay each say the other let the chain up, and neither says it aloud. The key is written by two hands now. He says the lead rang, and that he will not lift the stones, and that you may say if you wish them lifted. He has sounded the near shoal on three mornings with your notched stick and drawn it, part in ink and part in charcoal, and will not send a boat down it until his lead has been down it in a long swell three times.

"Four storms came on the shore between the second month and the sixth, the longest a day and a night, none worse than the town knows, and no boat was lost to a storm. The quay and the post and the new pile held. The Harbormaster says the court's own rule that when a long swell runs with no storm the Captains stand at the mouth with a line and no boat of the point goes out. The point heard itself named. The ferryman said he would keep it when he had buried his own, and he has kept it.

"The Master of Timber went to the point himself. The point took the keel, and the widow set no hand to it and forbade none. You laid your hand on it in the fourth month and he says it is the ninth. The point has a boat again, smaller than the one lost, and the younger son left bread on the step the morning she went out. The widow has not. The Master chose the ferryman's boat for the strake in the tenth month, and you laid your hand on it in the eleventh, and he says it is the tenth.

"The star dimmed three times, on the ninth and twenty-fourth nights of the second month and the thirteenth of the seventh. The old woman at the east lamp says it does not count up, and does not know what it counts. I have a stick of three notches in the jar.

"In the eleventh month, on the nineteenth day, a boat of the quay's own households was caught outside the shoal by a long swell with no storm in it, four aboard. I struck the bell twice an hour before dusk and twice in the first hour of the night. I had your lamp taken to the water's edge and lit, and said aloud that it was a light, to steer by. The Captains' boat stood at the mouth with the long line made fast to the outer pile, and the point stayed in. It was tried.

"Three coasting boats came from the levels with oak and grain, and the shepherds came twice. The ferryman towed in a late boat in fair weather and called it a small thing. The Midwife's list of the year's dead is in the jar, in her name, with the stick of three notches. I ask nothing in words. We want the boats home. That is the town's want as well as yours."
