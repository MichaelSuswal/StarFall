# People: unnamed

The agreed record of god-sea's people, written by the Keeper on creation Night 3a, 2026-11-24, from the god's own description as confirmed in two rounds. Quoted blocks are the god's words. This record holds what the people are, know and believe; what they believe of their past may be wrong.

Unnamed by their god, and their home unnamed (unnamed harbor town [f30]), until a mortal names them in play.

## Who they are
> Mortal folk of the common kind, small and weathered, a people of one harbor town and the shore around it. They are fishers, boatwrights, salt-boilers and ferrymen first, farmers a poor second. They are not a conquering or wandering people. They have held this coast a long time and have lost a good deal of it to the sea. This is a wish: I do not say how long.

## How many
About 2,000, inside and just outside the walls of the unnamed harbor town [f30]. Within the god's size lean. Nothing was argued about size.

## How they live
> In a walled harbor town of grey stone below and old timber above, on a cold broken shore, with small fields and goat and sheep pasture on the headland's lee side. Boats go out in the short fair days and come in before the weather. Autumn is the busy season: the last catch is salted and smoked, the boats are hauled and caulked, and the stores are counted against the winter. Winter is mending, boiling salt, spinning, and waiting. Spring is lean. Summer is earned. They keep a count of storms by the year, not only of days. They keep a lamp at the harbor mouth, lit every dusk through the dark half of the year.

## Their code
> They honor the one who stays: the keeper, the ferryman who goes back out for a late boat, the midwife called at any hour, the lamp-warden who does not leave the lamp. They honor an oath to the harbor. The worst of them would still not leave a boat's crew in the water while a hand could be lifted, and would not put out the harbor lamp while a boat was unaccounted for. These are customs and oaths. They are temper and honor, and they can fail.

## Skills and culture
> Ordinary and free: clinker-built open boats and small decked ones, nets, long lines, weirs, salting, smoking, drying on racks, boiling brine for salt, tarring, rope from hemp and from spun weed-fibre, sail from coarse wool and linen, a stone and timber quay, and a small bronze and iron smithy for nails, hooks and tools (no steel). They read weather by cloud, swell and gull. They keep a tally on notched sticks, and the court's clerks keep a harbor-book of boats out and boats back. They bury the drowned who are found, and set an empty cairn for the ones who are not.

## Edges and their weaknesses
None. The god proposed no edge and none was priced; no weakness is owed. The habits and prides above are tempers, able to fail. The force under Helpers is ordinary by the measure in the ruling ledger (L-0030).

## Their magic
One or two in the town work a small thing: a net-mender who can call a fouled line free by holding it still in her hands until it gives, at the cost of the night's sleep after; and, some say, the old woman at the east lamp, who knows a day ahead when a boat will not come back and is cold to the bone for a week after each knowing. Ordinary in this world; each bears her own cost; no edge.

## What they believe about gods
> On the morning before I come: that the sea is not a god but is old and keeps what it keeps. A few old people say that someone keeps the keeping, a patient thing that walks the bottom and does not answer. Most doubt it. They do not pray to it. Fishers leave a heel of bread or a fish-head on the quay's lowest step and do not say why. The line that holds the throne is called sacred for an old promise, the way the line of lamp-wardens has always been, and the people know the promise but not who made it. This is their belief and a wish about their past; the world decides.

## Rule and worship
> Within my leans. A mortal line holds the throne in my name and is held sacred for it, and a court stands between the throne and the people, and I am reached only through it. The line is the old line of lamp-wardens, the family that has kept the harbor lamp. It is sacred for the lamp and the promise, and a monarch of it keeps the lamp and the harbor-book and says the court's word. No worship. They ask nothing of me in rites, and I ask nothing of them. What they do about me is theirs.

## Hierarchy
- The Warden-Monarch, of the line of lamp-wardens: holds the throne, keeps the harbor lamp and the harbor-book, says the court's word.
- The Harbormaster: the quay, boats and tides; the town's order; the harbor court at low water on the quay steps.
- The Master of Timber: the yard and the boatwrights.
- The Reeve of the Fields: the headland farms and stores.
- The Eldest Midwife: births, sickness and the dead; the peace between households.
- The two Captains: the boats and the muster of about one hundred and fifty, a levy drilled each spring.

## Helpers
Designed, not yet serving, 2026-11-24 (creation Night 3a), by the god's own choice with the cost stated to it. The offices below stand and are filled, each under the people's own custom. None takes the god's word at landing. Each becomes the god's helper when its holder hears the god and takes its word, in play: for an office held for one awaited, when its holder takes the god for that one. Until then no one acts for the god while it is away, its unplayed time runs on its own conduct alone, and the people go their own way under their own court. A bend, not a refusal of a default; not priced; it bought nothing.

The offices are those under Hierarchy. Orders travel from the court's clerks to the Harbormaster, and from him to the boat-captains and the street-elders. A bronze bell below the lowest quay step, and notched sticks left in a sealed jar there, are an old custom of the court for leaving word 'for the keeping'; no one living has had an answer.

## How the god meets them
They are already there, with their own history. They keep an old promise and do not know who made it; they do not know the god is coming, and have no name for it. Designed, not yet serving.

## What they know of the land
Each place with its map id and the grade of their knowledge. Travel times are from the map at the pace table's rates for a fair summer day, by the route and means named.
- The Garrow Shore [r01]: seen, their own coast, by every cove and landing.
- unnamed harbor town [f30]: seen, their home.
- Garrow Skerries [f31]: seen; fished round, and feared in an onshore blow.
- Cliff of Bones [f32]: seen; a place boats do not land.
- Drowned Causeway [f33]: seen at the lowest tides; they say it led to a town the sea took.
- The Hern Sea [r30]: seen inshore; reported farther out by the few who have gone.
- The Bren Hills [r05]: reported, bare hills along the coast to the west where shepherds go in summer.
- The Ansel Plain [r04]: reported, grass country behind the hills, by drovers who bring wool.
- The Iss Levels [r07]: reported, by coasting boats; a walled river town there (unnamed walled town [f35]), 250.8 miles on foot over open ground, 18 days.
- The Kennoch Heights [r02]: rumored, mountains far to the west where a people of the passes take a toll.

## Neighbors and their trouble
Neighbors: shepherds of the Bren Hills [r05], who trade wool and mutton for salt fish; coasting boats from the Iss Levels [r07] in summer. Their trouble: sound timber is short on this coast and the yard is patching old hulls; two boats did not come back last autumn; and the Harbormaster and the line of lamp-wardens are at odds over who answers for the harbor chain.

## Named mortals
None named. Office holders are filed by office until play names them.

## What the old tell
Added on creation Night 3b, 2026-11-24. The people's own telling, which may be wrong; nothing agreed on Night 3a is changed.
- That the promise is older than the walls: whoever keeps the lamp is kept count of. The eldest say it was made to a lamp-warden named Brennis by something that came up the quay steps and went down them again, and that the bell was hung so it could be called. Most say Brennis made it herself, to the town.
- That the Drowned Causeway [f33] led to a town called Low Garrow, which the sea took in one night for its pride, and that this town was founded by those the sea spared. They do not say that most of Low Garrow had already left.
- That when the old were children, walkers came who sang names all night on the quay, asked the name of every cove and sang them back, and wanted nothing. The town calls them the tally-voices.
- That the stones of the causeway were once square.
